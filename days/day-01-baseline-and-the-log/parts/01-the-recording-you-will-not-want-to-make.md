# The recording you will not want to make

`CF-01`, `CF-02` · Day 1

## The one-line answer

You cannot fix a sentence you never heard yourself say, so today you make the recording you have
been avoiding, and you keep it.

## The model

Here is the thing nobody tells you about speaking a second language: **you do not hear yourself
while you are speaking.** Your attention is spent on choosing the next word. The sound coming
out of your mouth is running unsupervised.

So there are two versions of every sentence you produce. There is the sentence you meant — clean,
correct, the one you would have written. And there is the sentence you actually made. Most
learners spend years assuming these are the same thing.

They are not, and the distance between them is exactly what this course closes. But you cannot
close a distance you cannot measure, and the only instrument that measures it is a recording of
your own voice, played back.

Today you make that recording. It will be uncomfortable. Everyone's is. Your voice will sound
wrong to you — that is not your English, that is bone conduction, and it happens to speakers of
every language listening to themselves.

Three phrases, because you will need them from tomorrow onward and there is no reason to wait:

> How do I put this…
> The thing is, I've never really thought about that.
> Let me start again.

That third one is not a failure phrase. It is a professional one. Fluent speakers restart
sentences constantly; they just do it out loud and keep moving instead of stopping.

**Today's phrases** — all three go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| How do I put this… | You need a moment and want it to sound deliberate |
| The thing is, … | You are about to give the real reason |
| I've never really thought about that. | The honest answer, and it buys you time |
| Let me start again. | The sentence went wrong and you are restarting out loud |

## Say it now

Say each line three times. Not in your head — out loud, at normal volume, with a room around you.

1. *How do I put this… the thing is, I've never really thought about that.*
2. *Let me start again.*
3. *The thing is, I don't know yet, but here's what I think.*

The point of a scripted drill is not the meaning. It is that your mouth makes the shape without
consulting you first. Three times is a floor, not a target.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d01-baseline.m4a`.**

The prompt — write it down, you need it again on Day 71:

> **Describe a place you know well, and then explain why someone should or should not go there.**

Rules, and they matter today more than any other day:

- Two minutes, roughly. Do not time it precisely.
- **One take.** No restart, no second attempt, no deleting.
- No notes, no plan, no thirty seconds of preparation.
- When you get stuck, use the phrases above and keep going. Do not stop the recording.

- **Solo form:** speak to the recorder as though to a person who is genuinely curious.
- **With a partner:** have them listen without responding at all. No nodding, no "mhm". Their
  silence is the exercise.

This will be the worst recording in your portfolio. That is its entire job.

## The deliberate failure

Immediately after the baseline, record thirty more seconds on the same topic — but this time,
**speak as fast as you possibly can.** Push past comfortable. Try to break it.

**What breaking sounds like:** the endings go first. Plurals vanish, past-tense endings
disappear, consonant clusters collapse to their first sound. Then the grammar goes — you start a
sentence one way and finish it another.

Listen for **which** thing broke first. That is not random. It is the weakest joint in your
spoken English, and it is the thing you will find yourself repairing around Day 64. Write down
what you heard.

## When it breaks in the wild

Someone asks you a normal question and you hear yourself producing a sentence that is going
wrong — the tense is off, the word was not the one you wanted — and you stop. You go back to the
start. You apologise for your English.

The stopping costs more than the error did. The listener had already understood you and had
moved on; the apology drags their attention back to your English and away from what you were
saying.

**The repair:**

> Let me start again — what I mean is…

Said in a normal voice, at normal speed, with no apology attached. Then continue. This is what
fluent speakers do a dozen times an hour and nobody notices, because they do not stop.
