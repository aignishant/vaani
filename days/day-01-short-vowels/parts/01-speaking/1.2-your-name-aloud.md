## One-line answer

Use a clear word inside a message about yourself, then listen to what you actually recorded.

## The story

Imagine the neighbour asks who you are. You have practised a greeting, so you say it all in
one rush. Your name is hard to catch. You repeat the whole greeting with the same rush.
The neighbour still has to ask. Giving your name room to be heard solves the real problem.

This scene is for practice; it is not a report of your speech.

## The idea in plain language

You are making your first **self-introduction**: a message that tells someone who you are.
The useful result is that a listener can catch your name.

Use the [four vowel sounds](1.1-four-vowel-sounds.md) as a warm-up. Then carry the sound in
bit into something true about yourself. You can follow a small ready-made phrase without
knowing all its grammar yet. Put your own information into it.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your [day 20 spoken introduction](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) grows
from this first message. Practising only a row of sounds would leave you without a message
to give when someone asks who you are.

## The rule

First say bit, bet, bat and but. Compare the middle sounds using
[part 1.1](1.1-four-vowel-sounds.md) if needed. Then put the words aside.

Here is the shape of a greeting. The person in the example is not you:

> Hi, I'm Priya.

Hi greets someone. I'm introduces you when your name follows it. Put your own name there.
Say your name as you normally say it. Do not force one of the four practice sounds into it.

Next, choose something true about how you feel. A bit means “slightly” here.

> I'm a bit tired.

Tired means you need rest. You may use happy if you feel pleased.
Choose one that fits you a little. If none fits, record your greeting and add the word bit
separately; an untrue feeling is not required for a sound exercise.

1. Start your phone's voice recorder.
2. Say your own greeting and your chosen feeling, or the separate word bit.
3. Stop and play it back. Can you hear your whole name? Does bit keep its middle sound?
4. If a word is unclear, practise just that word. Record the whole message again.

Checked 2026-09-18: [Meeting new people](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/meeting-new-people)
for the greeting; [bit](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/bit_1)
for /bɪt/ and the meaning of a bit;
[tired](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/tired) and
[happy](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/happy) for the feeling
words. The feeling choices are prompts, not claims about you.

## When it breaks

Deliberately change bit to bet:

> I'm a bet tired.

This is the exercise's wrong version, not an observed learner error. A listener may guess
what you meant, but bet does not carry the intended meaning. Return to bit by trying the
higher tongue position in [part 1.1](1.1-four-vowel-sounds.md). Keep the rest of your message.

If your name is missing or cut off by the recording, the check is RED even if the four
practice words sounded clear. Record the message again with your whole name audible.

## In real life

Someone meeting you wants your name, not a performance of a practice row. Use the greeting
you prepared and let the other person reply. If they ask again, repeat your name clearly.
You do not need to start every word of the greeting again.

Your own replay is a first check. Because you know your name, you may fill in a missing
sound without noticing. If someone is available and willing, let them hear the greeting
without telling them your name first. If they cannot catch it, try another recording.
Working alone is still fine; do not claim a stranger checked it when only you listened.
