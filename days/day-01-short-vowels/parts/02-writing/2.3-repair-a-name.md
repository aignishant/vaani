## One-line answer

Change one starting capital to its small form on purpose, find it, and restore your name's usual form.

## The story

Imagine checking the label in your notebook before handing it to someone. You read your
name and think, “That is me.” Because the name is familiar, you pass over a changed starting
letter. Reading what you expected is not the same as checking what is on the page. Pointing
at the actual letter makes the difference visible.

This is a practice scene. We have not inspected your handwriting or found this mistake in it.

## The idea in plain language

Use [part 2.1](2.1-capital-and-small-letters.md) if capital and small forms are new to you.
[Part 2.2](2.2-your-name-on-paper.md) shows how to put your own name on a page.

Today's check compares a chosen name form with the form you actually wrote. RED means they
do not match at the starting letter. GREEN means they match after you fix it. The wrong
version stays visible as evidence that your check could find something.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 written page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) needs a read-back
that can catch a visible problem. “I looked at it” is not enough if you cannot point to the
letter you checked.

## The rule

For a name normally beginning with a capital, make one deliberate change. Here is the model
with a capital P changed to a small p:

> priya Rao

Mark it RED. The smallest repair is:

> Priya Rao

Both p and P are real forms of the same letter. In this ordinary name, the position calls
for its usual capital. The rest of the name does not need changing.

| Look at this position | Deliberate change | Restored form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start of the first name | **p**riya Rao | **P**riya Rao |
| Start of the next name | Priya **r**ao | Priya **R**ao |

1. Write your own name correctly as your reference.
2. On the next line, copy it but change one starting capital to its small partner.
3. Cover the correct line. Point to each starting letter in the changed line. Mark the
   altered one RED.
4. Uncover the reference. Restore only the altered letter on a new line. Mark it GREEN
   once the two forms match.
5. Write your own “My name is …” line again with the correct name form.

If your established name has no starting capital, use the model above for the deliberate
case change, then check and write your own name exactly as you use it. Do not invent an
error in your name to make the exercise fit.

Checked 2026-09-18: [Capital letters and apostrophes](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/capital-letters-apostrophes)

for the capital/small pairs. These reference pages do not claim that you made this mistake.

## When it breaks

Suppose you intended to restore Priya Rao but produced:

> PRiya Rao

Now the starting capital is present, but the following r has changed too. Restoring one
letter should not disturb a correct neighbour. Compare every letter with the reference,
then write the name again.

If you marked the deliberately altered version GREEN, your check missed its target. Do
the check again with your finger on the changed starting letter. If you cannot choose its
correct form, return to the alphabet table in part 2.1.

## In real life

Familiar names are easy to skim. On a label or a form, check the written letters instead of
relying on the name in your head. A reader may recognise a name with a missing capital,
but that does not make the form match what you intended to write.

For someone else's name, ask for their spelling if you are unsure. Do not “correct” an
unfamiliar name into a familiar one. Keep the deliberate wrong line in your practice page;
use the corrected line when showing someone your name.

## Check yourself

- **Say out loud:** Which letter made the check turn RED, and what exact change repaired it?
