## One-line answer

Put a long vowel next to its short partner, hear the gap, then use the long one in a sentence about your life.

## The story

Your friend on the phone asks, "Was the bus full?" You want to say, "Yes, it was full." You
also want to say that a man on the bus was a fool. Both words have the same start and the same
end. Only the middle sound is different. One is short. One is long. If you say both the same
way, your friend hears one word twice.

This is a practice scene, not a report of your speech.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **pair** — two things that go together.
- **partner** — the other one in a pair.
- **gap** — the difference you can hear between two sounds.

Each long vowel from [part 1.1](1.1-three-long-sounds.md) has a short partner. Short vowels
were the subject of
[day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.1-four-vowel-sounds.md). Today you
say the two partners next to each other. You listen for the gap. Then you take the long one
into a true sentence.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your [day 20 recording](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is about you: what
you eat, what you need, what you do. Many of those words hold a long vowel. "I need", "I eat",
"I do", "I saw". If they come out short, the stranger hears other words, or no word.

## The rule

Say each pair. Short word first, then long word. Hold the second one.

| Short | Long | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [ship](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/ship) /ʃɪp/ | [sheep](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/sheep) /ʃiːp/ | Hold the "ee". Smile a little more. |
| [full](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/full_1) /fʊl/ | [fool](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/fool_1) /fuːl/ | Hold the "oo". Push the lips forward. |
| [cot](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/cot) /kɒt/ | [caught](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/caught_1) /kɔːt/ | Hold the "aw". Round the lips. |

All six pages were opened on 2026-09-22. Each has a sound button. Press it. Then say the pair.

Say a pair as one row, three times:

> ship — sheep — ship — sheep

Now use the long sound in a true sentence. Here is the shape. The person is not you:

> I need tea in the morning.

> I bought two eggs today.

Need holds /iː/. Two holds /uː/. Bought holds /ɔː/. Say your own sentence. Put your own
true thing in it.

1. Turn on your phone's voice recorder.
2. Say the three pairs.
3. Say one true sentence with need, one with two, one with bought.
4. Play it back. Is the long word longer than its short partner?

## When it breaks

You mean "I need tea." It comes out:

> I nid tea.

The "ee" was cut short. "Nid" is not a word. The listener stops and guesses. The fix is to
hold the middle of need, with a small smile, and say the sentence again.

If your playback has ship and sheep sounding the same, the pair check is RED. Do not move on.
Listen to the two model pages again. Say only that pair until you can hear the gap.

## In real life

In a real call, the listener also has the topic. If you are talking about a farm, "sheep" is
expected. If you are talking about the sea, "ship" is expected. So a small error is often
forgiven. But names, numbers and single words get no help from the topic. Hold the long vowel
when the word stands alone.

A native listener notices a short "ee" in need or eat. They usually still understand. They
may not understand a short "oo" in food or a short "aw" in bought, because the short partners
are other words.
