# Six vowels wearing each other's clothes

`PR-01` · Day 2

## The one-line answer

English keeps six short vowels in a space where most languages keep five or fewer, so two of
yours are almost certainly doing the work of three of theirs.

## The model

Think of your mouth as a room, and vowels as furniture placed in it. Your first language put the
furniture down years ago and screwed it to the floor. English wants six pieces in that room. If
your language uses five, two English pieces are stacked on top of each other — and every word
that depended on telling them apart arrives as a guess.

This is not about accent. Nobody needs to sound like anyone else. It is about a listener who
hears *bad* when you said *bed*, spends half a second reconstructing, and misses your next
sentence while doing it. Six sounds, and the guessing mostly stops.

Here they are, each with the word that owns it:

> **sit** — the tongue high and forward, jaw almost closed
> **set** — jaw drops slightly, tongue still forward
> **sat** — jaw drops properly, mouth wide, this one feels rude
> **cut** — jaw mid, tongue pulled back and relaxed, no lip shape at all
> **cot** — jaw low, back of the tongue down, lips slightly rounded
> **put** — jaw high, lips rounded and pushed forward a little

Say those six down the list. Put a hand flat under your jaw while you do it. **You should feel
the jaw move on almost every step.** If your hand stays still, you are producing one or two
vowels and relabelling them — which is exactly the collision.

The one that surprises everyone is **cut**. It is not a special sound. It is the sound your
mouth makes when it is doing nothing at all: jaw halfway, tongue flat, lips neutral. Learners
usually replace it with something more energetic, because it feels lazy. Lazy is correct.

**Today's phrases** — all of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| It's a bit of a mess. | Describing something disorganised — three short vowels in a row |
| I'm not sure I follow. | You did not understand and want them to say it again |
| That's not quite what I meant. | Correcting a misunderstanding, softly |
| It just depends. | The honest answer to most questions, and it buys you time |
| Give me a second. | You need to think, and you are saying so out loud |

## Say it now

Six pairs. Say each pair **five times, alternating**, hand under your jaw. You are not listening
for beauty. You are checking that your jaw moves between the two words.

1. *sit — set*
2. *set — sat*
3. *sat — cut*
4. *cut — cot*
5. *cot — put*
6. *bad — bed — bud*

Then say these three sentences, slowly, three times each:

7. *It's a bit of a mess, but it just depends.*
8. *That's not quite what I meant — give me a second.*
9. *I'm not sure I follow. Can you run that past me again?*

The sentences matter more than the pairs. A vowel you can produce in isolation and lose inside a
sentence has not been learned yet.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d02-free.m4a`.**

> **Describe the room you are sitting in right now — the furniture, the mess, the light, what is
> broken.** Then say one thing about it you would change.

Rooms are full of short vowels: *bit, bed, black, cup, box, cushion, shelf, mess, desk, dust.*
That is why this prompt and not another.

- **Solo form:** point at things as you name them. The pointing keeps you talking.
- **With a partner:** they sit facing away and draw what you describe. Then compare. What they
  drew wrong is usually a vowel, not a word.

Do not prepare. Do not stop to fix a word — if one comes out wrong, say it again correctly and
keep moving. That is Day 17's skill arriving early, and today is a good place to start.

## The deliberate failure

Record twenty seconds where you say every short vowel **the same way on purpose** — pick one
vowel from your own language and force all six words through it.

*sit, set, sat, cut, cot, put* — all identical. Then the sentence: *It's a bit of a mess.*

**What breaking sounds like:** the sentence stops being six distinct words and becomes a
rhythm with a shape but no content. Notice that it still sounds *confident*. That is the trap —
vowel collapse does not sound like an error from the inside. It sounds fine. It only fails at
the listener.

Now say the same sentence properly. The difference you just heard is the whole of `PR-01`.

## When it breaks in the wild

You say a word, the other person tilts their head slightly and repeats it back to you as a
question — *"...bed?"* — and you say "yes, yes" and move on, faintly embarrassed, without ever
finding out which sound went wrong.

That head-tilt is free information and you are throwing it away.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — b, e, d. The one you sleep in.

Spell it, then give a one-word context. It takes two seconds, it fixes the conversation
immediately, and it tells you which vowel to write into `docs/ERRORS.md` tonight. Fluent
speakers of every language do this. It is not a confession of weakness; it is repair.
