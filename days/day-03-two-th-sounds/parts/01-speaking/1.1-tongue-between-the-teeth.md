## One-line answer

For the "th" in think, the tip of your tongue touches your top teeth and only air comes out, with no voice.

## The story

You are at a shop counter. You ask, "How much is this?" The seller says, "Thirty." You say it
back to check: "Tirty?" He looks at you. "No — thirty." You hear the difference, but you
cannot make it. Your tongue stays behind your teeth, where it goes for "t". His tongue comes
out a little. That small move is the whole difference.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **tongue** — the soft, moving part inside your mouth.
- **tip** — the very front end of the tongue.
- **teeth** — the hard white parts you bite with. One is a tooth.
- **voice** — the buzz in your throat when you say "aaa". Put a hand on your throat to feel it.
- **consonant** — a sound made by stopping or squeezing the air in your mouth.

A **consonant** is a sound where the air is blocked or squeezed. The sounds in
[day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.1-four-vowel-sounds.md) were
**vowel sounds**, made with open air. Today's sound is a consonant. English writes it with
two letters, t and h, but it is one sound. Its **sound symbol** is /θ/. A sound symbol is a
mark that tells you which sound to make.

The rule is about where the tongue goes and whether the voice is on. For /θ/, the tongue tip
touches the bottom edge of the top teeth, or sits just between the teeth. Air squeezes past.
The voice is off.

The word consonant was checked on 2026-09-22 in
[consonant](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/consonant): "a speech
sound made by completely or partly stopping the flow of air through the mouth or nose".

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 speaking check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), you will say

become tink, tank, tree and tirty. A stranger hears different words.

## The rule

Look in the mirror. Open your mouth a little. Push the tip of your tongue out until it just
touches the bottom of your top teeth. Now blow air past it. Do not use your voice. That is /θ/.

Here is where things are:

| Part of the mouth | For /θ/ as in think | For /t/ as in tin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tongue tip | touching the top teeth, a little outside | behind the top teeth, inside |
| air | squeezes past the tongue, keeps going | stops, then pops out |
| voice | off | off |

Say the pairs:

> thin — tin — thin

> three — tree — three

In the mirror, you should see the tongue tip for thin and three. You should not see it for
tin and tree.

## When it breaks

You mean think, but the tongue stays inside:

> tink

The listener hears a word that is not English, or hears "tin" with a k. In a sentence, "I
tink so" is usually understood. Alone, three said as tree is a different word. The fix: look
in the mirror, put the tongue tip out until you can see it, and blow. Say think again.

If you cannot see your tongue in the mirror on any of the four words, the check is RED.

## In real life

Many people speak English all their lives with a /t/ where /θ/ should be. They are understood
most of the time, because the sentence helps. The places it fails are numbers and names: three,
thirteen, thirty, and the word thing. At a counter, "tirty" can be heard as "thirty" or as
"dirty". Give numbers the tongue.

A native listener notices the missing /θ/ at once. They usually say nothing. If they ask you
to repeat, put the tongue out and slow the one word down. You do not need to repeat the
sentence.
