## One-line answer

The "th" in this is made in the same place as the "th" in think, but with the voice switched on.

## The story

Back at the shop counter, you point and say, "Dis one, please." The seller gives you the one
next to it. You point again. "No, dis." He is not sure which. "This one?" he asks, and his
tongue comes out a little on the word. You copy him. "This one." He nods. Same word, same
tongue, and now a buzz in the throat.

This is a practice scene, not a report of your speech.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **buzz** — the small shaking you feel in your throat when the voice is on.
- **switch on** — start something. Here, start the voice.
- **the** — the most common word in English. It starts with today's sound.

In [part 1.1](1.1-tongue-between-the-teeth.md) you made /θ/: tongue tip at the top teeth,
air only. Today's sound has the same tongue and adds the voice. Its **sound symbol** is /ð/.
Put a hand on your throat. Say "aaa". You feel a buzz. That buzz is the voice. Now put the
tongue out as for think and keep the buzz going. That is /ð/.

English spells both sounds the same way: t h. So the spelling cannot tell you which one to
make. You learn it word by word. The good news: /ð/ lives in the small words you say all
day: this, that, the, then, they, there.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every sentence in your [day 20 recording](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases)
will have "the" or "this" or "that" in it. If every one comes out as "de" and "dis", the
stranger hears it fifty times. It is the most repeated sound you will make.

## The rule

1. Hand on your throat.
2. Tongue tip at the top teeth, a little outside, as in part 1.1.
3. Start the buzz and let the air out past the tongue.
4. Move straight into the vowel: this.

Where things are:

| Part of the mouth | For /ð/ as in this | For /d/ as in den |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tongue tip | touching the top teeth, a little outside | behind the top teeth, inside |
| air | squeezes past, keeps going | stops, then pops out |
| voice | on | on |

Open each word. Press the sound button. Say it with the recorder on. Pages opened 2026-09-22;
each has a sound button.

| With /ð/ | Sound |

Now say the two sounds side by side, hand on throat. Same tongue. Only the buzz changes:

> think — this — think — this

Now use it in a sentence about your own things. The shape, with things that are not yours:

> This is my phone.

> This is my bag, and that is my key.

## When it breaks

You mean this, but the tongue stays inside and the air stops:

> dis

The listener hears a "d". In "dis one", they usually still understand. In "they" said as
"day", they hear a different word. The fix is the same as in part 1.1: tongue out until you
can see it in the mirror. Keep the buzz. Do not stop the air.

A second break: you put the tongue out but forget the buzz. Then this sounds like "thiss"
with the think-sound. Hand on throat. Feel for the buzz.

## In real life

Fast speakers make /ð/ very lightly in the small words. The tongue barely touches. But it
still comes forward. What a native listener hears when it does not is "d": "de shop", "dat
one". They understand. They notice. In slow, clear speech, or when you say one word alone,
make it fully.

Under pressure, the small words are the first to slip. That is fine in a conversation. In
your recording, listen for "the". If it is "de" every time, that is the one to work on.
