## One-line answer

Keep the tongue inside on purpose, hear think become tink and this become dis, then bring the tongue out and repair.

## The story

At the counter you say, "Tank you." The seller smiles and says, "Thank you." You hear it.
You say it again with the tongue out: "Thank you." Now it matches. The next day you say
"tank you" again without thinking, and nobody corrects you. The only way to catch it is to
hear it yourself.

This is an imagined scene. Today's wrong version is deliberate. It rests on what many
beginners do, not on a mistake found in your own recording.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **repair** — a small fix that helps someone understand you. You met it on
  [day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.3-change-one-sound.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check failed. GREEN means it passed.

You made /θ/ in [part 1.1](1.1-tongue-between-the-teeth.md) and /ð/ in
[part 1.2](1.2-the-same-place-with-voice.md). Now you make the wrong sounds on purpose: /t/
for /θ/, /d/ for /ð/. You listen. Then you bring the tongue out and fix both.

Many languages have no /θ/ or /ð/ at all. If yours is one of them, your mouth reaches for
the nearest sound it knows, which is usually /t/ or /d/. That is why this check matters.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), "I think" and "this
is" will come many times. If you cannot hear your own "tink" and "dis" now, you will not hear
them on the day.

## The rule

Two rows. In each, the middle word is the deliberate wrong version. The last is the repair.

> think — tink — think

> this — dis — this

1. Mirror in front of you. Record both rows.
2. Play them back without looking. Can you hear the middle word change?
3. Record both rows again with all three words right: tongue out every time.
4. Play all four rows. Two should have a change. Two should not. If you cannot tell them
   apart, the check is RED.
5. Finish with your own "I think …" and "This is my …" sentences from parts 1.1 and 1.2,
   with the tongue out on every th.

The model words were checked on 2026-09-22 in
[think](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/think_1),
[this](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/this_1),
[tin](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/tin_1) and
[then](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/then_1). Each page has a
sound button.

## When it breaks

You meant think — tink — think, but the playback is:

> tink — tink — tink

The tongue never came out. The repair did not happen. Slow down. Look in the mirror. Say only
think, and do not move on until you can see the tongue tip. Then record the row again.

Another break: the tongue comes out, but on this the buzz is missing, so the repair sounds
like "thiss". Hand on throat for the /ð/ row.

## In real life

In a real conversation you will not hear most of your own "de" and "dat". Nobody will stop
you. So the recording is where you catch it, once a day, for a while. After some days you will
start to feel the tongue stay inside as it happens. That feeling is the goal.

A native listener forgives "tink" in a fast sentence. They do not forgive "tree" for three at
a ticket counter, because they hand you the wrong number of tickets. Numbers get the full
tongue, every time.
