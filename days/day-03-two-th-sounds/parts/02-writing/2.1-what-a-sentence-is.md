## One-line answer

A sentence says one complete thing; it starts with a capital letter and ends with a full stop.

## The story

You leave a note on the kitchen table for the person you live with: "gone to the shop back
soon call me if you need milk". They read it and stop. Gone to the shop back? Soon call me?
Where does one thing end? They read it three times and then just call you. A note that needs
a phone call has failed as a note.

This is a practice scene. It is not a report of your page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **sentence** — a group of words that says one complete thing.
- **full stop** — the small dot at the end of a sentence, like this: .
- **complete** — whole, with nothing missing.
- **run on** — when two sentences are written as one, with no stop between them.

A **sentence** says one complete thing. On paper it has two marks that show where it begins
and ends. It begins with a **capital letter**, the big form you met on
[day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.1-capital-and-small-letters.md). It
ends with a **full stop**, a dot. The reader's eye uses the capital to find the start and the
dot to find the end. Without them, the reader has to guess, and the note on the table shows
what guessing costs.

Both were checked on 2026-09-22. The dictionary page for
[sentence](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/sentence_1) says:
"In written English sentences begin with a capital letter and end with a full stop/period
(.), a question mark (?) or an exclamation mark (!)." Today you use only the full stop. The
page for [full stop](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/full-stop_2)
says it is "the mark (.) used at the end of a sentence".

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is ten sentences, and the
gate says it in so many words: "a capital letter and a full stop on every sentence." Every
writing day from here to the end is built from this one shape.

## The rule

Here is one sentence. The person is not you:

> I live in Pune.

Look at its two ends:

| Position | What is there | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| first letter | I — a capital | shows the reader where the sentence starts |
| after the last word | . — a full stop | shows the reader where it ends |

The full stop sits right after the last letter, with no space before it. After the full stop,
leave one space, then start the next sentence with a capital.

Two sentences in a row look like this:

> I live in Pune. I work in a shop.

Now the note from the story, written as sentences:

> I have gone to the shop. I will be back soon. Call me if you need milk.

Three things, three sentences, three capitals, three full stops. Each one can be read alone.

One more rule for the same page: the word I, meaning yourself, is always a capital, wherever it
sits in the sentence. That rule was checked on 2026-09-22 in
[Capital letters and apostrophes](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/capital-letters-apostrophes).

## When it breaks

The note as it was first written:

> gone to the shop back soon call me if you need milk

No capital at the start, so the reader does not know it has started. No full stops, so the
three things run into one. This is called a **run on**: sentences written as one. The fix is
to find each complete thing and give it its own capital and full stop.

A smaller break:

> I live in pune .

The place name has lost its capital, and there is a space before the full stop. Put the P
back. Move the full stop against the last letter.

## In real life

In quick messages to friends, people often drop full stops and capitals. That is a choice
they make for a reader who knows them. On a note, a form, a letter, an email to an office, the
marks come back, because the reader is a stranger and reads fast.

A stranger reading a run-on does not think "this person is a beginner". They think "I cannot
use this", and they stop reading. The capital and the full stop are the cheapest thing you
can do for a reader.
