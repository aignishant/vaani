## One-line answer

Write three true things about your day, each as its own sentence, and check the two ends of each one.

## The story

You leave a new note on the table. This time you think before you write. What are the things I
need to say? Three. You write the first, and stop. You write the second, and stop. You write
the third, and stop. The person you live with reads it once and puts the kettle on. No phone
call.

This is a practice scene, not a report of your page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **true** — really so; not made up.
- **one thing** — a single piece of news or fact. "I am tired" is one thing. "I am tired and
  the shop was shut and I need milk" is three.
- **check** — look again to see if something is right.

In [part 2.1](2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md) you learned the shape: capital, words, full stop.
Now you use it for real. The hard part is not the marks. It is deciding where one thing ends.
Before you write, say the thing out loud. If you take a breath in the middle and could stop
there, it is probably two sentences.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is ten sentences about
you. Three a day, done well, gets you there. And every day after this asks for sentences of
your own; a rule you have only seen is not a rule you have.

## The rule

Here is the shape, with a person who is not you:

> I got up at six. I made tea for my mother. The bus was late.

Notice three things:

| What | Where |
| --- | --- |
| three capitals | I, I, The |
| three full stops | after six, after mother, after late |
| one space | after each full stop, before the next capital |

Now write your own.

1. Say one true thing about today out loud. Keep it short.
2. Write it. Capital first. Full stop last.
3. Do it twice more.
4. Read the three back. Put your finger on each capital and each full stop. Count them. Three
   and three.

If a sentence is long and you cannot find the end, it may be two things. Cut it into two
sentences.

The marks were checked on 2026-09-22 in
[sentence](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/sentence_1) and
[full stop](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/full-stop_2).

## When it breaks

A beginner's version of the model, as it often comes out:

> I got up at six I made tea for my mother the bus was late.

One capital, one full stop, three things. The reader gets to "six I made" and stumbles. The
fix: find the end of each thing, put a full stop there, and start the next with a capital.

Another break:

> i got up at six.

The word I has lost its capital. I is always a capital. Fix that one letter.

## In real life

People who write English every day still get this wrong when they are in a hurry. The
long message with no stops is a message the reader skims. Short sentences, one thing each,
are read.

When you are nervous, on a form or in an email to an office, sentences get longer and the
stops disappear. The fix under pressure is the same as here: say the thing out loud first,
then write only that.
