# Reaching for a whole shelf, not one brick

`FL-02` · Day 4

## The one-line answer

Fluent speakers do not build sentences word by word — they pull down whole prefabricated
phrases, and the reason they sound fast is that most of what they say was never assembled at
speaking time at all.

## The model

Picture a workshop. In the slow version, every sentence is bricks: you pick up *I*, then *have*,
then *been*, then *thinking*, checking each one fits the last. Six decisions, six chances to
stall, and a listener watching you build.

In the fast version, the wall is already made in sections. *I've been meaning to* comes down off
the shelf in one piece. You did not assemble it. You reached for it.

That is the whole difference. Not vocabulary size — a speaker with three hundred ready-made
sections outruns a speaker with three thousand loose bricks, every time, because the sections
were pre-made and the bricks have to be checked.

English is unusually full of these. Listen to anyone talking and you will find that most of it
is not original construction:

> The thing is, …
> I've been meaning to ask you —
> to be fair, …
> It's not that I don't want to, it's just —
> I'll get back to you on that.
> Something along those lines.

None of those were built in the moment. And notice what they do: each one buys the speaker
several words of thinking time while *sounding like content*. That is not a side effect. That is
the point.

The rule for the rest of this course: **when you learn something new, learn it as a section, not
as a brick.** Never *meaning* alone — always *I've been meaning to*. Never *fair* — always *to be
fair*. Your `docs/CHUNKS.md` is a shelf, and from tonight you read it before you add to it.

**Today's phrases** — all of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| The thing is, … | You are about to give the real reason |
| To be fair, … | You are about to concede a point |
| I've been meaning to … | Something you intended to do and did not |
| It's not that I don't … , it's just … | Softening a refusal into an explanation |
| Something along those lines. | You cannot recall it exactly and are approximating |
| I'll get back to you on that. | You do not know and are not going to guess |

## Say it now

Say each line **as one piece**, not word by word. If you hear yourself pausing inside a chunk,
that chunk is not on the shelf yet — say it again until it comes out whole.

1. *The thing is, I've been meaning to ask you about it.*
2. *To be fair, it's not that I don't want to, it's just complicated.*
3. *I'll get back to you on that — something along those lines, anyway.*

Now the same three, at half your comfortable volume but the same speed. Quiet does not mean
slow. Many learners have a fast mouth and a quiet one and have never joined them.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d04-free.m4a`.**

> **Talk about three things you keep intending to do and have not done.** One sentence of what it
> is, one of why it has not happened.

The prompt is chosen so that *I've been meaning to*, *the thing is*, and *it's not that I don't*
all become the natural thing to say. Use them. Reach for the shelf on purpose.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** they say "why not?" after each one, and you answer without pausing. The
  pressure is the exercise.

Do not plan the three things first. Pick them as you speak.

## The deliberate failure

Record thirty seconds on the same topic with **one rule: no chunks at all.** Every phrase must
be built from scratch. Banned entirely: *the thing is*, *to be fair*, *I mean*, *you know*,
*sort of*, and every phrase in your `CHUNKS.md`.

**What breaking sounds like:** the silences relocate. They stop happening between sentences and
start happening in the middle of them — after *I* and before *have*. Mid-sentence silence is the
sound a listener reads as struggling, and between-sentence silence is the sound they read as
thinking. Same amount of silence, opposite impression.

That is what the shelf actually buys you.

## When it breaks in the wild

You have learned a phrase, you know it is right, and in the moment it will not come. So you build
the sentence from bricks instead, it comes out stiff, and afterwards you remember the phrase
perfectly.

The cause is nearly always that you learned it as a translation rather than as a sound. A chunk
recalled through your first language has to be translated at speaking time, which is the exact
delay it was supposed to remove.

**The repair:**

> Hang on — how do I put this… the thing is, …

You have just used two chunks to buy the time to find a third. That is not cheating. That is the
mechanism working.
