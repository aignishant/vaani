## One-line answer

For v, your top teeth rest on your bottom lip and the voice buzzes through the gap.

## The story

You are in a clothes shop. You ask for a vest. The shopkeeper hears "west" and looks towards
the door, as if you asked for a direction. You say it again. Same word comes out. You touch
your chest to show what you mean. He gets it: "Ah, a vest." Your lips were round for the
first sound. His teeth touched his lip. That is the only difference.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **lip** — one of the two soft edges of your mouth. Top lip, bottom lip.
- **rest on** — sit lightly on something, without pressing hard.
- **buzz** — the shaking in your throat when the voice is on.

Both of today's sounds are **consonants**: sounds made by squeezing or stopping the air. That
word was defined on
[day 3](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/01-speaking/1.1-tongue-between-the-teeth.md).
Both use the **voice**, the throat buzz you felt on day 3. The difference between them is
only what the lips and teeth do.

For /v/, the sound in very and vest, the top teeth touch the bottom lip. The air squeezes
between them. The voice is on. The **sound symbol** is /v/, the same as the letter.

Many languages have one sound that sits between English v and w. If yours does, you will
make both English sounds the same way, and a listener will hear one of them wrong half the
time. Today's mirror check is for that.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 speaking check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) you will say
"very", "I live", "I have", "seven", "eleven". If every v is a w, the stranger hears "wery",
"I liw", "sewen". They will follow, but with effort, and the effort is what the gate measures.

## The rule

Look in the mirror. Bite your bottom lip very gently with your top teeth. Now hum. Let the air
out through the teeth and lip. That is /v/. You should see your top teeth on your lip.

| Part of the mouth | For /v/ as in very |
| --- | --- |
| top teeth | resting on the bottom lip |
| lips | not round; the bottom lip is under the teeth |
| air | squeezes between teeth and lip |
| voice | on — feel the buzz |

Open each word. Press the sound button. Say it with the recorder on. Play both. Pages opened
2026-09-22; each carries a sound button.

| Word | Sound | Meaning here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [very](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/very_1) | /ˈveri/ | a lot; more than usual |
| [vest](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/vest_1) | /vest/ | a piece of clothing worn under a shirt |
| [vine](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/vine) | /vaɪn/ | the climbing plant that grapes grow on |

Say them in a row, looking for your teeth in the mirror each time:

> very — vest — vine

Now hold the sound alone: vvvvv. Feel it on your lip.

To watch a mouth make it, open the free series
[The Sounds of English](https://www.bbc.co.uk/learningenglish/english/features/pronunciation)
and look under "Voiced Consonants". The page was opened on 2026-09-22. Each video names its
sound on screen; find the one for /v/.

## When it breaks

You mean vest, but the lips go round and the teeth stay off the lip:

> west

That is a different English word. The listener hears a direction. The fix: bite the bottom
lip gently, and start the word from there. Say vest again.

If, in the mirror, you cannot see your top teeth on your lip for any of the three words, the
check is RED.

## In real life

Many people speak good English with a v that is halfway to w. They are understood from the
sentence. The places it fails are single words and names: vest, van, vote, and any name with
a v in it. If your own name has a v, this sound is yours to own.

A native listener notices "wery" at once. They understand it. They will not correct you. In a
recording made for a stranger, a clear v is a small thing that makes you sound sure.
