## One-line answer

For w, your lips go round and push forward, your teeth stay away from your lip, and the voice is on.

## The story

Still in the clothes shop. You want to say, "Wait, I want the white one." It comes out, "Vait,
I vant the vite one." The shopkeeper understands, mostly. But "vite" is not a word, and he
has to work for it. Your teeth touched your lip on every w. For w, they should not touch at
all. Round lips, like you are about to say "oo".

This is a practice scene, not a report of your speech.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **round** — make your lips into a small circle, like for a kiss.
- **push forward** — move your lips out, away from your teeth.
- **pair** — two things that go together. Here, two words that differ by one sound.

In [part 1.1](1.1-v-teeth-on-the-lip.md) you made /v/ with the teeth on the lip. Today's
sound, /w/, is the opposite in one way: the teeth do not touch anything. The lips do the work.
They go round and push forward, and then open into the vowel. The voice is on for both.

The quick test: say "oo" as in boot, from
[day 2](../../../day-02-long-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-long-sounds.md), and then
open into "et". Oo-et. Said fast, that is wet.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your [day 20 recording](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) will have "we",
"work", "want", "where", "with". If every one starts with teeth on the lip, the stranger hears
"ve", "vork", "vant", "vere", "vith". They understand, and they tire.

## The rule

Look in the mirror. Make your lips a small round circle. Push them forward. Check: no teeth
showing, no teeth on the lip. Now say "oo" and open into the vowel. That is /w/.

| Part of the mouth | For /w/ as in wet | For /v/ as in vest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| lips | round, pushed forward | not round |
| top teeth | away from the lip | on the bottom lip |
| voice | on | on |

Open each word. Press the sound button. Say it with the recorder on. Pages opened 2026-09-22;
each carries a sound button.

| With /w/ | Sound | Partner with /v/ | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [wet](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/wet_1) | /wet/ | — | — |
| [west](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/west_1) | /west/ | [vest](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/vest_1) | /vest/ |
| [wine](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/wine_1) | /waɪn/ | [vine](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/vine) | /vaɪn/ |

Say the pairs. Watch the mirror. Round lips for the first, teeth on lip for the second:

> west — vest — west

> wine — vine — wine

Now use both sounds in true sentences. The shape, with a person who is not you:

> We live in a very small flat.

We has /w/. Live and very have /v/. Say your own sentence with we and very in it.

The free video series
[The Sounds of English](https://www.bbc.co.uk/learningenglish/english/features/pronunciation)
has a video for /w/ under "Other Consonants" or "Voiced Consonants". The page was opened on
2026-09-22; the sound is named in the video, so look for /w/ on screen.

## When it breaks

You mean wet, but the teeth touch the lip:

> vet

That is a different English word: an animal doctor. The fix: lips round, teeth away, start
from "oo". Say wet again.

If in the mirror your teeth touch your lip on wet, west or wine, the check is RED. Push the
lips forward more. The teeth cannot reach a lip that is pushed out.

## In real life

In fast speech the lips do not round very much for w, but the teeth still never touch the
lip. That is the part that stays. If you keep the teeth off, a light w is fine.

The pairs that cost you in real life are few: wine and vine, west and vest, wet and vet. What
costs more is the tiredness of a listener who hears "ve", "vant", "vork" all the way through.
They follow you, but they have to lean in. In your recording, listen for "we" and "with". If
they sound like "ve" and "vith", this is the day to come back to.
