## One-line answer

When there is more than one of a thing, its name usually gets an -s on the end; after some endings it gets -es or -ies.

## The story

You are packing for a train journey with your family. You write a list for your brother, who
is packing the other bag: "2 bag, 3 shirt, 1 box, 2 torch". He packs one bag, one shirt, one
box, one torch. The numbers were there. But the words all said "one". He read the words.

This is a practice scene. It is not a report of your page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **noun** — a word that names a thing, a person or a place. Bag, brother, station.
- **singular** — the form of a noun for one thing. Bag.
- **plural** — the form of a noun for more than one thing. Bags.
- **ending** — the last letter or letters of a word.

A **noun** is a naming word. When you mean one of the thing, you use its **singular** form.
When you mean more than one, you use its **plural** form. In English the plural is almost
always made by adding -s to the end. The number in front, or the word "some", is not enough on
its own; the noun itself changes. A reader looks at the end of the noun to know how many.

The words were checked on 2026-09-22 in
[noun](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/noun),
[singular](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/singular_1) and
[plural](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/plural_1).

## The rule

The main rule and its three small changes. All four were checked on 2026-09-22 in
[Count nouns](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/count-nouns),
and the examples below are that page's own.

| The noun ends in | You add | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| most letters | -s | book → books, school → schools, friend → friends |
| -s, -ch, -sh, -ss, -x, -o | -es | class → classes, watch → watches, wish → wishes, box → boxes, potato → potatoes |
| a consonant letter, then -y | change -y to -ies | lady → ladies, country → countries, party → parties |
| a vowel letter, then -y | just -s | boy → boys, day → days, play → plays |

A consonant letter is any letter that is not a, e, i, o or u.

Here is the shape in a sentence. The things are not yours:

> I have two bags and three shirts.

> There are four boxes in the room.

Bag took -s. Shirt took -s. Box ends in -x, so it took -es.

Look at the end of the noun. Ask: what is the last letter? Then choose the row.

## When it breaks

The list from the story:

> 2 bag, 3 shirt, 1 box, 2 torch

Every noun after a number bigger than one is still singular. The reader gets one of each.
The fix: bags, shirts, torches. Box stays box, because there is one. Torch ends in -ch, so it
takes -es.

A second break:

> two boxs

The -s is there, but box ends in -x, so it needs -es. Read the last letter before you add.

## In real life

In speech, the plural -s is small and easy to miss. On paper it is not: the reader sees the
end of every noun. Lists, forms and messages about numbers of things are where it matters
most. "Send 3 copy" and "send 3 copies" are read by the same person in different moods.

A friend reading a message forgives a missing -s. An office does not read it as friendly; it
reads it as careless, and the form comes back. When you are not sure of the ending, look at
the last letter and use the table.
