## One-line answer

For l, the tip of your tongue presses on the ridge just behind your top teeth and stays there while the voice goes past its sides.

## The story

Still asking the way. The man says, "Go left at the light." You say it back: "Left at the
light." Then you say your own street: "I live on the road past the school." Three l sounds
and three r sounds in one breath. For every l, your tongue tip lands behind your teeth and
stays. For every r, it curls up and lands nowhere. You feel it switching.

This is a practice scene, not a report of your speech.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **ridge** — the small bump on the roof of your mouth just behind your top teeth. Feel it
  with your tongue.
- **press** — push and hold, not tap.
- **sides** — the left and right edges of your tongue.

In [part 1.1](1.1-r-the-tongue-does-not-touch.md) the tongue did not touch. Today's sound,
/l/, is the opposite: the tongue tip touches the ridge behind the top teeth and stays
touching. The voice comes out around the sides of the tongue. The **sound symbol** is /l/.

So r and l are a pair of opposites in one place: same part of the tongue, same part of the
mouth, touch or no touch. That is why they are taught together, and why the check in
part 1.3 swaps them.

## The rule

Look in the mirror. Put the tip of your tongue on the ridge behind your top teeth. Press
lightly. Now hum: llllll. The sound comes out around the sides. Keep the tip pressed the whole
time.

| Part of the mouth | For /l/ as in light | For /r/ as in right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tongue tip | pressed on the ridge behind the top teeth | curled up, touching nothing |
| air and voice | pass around the sides of the tongue | pass over the top of the tongue |
| lips | relaxed | a little round |

Open each word. Press the sound button. Say it with the recorder on. Pages opened 2026-09-22;
each carries a sound button.

| [light](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/light_1) | /laɪt/ | [right](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/right_1) | /raɪt/ |
| [load](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/load_1) | /ləʊd/ | [road](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/road) | /rəʊd/ |

Say the pairs. Feel the tongue land for the first word and float for the second:

> light — right — light

> load — road — load

Now put both in true sentences. The shape, with a person who is not you:

> I live on the left side of the road.

Live, left and side have /l/. Road has /r/. Say your own sentence about where you live, with
at least one l and one r in it.

has a video for /l/. The page was opened on 2026-09-22; the sound is named in the video, so
look for /l/ on screen.

## When it breaks

You mean light, but the tongue does not land:

> right

That is a different English word. The listener hears "go right at the right" and turns the
wrong way. The fix: put the tip on the ridge and hold it before you start the word.

The other way round: you mean rice, and the tongue lands on the ridge:

> lice

Also a different word, and not one you want at a table. If the pairs in the table sound the
same on playback, the check is RED.

## In real life

If your first language separates r and l clearly, this pair costs you nothing, and today is
about the English shape of each rather than the difference between them. If your first
language does not, this pair is one of the few that changes the meaning of a word outright:
right and light, road and load. Directions are where it bites: left, right, light, road, all
in one sentence.

A native listener hears an l where an r should be and stops for a moment. They usually work
it out from the sentence. On a single word, they cannot.
