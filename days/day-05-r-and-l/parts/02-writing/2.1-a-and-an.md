## One-line answer

Before a single thing you mention for the first time, write a; if the next word starts with a vowel sound, write an instead.

## The story

You are writing a short message to a new landlord to tell him who you are. "I am teacher. I
have dog. I need room with window." He reads it and understands. He also reads it slowly,
because every noun stands alone, like a list of words, not sentences. English puts a small
word in front of a single thing, and readers expect it the way they expect a full stop.

This is a practice scene. It is not a report of your page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **article** — one of the small words a, an and the, written before a noun.
- **vowel sound** — a sound made with open air: the sounds at the start of egg, apple, old.
  You met vowel sounds on day 1; today only the first sound of the word matters.
- **consonant sound** — any other sound at the start of a word: the sounds at the start of
  dog, room, teacher.
- **mention** — say or write something for the first time.

An **article** is a small word that goes before a **noun**, the naming word from
[day 4](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md). The article a
means "one, and you do not know which one yet". You use it when you **mention** a single thing
for the first time. If the next word starts with a vowel sound, a becomes an, so the two
words do not run into each other.

The rule is about the sound, not the letter. That is the trap. Most words starting with a,
e, i, o or u start with a vowel sound, but not all.

The rule was checked on 2026-09-22 in
[Articles: 'a', 'an', 'the'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/articles-a-an-the):
"We use a when the word that follows it begins with a consonant sound. We use an when it's
followed by a vowel sound."

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) says what you are and
what you have. "I am a student." "I have a brother." Every one of those needs a or an. A
page with none reads like a list. Many languages have no article at all; if yours is one,
this small word is the one you will forget most, and the one a reader notices most.

## The rule

| The next word starts with | Write | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a consonant sound | a | a dog, a room, a teacher, a window |
| a vowel sound | an | an egg, an apple, an old bag |

The shape, in a message from a person who is not you:

> I am a teacher. I have a dog. I need a room with a window.

Four single things, each mentioned for the first time, each with a in front.

Now the sound trap. The page checked above gives this example: "She has a university
degree." University starts with the letter u, but the sound is "yoo", which is a consonant
sound. So it takes a, not an. Say the word out loud. Listen to the first sound. Then choose.

Write about yourself:

1. What are you? "I am a …" or "I am an …".
2. What do you have? "I have a …" or "I have an …".
3. Say each noun out loud before choosing a or an.

## When it breaks

The message from the story:

> I am teacher. I have dog. I need room with window.

Every article is missing. The reader understands and slows down. The fix: put a before each
single thing mentioned for the first time.

A second break, very common:

> I have a umbrella.

Umbrella starts with a vowel sound, so it needs an. Say "a umbrella" out loud; the two vowel
sounds bump. "An umbrella" flows.

A third: writing "an" because the letter is a vowel, without listening:

> an university

The sound is "yoo", a consonant sound. It takes a. The sound decides, not the letter.

## In real life

In quick speech the article is tiny, and a listener fills it in. On paper it is either there
or not, and a reader sees the gap every time. A missing a is the most common thing a teacher
or an office reader marks on a beginner's page, and it is also the easiest to fix, because
the rule is short.

A native reader forgives "a umbrella" with a small wince. They forgive a missing article in
a friend's message. On a form, a letter or a job application, the missing articles are what
make the page read as "not yet fluent", before anything else is judged.
