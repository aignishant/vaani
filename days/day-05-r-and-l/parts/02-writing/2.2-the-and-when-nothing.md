## One-line answer

Write the before a thing the reader already knows which one you mean, and nothing before plural or uncountable things spoken of in general: a the first time, the the second time, nothing for all of them.

## The story

Your message to the landlord goes on. "I have a dog. The dog is small and quiet." The first
time, a dog: he does not know it yet. The second time, the dog: now he does. Then you write,
"I like dogs." Not a dog, not the dog: dogs in general, all of them, so no small word at all.
Three sentences, three choices, and each one tells him something.

This is a practice scene, not a report of your page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **already knows** — has heard about it before, or there is only one of it.
- **in general** — all of them, not one particular one.
- **uncountable** — a thing you cannot count one by one, like water, rice or milk.
- **particular** — one certain one, not any one.

In [part 2.1](2.1-a-and-an.md) you used a and an for one thing mentioned for the first time.
The article the is for a thing the reader already knows which one you mean: because you
mentioned it before, or because there is only one, like the sun or the station in your town.
And sometimes there is no article at all: when you talk about **plural** things, from
[day 4](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md), or uncountable
things, **in general**.

Both rules were checked on 2026-09-22 in
[Articles: 'a', 'an', 'the'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/articles-a-an-the):
"We use the … when the listener already knows which thing we are talking about because it was
mentioned before or because there's only one of them," and "When we talk about things in
general, we normally use a plural or uncountable noun with no article."

## Why Vaani needs it

On the [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) you will mention your
home, then say more about it. "I live in a flat. The flat has two rooms." And you will say
what you like: "I like tea." The three choices are the difference between a page that reads
as sentences and a page that reads as a list.

## The rule

| The reader | You write | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| does not yet know which one | a or an | I have a dog. |
| already knows which one | the | The dog is small. |
| is being told about all of them, in general | nothing | I like dogs. |

Two of the page's own examples, checked above: "I'm going to take the dog for a walk" (the
listener knows which dog) and "Birds eat worms" (all birds, all worms, no article).

The shape, from a person who is not you:

> I live in a flat. The flat has two rooms. I like small rooms.

A flat: first mention. The flat: now known. Small rooms: rooms in general, plural, nothing.

Now yours. Write a first sentence with a or an. Write a second sentence that says more about
the same thing, with the. Write a third about something you like in general, with no article.

## When it breaks

A beginner's version, as it often comes out:

> I live in flat. Flat has two rooms. I like the small rooms.

Two articles missing, and the on a general statement. "The small rooms" makes the reader
ask: which small rooms? The fix: a flat, the flat, small rooms.

A second break:

> I like the tea.

If you mean tea in general, no article. "The tea" means one particular tea: the one on the
table, the one you just mentioned. Ask: does the reader know which one? If not, and it is
general, write nothing.

## In real life

Native speakers make this choice hundreds of times a day without thinking, and readers feel
it when it is off. "I went to shop" and "I went to the shop" are both understood; the second
sounds like English. On a page for a stranger, the pattern a-then-the is what makes a story
follow: a thing appears, and then we talk about it.

Names of places are where even good writers stumble. Cities and most countries take nothing:
Pune, India. A few take the. When you are not sure, copy how the name is written on a sign or
a ticket.
