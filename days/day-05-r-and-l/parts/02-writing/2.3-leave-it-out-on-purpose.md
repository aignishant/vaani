## One-line answer

Write your message with every article missing or wrong on purpose, read it as the landlord, mark each gap, then put the right small word in each.

## The story

You read your message to the landlord back before sending it. It looks fine. It is your
message; you know what it means. Then you read it in his voice, a stranger who has never met
you or your dog. "I am teacher. I have dog. Dog is quiet." In his voice, every noun stands
alone, and you hear the gaps you could not see.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong version is deliberate. It rests on what beginners
whose first language has no articles often write, not on a mistake found in your own page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **gap** — a place where a word should be and is not.
- **read back** — read your own page again, slowly, as if it were someone else's.
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You used a and an in [part 2.1](2.1-a-and-an.md), and the and nothing in
[part 2.2](2.2-the-and-when-nothing.md). Now you write your sentences again with three
deliberate breaks: one article missing, one a that should be an, one the on a general
statement. You read back as a stranger. Each break is a RED mark. Then you fix only the marks.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) will have ten or more
places where an article belongs. A read-back that cannot find three planted gaps will not
find the real ones.

## The rule

Under your correct sentences from parts 2.1 and 2.2:

1. Copy them with three deliberate breaks: leave one a or an out; change one an to a; put
   the before a plural or uncountable thing spoken of in general.
2. Cover the correct version.
3. Read the broken version out loud, in the voice of someone who has never met you. Put your
   finger before each noun. Ask: is there a small word here? Should there be? Is it the right
   one? Does the reader know which one?
4. Mark each break RED. There should be three.
5. Uncover. Fix only the three, on a new line, and mark it GREEN.

The model, broken on purpose:

> I am teacher. I have a old dog. I like the dogs.

Three RED marks: a is missing before teacher; a old should be an old; the dogs should be
dogs, because it means all dogs.

The rules were checked on 2026-09-22 in
[Articles: 'a', 'an', 'the'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/articles-a-an-the).

## When it breaks

You read the broken version back and mark it GREEN:

> I am teacher. I have a old dog. I like the dogs. — GREEN

If your first language has no articles, the gaps are easy to walk past, because the sentence
still makes sense to you. The check itself failed. Do it again with your finger before each
noun, saying "small word?" out loud each time.

Another break: you over-fix and put an article on everything:

> I am a teacher. I have an old dog. I like the dogs. I like a tea.

"A tea" is wrong for tea in general. Fix only the marks, and check each fix against the
table in part 2.2.

## In real life

Nobody plants a missing article on a real page. But everyone whose first language has no
articles leaves some out by accident, for years, and readers notice every one. The finger
before each noun is the habit that catches them. It is slow the first week and automatic
after that.

A friend reads past the gaps. A landlord, an office, a job application does not read past
them; the page is judged as "not fluent" before its content is read. Keep the broken message
on your practice page as proof that your read-back can go RED.

## Check yourself

  deliberate when you add it to the error ledger.
- **Say out loud:** Which of the three breaks was hardest to see, and what question did you
  ask yourself to find it?
