## One-line answer

Two endings can be made in the same place and differ only by the voice; keep the buzz going to the end for d and g, and cut it for t and k.

## The story

The rickshaw stops. You tell the driver, "The road is bad." He hears "bat" and looks for a
cricket bat. You say, "Bad. The road is bad," and this time you keep the buzz in your throat
right through to the end of the word. He nods at the potholes. Same tongue, same place; only
the buzz was missing.

This is a practice scene, not a report of your speech.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **buzz** — the shaking in your throat when the voice is on. You felt it on day 3.
- **cut** — stop suddenly.
- **partner** — the other one in a pair.

In [part 1.1](1.1-finish-the-word.md) you finished the word. Now you choose which ending. The
**voice**, defined on
[day 3](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/01-speaking/1.2-the-same-place-with-voice.md),
is what separates d from t and g from k. Each pair is made in one place. For d and g the
buzz keeps going into the ending. For t and k the buzz stops before it.

Many speakers cut the voice early on every ending, so bad becomes bat and bag becomes back.
Hand on throat is the test.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your [day 20 recording](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) will have "bad",
"good", "big", "had", "did". If the buzz stops early, they come out as bat, goot, bik, hat,
dit. The stranger hears real words, and not the ones you meant.

## The rule

Hand on your throat. Say bad. Feel the buzz go all the way to the end. Now say bat. The buzz
stops just before the t. Same tongue, same place; only the throat changes.

Bad and bag keep the buzz to the end. Say your own sentence about your street or your things,
with at least one d or g ending and one t or k ending.

## When it breaks

You mean bad, but the buzz stops early:

> bat

A different English word. The listener looks for a bat. The fix: hand on throat, and keep the
buzz going until the tongue lands for the d. Then release.

You mean bag, and the buzz stops:

> back

Also a different word, and one you say often. If bag and back sound the same on playback, the
check is RED. Say only bag, holding the buzz, until the g is there.

## In real life

Native speakers do not make the final d and g loudly. What they do is keep the vowel a little
longer before a voiced ending, and cut it short before a voiceless one. So the listener hears
"bad" partly from the length of the a. You do not need to learn that now. Keeping the buzz to
the end of the word does it for you.

A native listener hears "bat" for "bad" in a sentence and usually works it out. Alone, or in
a name, they cannot. Under pressure, in fast speech, endings are the first thing to go. The
recording is where you find out which of yours go first.
