## One-line answer

To say what someone is, English puts am, is or are between the person and the rest: I am, you are, he is, she is, it is, we are, they are.

## The story

You join a group chat for new people at work. You write your first message: "Hello. I
Ravi. I from Pune. I new here. My brother also here." Everyone understands you. But every
sentence feels like it is missing a beat, like a step with no stair under it. The word that
is missing is the same one every time.

This is a practice scene. It is not a report of your page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **verb** — a word that says what someone does or is. Eat, go, and today, be.
- **be** — the name of the verb that has the forms am, is and are.
- **form** — the shape a word takes with a certain person: am with I, is with he.
- **join** — connect two parts.

A **verb** is a word that says what someone does or what they are. Most verbs are actions.
The verb **be** is different: it does not say what you do, it joins you to what you are. "I
am a teacher." "She is tired." "They are here." Without it, the two parts sit side by side
with nothing between them, like the messages in the story.

Be has three forms in the present. Which one you use depends on who the sentence is about.

The word verb was checked on 2026-09-22 in
[verb](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/verb_1): "a word or
group of words that expresses an action (such as eat), an event (such as happen) or a state
(such as exist)".

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is ten sentences about
you, and most of them will start "I am" or "My family is" or "We are". A page with the verb
missing reads as a list of notes. This one word turns the notes into sentences.

## The rule

The forms, checked on 2026-09-22 in
[Present simple: 'to be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-be).

| Who | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | am | I am a student. |
| you | are | You are late. |
| he, she, it | is | She is a doctor. |
| we | are | We are from Pune. |
| they | are | They are at home. |

The page's own example is "I'm a student." Here it is in its full form: I am a student.

The shape is always: the person, then the form of be, then the rest.

> I am new here.

> My brother is a driver.

For "my brother", "the road", "my city", use is, because each is one person or thing, like
he, she or it. For "my parents", "the roads", use are, because each is more than one, like
they.

Now write about yourself. Start with I am. Then write about one other person with is. Then
about two or more people with are.

## When it breaks

The messages from the story:

> I Ravi. I from Pune. I new here.

Every sentence has lost its verb. The reader fills it in, and feels the gap each time. The
fix: put am after I in every one. I am Ravi. I am from Pune. I am new here.

A second break, common when the form is remembered but the person is not:

> My brother are a driver.

One brother is one person, so is. Ask: one, or more than one? One takes is; more takes are.

A third:

> I is happy.

I takes am, always, and only I takes am. Nothing else does.

## In real life

Native speakers almost never leave be out on paper, so a page without it stands out at once.
In speech it is short and often joined to the word before it; you will meet that in the next
part. On paper, in a message to people who do not know you, the full form is safe and clear.

If your first language has a word like be that can be left out, or one that does not change
with the person, this is the verb to watch. A reader forgives "I is". A form or an office does
not; the sentence is read as unfinished.
