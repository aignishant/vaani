## One-line answer

In everyday writing, am, is and are join to the word before them with an apostrophe, and not joins after them: I'm, she's, they're, isn't, aren't, I'm not.

## The story

The group chat goes on. Someone writes, "I'm at the office. The lift isn't working. We're on
the stairs." You read it and copy the shape for your own message: "I'm not at the office
today. My brother's at home. He isn't well." Short, quick, and every sentence still has its
verb; it has just moved closer to the word before it.

This is a practice scene, not a report of your page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **short form** — two words written as one, with some letters left out.
- **apostrophe** — the small mark that shows where letters are missing, like this: '.
- **not** — the word that turns a sentence into its opposite. I am here; I am not here.
- **everyday** — normal, informal; a message to a friend, not a form.

In [part 2.1](2.1-am-is-are.md) you wrote the full forms: I am, she is, they are. In
everyday writing, people push the form of be onto the word before it and drop a letter. The
**apostrophe** marks the missing letter. I am becomes I'm. She is becomes she's. They are
becomes they're. To say the opposite, not comes after the verb, and it too can join: is not
becomes isn't, are not becomes aren't. Am not does not join in the same way; it stays as I'm
not.

## The rule

The forms, checked on 2026-09-22 in
[Present simple: 'to be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-be),
which lists the contractions "I'm / You're / He's / We're / They're" and "I'm not / isn't /
aren't".

| Full | Short | Full, with not | Short, with not |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I am | I'm | I am not | I'm not |
| you are | you're | you are not | you aren't |
| he is, she is, it is | he's, she's, it's | he is not | he isn't |
| we are | we're | we are not | we aren't |
| they are | they're | they are not | they aren't |

Look at where the apostrophe sits: exactly where the missing letter was. I am → I'm: the a is
gone, and the apostrophe stands in its place. Is not → isn't: the o is gone.

The shape, from a person who is not you:

> I'm from Pune. My sister's a nurse. We aren't at home today.

The page's own example is "My mum's a doctor": a name or a noun can take the short form too.

Now write yours. Three true sentences, each with a short form. Make at least one of them a
sentence with not.

## When it breaks

A beginner's version, as it often comes out:

> Im from Pune. My sisters a nurse. We are'nt at home.

Three breaks. Im has no apostrophe, so it is not a word. Sisters without the apostrophe
reads as more than one sister. Are'nt has the apostrophe in the wrong place: the missing
letter is the o of not, so the mark goes between n and t. Fix: I'm, sister's, aren't.

A second break:

> I amn't here.

Am not does not shorten that way. Write I'm not.

## In real life

Short forms are what native speakers write to each other all day, and what they say almost
always. On a message to a friend, the full forms sound stiff. On a formal letter, a job
application or a form, the short forms are wrong; you will meet that rule in phase 5. For
now: everyday message, short forms; official page, full forms.

A reader forgives a missing apostrophe in a quick chat. In anything else, "im" and "dont"
are the first things that mark a page as careless. The apostrophe is small and it is looked
for.
