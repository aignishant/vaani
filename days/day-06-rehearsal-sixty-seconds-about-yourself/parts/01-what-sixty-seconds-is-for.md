# What sixty seconds is actually for

`CF-03` · Day 6

## The one-line answer

Sixty seconds about yourself is not a summary of your life — it is a door you hold open, and its
only job is to give the other person somewhere obvious to walk in.

## The model

Almost everyone gets this wrong in the same direction. Asked to talk about themselves, they
produce a compressed biography: born here, studied that, then this job, then that job. Accurate,
complete, and impossible to reply to. The listener nods and says "oh, nice", and the conversation
has to be started again from scratch.

A good sixty seconds is built the other way round. It leaves **hooks** — small specific things a
listener can grab. One unexpected detail is worth four accurate ones, because the unexpected one
generates the next question and the accurate ones do not.

Three moves, in order:

**Where you are now**, in a sentence a stranger could repeat. Not your job title — what you
actually do with your days.

**How you got here**, in the shape you drilled yesterday. This is the third pass of the 4/3/2,
not the first.

**One hook**, deliberately left open. Something slightly odd, unfinished, or currently going
wrong. This is the part people cut because it feels unpolished, and it is the part that makes the
conversation continue.

> So at the moment I'm mostly doing …
> I sort of ended up in it by accident, actually.
> The bit I'm still figuring out is …

That last one is the hook. Notice it admits something incomplete. A finished-sounding
introduction closes the door it was supposed to hold open.

And do not open with an apology about your English. It costs you the first sentence, it tells the
listener to start evaluating your English rather than listening to you, and it is the single
easiest thing to delete from your speaking forever.

**Today's phrases** — all of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| At the moment I'm mostly … | Opening with the present rather than the history |
| I sort of ended up in it by accident. | The honest origin story, and a good hook |
| The bit I'm still figuring out is … | Leaving the door open on purpose |
| It's hard to explain, but basically … | Introducing something your listener will not know |
| What about you? | Handing the turn back — never skip this |

## Say it now

The opener alone, five times, out loud. Just the first sentence.

1. *At the moment I'm mostly {{what you actually do with your days}}.*

Say it five times until it arrives without a run-up. The first sentence is the only one that has
to be automatic; if it lands, the rest follows, and if it stalls, the whole minute is spent
recovering.

Then the hook, three times:

2. *The bit I'm still figuring out is {{something genuinely unfinished}}.*

Then the handback, three times, because everyone forgets it:

3. *…anyway. What about you?*

## Say it yours

**This is the take. Recorder on. File: `audio/d06-sixty.m4a`.**

> **Sixty seconds about yourself.** Where you are now, how you got here, one open hook, then hand
> the turn back.

Rules:

- No notes. No plan beyond the three moves.
- **Keep the first take.** You may record a second afterwards, but the first one is not deleted
  and is the one that goes in the portfolio row.
- If you stall, use a chunk and keep going. Stalling is not a reason to stop the recorder.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** they must ask you a follow-up question at the end, without prompting. If
  they cannot think of one, your hook was not open enough — and that is useful information, not
  a failure.

## The deliberate failure

Record thirty more seconds, same topic, but **make it a résumé**: dates, titles, institutions,
in order, no hook, no opinion.

**What breaking sounds like:** it is genuinely more informative and completely unanswerable. Read
it back and try to think of a follow-up question. You will struggle, and you will have just
discovered why the polished version of an introduction is the worse one.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are introduced to someone, you give the polished résumé version, and there is a small pause.
They say "oh, nice." Then somebody changes the subject, and you have the distinct feeling that
you were fine and it did not go anywhere.

Nothing went wrong. That is the problem — a closed introduction is a correct answer to a question
nobody was really asking. They wanted a door, and you handed them a document.

**The repair:**

> …anyway, the bit I'm still figuring out is {{something unfinished}}. What about you?

Add the hook late if you left it out, then hand back. It works even several sentences after the
introduction has technically ended, because you are not correcting anything — you are just
continuing.
