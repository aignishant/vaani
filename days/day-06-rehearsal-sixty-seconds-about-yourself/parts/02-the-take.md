# The take, and listening to it without flinching

`CF-03` · Day 6

## The one-line answer

The playback is the actual exercise, and the only skill it needs is the ability to hear a mistake
without deciding anything about yourself.

## The model

You have now recorded yourself six times. If you are like most people, you have listened
properly perhaps twice, and both times a particular thing happened: somewhere around ten seconds
in you heard something bad, a hot feeling arrived, and after that you were no longer listening.
You were bracing.

Bracing is the problem. It is not that the playback is unpleasant — it is that the unpleasantness
consumes the attention you needed for the information. You come away with a verdict (*"that was
awful"*) instead of data (*"I said 'is' three times when I meant 'was'"*), and a verdict cannot be
practised.

The fix is mechanical rather than emotional. **Listen with a job.** A listener with a specific
task does not have room to form a verdict.

Tonight's job is small and countable: find two things. Not the two worst things. Just two
specific, quotable things — an actual word you said, and the word you meant.

Two is deliberately low. The instinct is to catalogue everything, which produces a page of
findings, no action, and a bad evening. Two findings a day, for seventy days, is a hundred and
forty findings — and by Day 67 you will discover they are mostly the same four errors repeating,
which is the entire design of `docs/ERRORS.md`.

> Right — what did I actually say there?
> That's a new one, that's going in the ledger.

**Today's phrases** — appended tonight with the others.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Let me rephrase that. | Mid-conversation, after hearing yourself go wrong |
| What I should have said was … | Reviewing, or correcting a step late |
| That came out wrong. | Immediate, casual repair |

## Say it now

Before the playback, say this out loud, once, at normal volume:

1. *I'm looking for two things, and then I'm done.*

This sounds silly. Do it anyway. Saying the scope out loud is what stops the cataloguing.

## Say it yours

**No new recording. This rep is the playback of `audio/d06-sixty.m4a`.**

> **Play the take once, all the way through, without pausing and without stopping it early.**
> Then write exactly two rows into `docs/ERRORS.md`.

Each row needs the actual words. Not "my pronunciation was bad" — *"I said 'bad' where I meant
'bed'"*, or *"I said 'I am working here since three years'"*. A row you cannot practise is not a
row.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** they listen too and offer one finding. Take it as data. Do not defend the
  take.

## The deliberate failure

Play the first twenty seconds again and this time **let yourself judge it freely** — allow the
verdict, the cringe, the whole thing.

**What breaking sounds like:** notice that you stop hearing words. You can feel the discomfort
clearly and you could not quote a single sentence back. That is the exact mechanism that has kept
you from improving from your own recordings until now, and you have just watched it operate on
purpose, which makes it much easier to spot next time.

## When it breaks in the wild

Someone records a meeting, or you get a voice note back, and you never listen to it. Weeks of
free evidence about your own speaking, discarded, because the first three seconds are
uncomfortable.

**The repair:**

> Two things, then I'm done.

Same rule, applied outside the course. It works on real recordings for the same reason it works
here.
