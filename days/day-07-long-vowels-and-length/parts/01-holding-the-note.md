# Holding the note

`PR-03` · Day 7

## The one-line answer

Some English vowel pairs are not two different sounds — they are the same sound held short or
held long, and the length alone changes the word.

## The model

A singer holding a note for one beat and the same note for three beats has not changed the note.
The pitch is identical. The only difference is duration, and any listener hears it immediately as
two different things.

English does that with vowels, and it is worth knowing because it is genuinely strange. Most
languages treat vowel length as decoration — you can stretch a vowel for emphasis and the word
stays the same word. In English, for a handful of pairs, the stretch **is** the word.

Five pairs carry most of the load:

> **ship** — **sheep**
> **full** — **fool**
> **cot** — **caught**
> **pull** — **pool**
> **live** (as in, where you live) — **leave**

Say *ship*, then *sheep*. Most learners produce the same length twice and try to make the
difference with the tongue alone. It does not carry. The tongue does move — *sheep* is tighter
and more forward — but **length is doing most of the work**, and it is the part you can control
consciously from today.

Here is the physical trick. Tap your finger on the table once for the short one, and hold your
finger down for the long one:

> tap — *ship*
> hooooold — *sheeeep*

Exaggerate it well past what feels reasonable. You will be convinced you sound ridiculous. Record
it and you will find you have landed roughly on normal. That gap — between what feels extreme and
what sounds normal — is a real feature of learning any second-language contrast, and the way
through it is to trust the recording over the feeling.

One more thing, and it is the part that catches people out. **A long vowel gets shortened when
the word ends in a hard sound.** The vowel in *leave* is longer than the one in *leaf*, even
though both are the long one. You do not need to do anything about this deliberately — it happens
by itself once the contrast exists. It matters only so that you do not hear it in a recording and
think you have made a mistake.

## Say it now

Five pairs. Say each **five times, alternating**, with the finger-tap. Tap for short, hold for
long.

1. *ship — sheep*
2. *full — fool*
3. *cot — caught*
4. *pull — pool*
5. *live — leave*

Then in sentences, where it is much harder, three times each:

6. *I'll leave it with you — I live just round the corner.*
7. *Give it a bit longer; it's not a big deal.*
8. *That's a bit of a leap, to be fair.*

Line 6 is the one to spend time on. Two members of the same pair in one sentence is the hardest
version of this and the version real speech actually produces.

## The deliberate failure

Record twenty seconds where you make **every** vowel long. Stretch all of them. *I liiive on a
streeeet neeear the paaark.*

**What breaking sounds like:** the rhythm goes flat. English rhythm depends on some syllables
being much shorter than others, so making everything long does not make you sound careful — it
makes you sound like you are reading a list. It also, oddly, becomes harder to understand than
the short-everything version.

That is the finding: **length is a contrast, not a quality.** Long only means something because
short exists next to it. This is also a preview of Day 13, where the same principle runs at the
level of whole words.

## When it breaks in the wild

You say *"I want to leave"* and someone hears *"I want to live"*, or you say a number and it is
misheard, and the conversation goes visibly sideways for a few seconds before someone works it
out.

Length errors fail differently from shape errors. A shape error usually produces a non-word that
gets queried immediately. **A length error produces a different real word**, which does not get
queried — it just quietly means something else, sometimes for the rest of the conversation.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — leave, as in leave the building. Not live.

Give the contrasting word explicitly. Repeating the same word louder does not help; the listener
heard you perfectly and mapped it to the other word.
