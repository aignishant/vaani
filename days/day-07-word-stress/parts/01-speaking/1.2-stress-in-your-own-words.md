## One-line answer

Take the ten words you say most about yourself, find the loud syllable in each, and say them with the recorder on until the shape is yours.

## The story

You are at the station counter again. Now it is your turn to speak. You ask for a ticket to
your home town. The man does not move. You say it again, louder. Still nothing. Then you
remember the woman beside you, and you say the town's name with one syllable big and the rest
small. He nods and prints the ticket. Loudness did not help. Shape did.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **hit** — say a syllable with extra force.
- **shrink** — become smaller and shorter.
- **check** — look at the dictionary to be sure.
- **own words** — the words about your life, not from a list.

In [part 1.1](1.1-the-loud-syllable.md) you learned that a **syllable** is one piece of a
word and that **stress** is the extra force on one syllable. Now you do it on the words you
will actually say. Not a list from a book. Your city. Your job. Your family words. Your
favourite food. These are the words in your day 20 self-introduction, and they are the words a
stranger needs to catch first.

For every word, the dictionary tells you the answer. You do not have to guess. The stressed
syllable has a small mark ˈ before it. You read the mark, you press the sound button, you copy
the shape.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every later speaking day uses these ten words. On [day 9](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed)
you introduce yourself in five spoken sentences. If your city has the wrong shape there, it
will have the wrong shape at the gate. Fix the ten words now, and every day after this borrows
the fix.

## The rule

Make a list of ten words you will say about yourself. Pick from these groups, and use your
real answers:

- your city or town, and your state or country
- your work, or the word student, and the place you work or study
- two family words you use most
- two things you like: a food, a game, a kind of music
- two words from your daily life: a place you go, a thing you use

For each word:

1. Say it slowly and count the syllables with taps.
2. Look it up. Find the mark ˈ. That syllable is the loud one. Press the sound button.
3. Say it three times with the recorder on: slow, normal, then inside a short sentence.
4. Play the dictionary and your recording one after the other. Same shape? Move on. Different?
   Say it again.

Two examples of the shape, so you can see how to write your own list. Choose your own words:

> DOCtor. I'm a DOCtor.
> comPUter. I work on a comPUter.

For the words checked in this part, the pages were opened on 2026-09-22 and each has a sound
button: [hotel](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/hotel) /həʊˈtel/,
stress on the second syllable, and
[breakfast](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/breakfast)
/ˈbrekfəst/, stress on the first.

Names of towns and people are often not in a learner's dictionary. For those, listen to how a
local person says the name, and copy that shape. Your own name is yours; say it the way your
family says it, and keep the loud syllable steady every time.

The trap: place names from your own language may carry equal syllables in your language and
one loud syllable when said in English. Both are right in their own language. In an English
sentence, give the name one loud syllable, or it will sound like a pause in the sentence.

## When it breaks

You say "hotel" with the stress on the first syllable:

> HOtel

Many learners do this, because "hotel" is a two-syllable noun, and most two-syllable nouns
stress the first syllable. But hotel is one of the exceptions. The listener hears something
close to "hot tell". The fix is to check, not to guess: the dictionary says hoTEL.

You say your job word with all syllables equal, because you have said it that way for years:

> en-gi-neer

The listener waits for the word to finish. The fix: find the loud one, engiNEER, and hit it.

If your recording and the dictionary do not match on three of your ten words, the check is RED
for those three. Say them again.

## In real life

Fluent speakers do not think about stress. They learned each word with its shape, the way you
learned the tune of a song with its words. That is what you are doing now, one word at a time,
starting with the ten that matter most.

Under pressure, at a counter or on a bad phone line, the shape is the last thing to go. A
native listener will forgive a wrong consonant inside a word with the right shape. They will
not catch a word with perfect consonants and the wrong shape; they will ask you to repeat.
