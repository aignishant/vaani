## One-line answer

Say a word with the stress on the wrong syllable on purpose, play it back, hear how the word hides, then say it right beside the wrong one.

## The story

Back at the station. You try something. You say your home town's name to the counter man three
ways: loud on the first syllable, loud on the last, then the way the locals say it. On the
first two, his eyebrows go up. On the third, his hand goes to the ticket machine. You have just
heard, from the outside, what stress does. Now you will do the same with the recorder as your
counter man.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong versions are deliberate. They rest on what beginners
commonly do, not on a mistake found in your own recordings.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **vanish** — disappear; go where nobody can find it.
- **row** — three things said one after another.
- **repair** — a small correction that helps someone understand, from
  [day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.3-change-one-sound.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You know the rule from [part 1.1](1.1-the-loud-syllable.md) and you have your ten words from
[part 1.2](1.2-stress-in-your-own-words.md). Now you break the rule on purpose, so your ear
learns what wrong sounds like. A mistake you can hear is a mistake you can fix in the middle of
a sentence. A mistake you cannot hear stays for years.

## Why Vaani needs it

On [day 13](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed) you learn
sentence rhythm, which is built on word stress. If your ear cannot catch a moved stress in one
word today, it will not catch a flat sentence then. And at the day 20 check, only your own ear
is there to repair a wrong shape before the stranger asks.

## The rule

Take three of your ten words with three or more syllables. For each, record a row of three:

1. **Wrong, on purpose.** Put the stress on the first syllable, whether or not that is right.
2. **Wrong, on purpose.** Put the stress on the last syllable.
3. **Right.** The dictionary shape.

The model, on the word from part 1.1:

> PHO-to-graph-er — pho-to-graph-ER — pho-TO-graph-er

Play the row back. Listen as a stranger. Ask: in which of the three do I hear a real word? If
all three sound the same to you, you are not yet moving the stress. Make the loud syllable much
louder and much longer than you think is polite. Record again.

Now the repair. Say one true sentence about yourself with one of the three words in it. Say
the word wrong, stop, and say it right, the way you would if someone looked puzzled:

> I want to be a PHOtographer — a phoTOgrapher.

The shape was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[photographer](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/photographer),
/fəˈtɒɡrəfə(r)/, which carries a sound button.

## When it breaks

The break this part is about: you record the row, and all three sound the same.

> pho-to-graph-er — pho-to-graph-er — pho-to-graph-er

Your mouth said three things, but your voice did not. This is the most common result on a
first try, because it feels rude to make one syllable so much bigger. The fix: say the loud
syllable as if calling across a road. Then bring it down a little.

A second break: you move the stress, and the vowel in the old stressed syllable stays clear
and long.

> PHO-TO-graph-er

Now two syllables are loud. That is two stresses, and English words carry one. The fix: when
you move the stress, let the syllable you left shrink to a small quiet sound.

If your row has no audible difference on playback, the check is RED. Record again.

## In real life

Nobody moves stress on purpose in a real conversation. But everyone who learned English as an
adult carries a few words with the stress in the wrong place, learned from reading before
hearing. A fluent speaker who hears a puzzled look does exactly what you practised: says the
word again with the shape fixed, no apology, and carries on. That small repair is a normal part
of speaking, not a failure.

A native listener notices a moved stress at once and often cannot say why the word sounded
odd. They forgive it in a long rare word. In your city's name or your job, they will ask, and
the question costs you the flow of your sentence. Better to catch it yourself.
