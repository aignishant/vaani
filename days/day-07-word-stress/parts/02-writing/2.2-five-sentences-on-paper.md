## One-line answer

Write your five true sentences in one block, name first and like last, each with its capital letter and full stop, and read it back as the person at the desk.

## The story

The library woman gives you a fresh form. This time you know what goes in the box. You write
your name line. Then where you live. Then your work. Then who you live with. Then the one thing
you like. She reads it in one look and says, "Ah, you're near the bus stand. Cricket books are
on the left." Five lines, and she already knows how to help you. That is what an order does.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **block** — several sentences written together, one after another.
- **order** — which sentence comes first, second, third.
- **follow** — understand each line because of the line before it.
- **fresh** — new, clean, not used.

In [part 2.1](2.1-one-true-fact-per-sentence.md) you made five facts, one per line. Now you
put them together in the order a stranger expects: who you are, where you are, what you do,
who is with you, what you like. Big to small. A reader meets your name first, and your
favourite food last, the same way it would happen if you met them in person.

Each sentence keeps its own [capital letter](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.1-capital-and-small-letters.md)
and its own [full stop](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md).
Five sentences, five capitals, five full stops. The reader counts them without knowing they
are counting.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is ten sentences in a

## The rule

1. Take your five lines from part 2.1. Put them in this order: name, place, work or study,
   people, one like.
2. Write them as one block, each sentence starting right after the last one's full stop, with
   one space between.
3. Put your finger on the first letter of each sentence. Is it a capital? Put your finger on
   the last mark of each sentence. Is it a full stop?
4. Read the block out loud in the voice of the woman at the desk. After each sentence, ask:
   do I know one more thing about this person than I did before?

The model, for a person who is not you:

> I am Meera. I live in Nashik, near the bus stand. I work in a cloth shop. I live with my
> parents and my younger brother. I like cricket.

Notice the second sentence. "Near the bus stand" is extra, but it is still one fact about
place. You may add a small detail to a line. You may not add a second kind of fact to it.
Work stays on the work line.

The shapes of a written self-introduction were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Online course introductions](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/online-course-introductions),
which says to "keep your introduction short and clear".

The trap: writing each sentence on its own line, like a list. A list is what a form wants. A
block is what a message, a letter or a page wants. On day 20 you write a page. Practise the
block now.

## When it breaks

You write the five lines in the order they came into your head:

> I like cricket. I live with my parents. I am Meera. I work in a cloth shop. I live in Nashik.

Every sentence is true and correct. But the reader meets your cricket before your name, and
has to hold four facts before they know who you are. The fix: start with the name, end with
the like.

You lose a full stop at the join between two sentences, because you were thinking about the
next fact:

> I work in a cloth shop I live with my parents.

The reader runs the two together and reads "a cloth shop I live with". A
[run-on](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md). The fix:
finger on the end of each sentence, and put the full stop back.

If your block has fewer than five capitals or fewer than five full stops, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody writes a perfect five-line block to a friend. But the order is the order fluent writers
use without thinking: name, where, what, who, a small personal thing. Read any short
introduction in a new group and you will see it. Break the order and the message still works,
but the reader does a little extra work, and a stranger who does extra work stops reading
sooner.

A formal reader, an office or a landlord, reads the first sentence and decides whether to read
the rest. Give them your name first. Everything else follows.
