## One-line answer

A greeting is a pair: you say hello, they say hello back, and "How are you?" expects a short answer and the same question returned.

## The story

You meet your upstairs neighbour on the stairs every morning. He says "Good morning! How are
you?" and you say "Good." He waits half a second, then goes on down. The next day the same.
After a week he just nods. You did nothing wrong, you think. You answered. But you never
asked him back, so to him the door closed each time.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **greeting** — the words you say when you meet someone.
- **turn** — one person's part of a talk; then it is the other person's turn.
- **return** — give back; ask the same question back.
- **nod** — move your head down once to say hello without words.
- **stairs** — steps inside a building.

A greeting in English is two turns, and often four. You say hello. They say hello. One of you
asks "How are you?" The other answers in three or four words and asks it back. Only then does
the real talk start, or you both go on your way.

"How are you?" is not a real question about your health. It is a greeting shaped like a
question. The answer is short and the question comes back. A long true answer about your bad
knee is for a friend who asks a second time.

The time of day changes the first word. Good morning until midday. Good afternoon until the
evening. Good evening after that. "Hello" and "Hi" work at any time. "Good night" is not a
greeting at all; it is a goodbye, and it comes in [part 1.2](1.2-goodbye-and-see-you.md).

At the [day 20 speaking check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), your

## The rule

The four turns, with two people:

> Hello. How are you?
> I'm fine, thanks. And you?
> I'm good, thanks.

That is one complete greeting. Three lines, two people, and both have asked and answered.

Your answers can be any of these short shapes. Pick the true one:

| Feeling | Say |
| --- | --- |
| good | I'm fine, thanks. / I'm good, thanks. / Very well, thank you. |
| so-so | Not bad. / I'm okay. |
| tired | A bit tired, but fine. |

Then always: "And you?" or "How are you?" back.

The words were checked on 2026-09-22. [hello](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/hello)
/həˈləʊ/ has the stress on the second syllable; say hel-LO, not HEL-lo.
[good morning](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/good-morning),
[good afternoon](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/good-afternoon)
and [good evening](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/good-evening)
each carry a sound button. Press it, say the phrase with the recorder on, and play both back.
The greeting shape "Hi, I'm …" and "Nice to meet you" appear in
[Meeting new people](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/meeting-new-people),
opened the same day.

Stand in front of the mirror. Say the three lines, both parts, changing your voice a little for
the second person. Your face should lift on "Hello". A greeting with a flat face reads as a
complaint.

The trap: in many languages the polite answer to "How are you?" is a real answer, or silence
and a smile. In English the polite answer is short, and the question comes back. Without the
return, the other person hears "I don't want to talk."

## When it breaks

You answer and stop:

> Good.

One word, no thanks, no return. The listener hears a full stop. The fix: "I'm good, thanks.
And you?" Three small pieces, and the door stays open.

You say the wrong time word:

> Good night!

as you arrive at the office in the evening. The listener hears you leaving. The fix: "Good
evening." Night is for going.

If, on playback, your "How are you?" answer has no "thanks" and no return question, the check
is RED.

## In real life

Fast speakers squash the greeting: "Hi, how're you?" "Good, you?" "Good." It is over in a
breath, but all four turns are still there. Listen for them. Under pressure, a busy shop, a
late bus, people drop to a nod and "Morning!" and that is a full greeting too, because both
people give one.

A native listener forgives a slow greeting. They do not forgive a missing return; it reads as
cold, even when you meant nothing by it. The return question is the smallest thing in this
part and the one that matters most.
