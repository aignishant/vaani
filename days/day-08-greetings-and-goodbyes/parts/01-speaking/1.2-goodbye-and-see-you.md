## One-line answer

A goodbye names the next meeting, "See you tomorrow", "See you later", or wishes the other person well, "Have a good day", and "Good night" is only for the end of the day.

## The story

Your neighbour and you now greet each other properly on the stairs. Then comes the moment at
the bottom, where he turns left and you turn right. You say nothing, or you say "Okay". He
says "See you tomorrow!" and you realise that his goodbye had tomorrow in it. Yours had
nothing. The next morning he greets you first, again, because your leaving never promised
another meeting.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **goodbye** — the words you say when you leave someone.
- **later** — at a time after now, today.
- **wish** — say that you hope something good for someone.
- **promise** — say that something will happen.

In [part 1.1](1.1-hello-and-how-are-you.md) you learned that a greeting is two turns. A
goodbye is two turns as well, and it does one of two jobs. Either it names when you will meet
again: later today, tomorrow, next week. Or it wishes the other person something: a good day,
a good weekend, a good night's sleep. Often it does both.

"Bye" alone is fine between friends. To a neighbour, a shopkeeper or a colleague, add the next
meeting or the wish. It costs two words and it is what makes the goodbye warm.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every recorded conversation from [day 9](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed)
onward has a start and an end. A recording that stops after the last fact sounds cut off. The
day 20 self-introduction ends with a goodbye or a thank-you, and a stranger hears whether the
end was chosen or just happened.

## The rule

Choose one from the left and one from the right, or one from each:

| Name the next meeting | Wish them well |
| --- | --- |
| See you later. | Have a good day. |
| See you tomorrow. | Have a good weekend. |
| See you on Monday. | Take care. |
| See you soon. | Good night. |

The two turns:

> Bye! See you tomorrow.
> See you. Have a good day.

Say each line at the mirror. On "See you", your voice goes up a little, like a small wave. On
"Good night", it goes down and slows, because the day is ending.

The words were checked on 2026-09-22 and each carries a sound button:
[goodbye](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/goodbye) /ˌɡʊdˈbaɪ/,
stress on bye; [bye](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/bye_1);
[goodnight](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/goodnight). The
closing phrases "See you soon", "Take care" and "Keep in touch" appear as tips in
[A thank-you email](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/thank-you-email),
opened the same day.

The trap is "Good night". It looks like "Good morning" and "Good evening", so beginners use it
to say hello after dark. It never says hello. It says "I am going to sleep" or "we are done for
today". Arrive with "Good evening"; leave with "Good night".

## When it breaks

You leave without a goodbye word:

> Okay.

The listener is not sure you have left. They may keep talking to your back. The fix: "Okay,
bye! See you tomorrow." Now the leaving is clear and the next meeting is in it.

You say goodbye in a flat, falling voice:

> See you later.

with the tune of "It is raining." The listener hears that you do not want to see them later.
The fix: let "See you" lift, like a small wave of the hand.

If, on playback, your goodbye names no next meeting and gives no wish, the check is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers pile goodbyes up: "Right, I'd better go. Bye! See you Monday. Have a good
weekend." Three or four small phrases, said while already moving. You do not need the pile.
One next-meeting phrase or one wish is a full goodbye.

Under pressure, a bus arriving, a phone ringing, people drop to "Bye!" with a wave, and the
wave does the work of the wish. On the phone there is no wave, so the words carry it all:
"Okay, thanks. Bye!" A native listener notices a call that ends without a bye as a call that
was cut off, and may call you back to check.
