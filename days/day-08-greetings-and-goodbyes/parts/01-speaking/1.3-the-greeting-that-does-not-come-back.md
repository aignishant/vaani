## One-line answer

Record the greeting with the return question missing and the goodbye with "Good night" in the wrong place, play both back as the neighbour, then record the repaired versions beside them.

## The story

You decide to test it. One morning on the stairs you answer your neighbour with "Good." and
nothing else, on purpose, and you watch. He waits, then goes. The next morning you say "Good,
thanks. And you?" and he stops, tells you about his daughter's exam, and you talk for a
minute. Same stairs, same man. Two words changed everything. Now do the test with the recorder
as the neighbour.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong versions are deliberate. They rest on what beginners
commonly do, not on a mistake found in your own recordings.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **door closes** — the talk ends before the other person wanted it to.
- **beside** — next to; right after.
- **repair** — a small correction that helps someone understand, from
  [day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.3-change-one-sound.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the greeting from [part 1.1](1.1-hello-and-how-are-you.md) and the goodbye from
[part 1.2](1.2-goodbye-and-see-you.md). Now you break each on purpose. The greeting loses its
return. The goodbye uses "Good night" as a hello. You listen as the other person. You hear the
door close. Then you record the repair right beside the break, so your ear has both.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), the stranger's first
impression is the greeting and the last is the goodbye. Both are small, and small things are
what the ear catches first. A learner who has heard their own closed door on a recording does
not close it on the day.

## The rule

Two rows. Record each as a short exchange with two voices, wrong first, then right.

**Row 1 — the missing return.**

> Morning! How are you? — Good.
> Morning! How are you? — Good, thanks. And you?

**Row 2 — the wrong time word.**

> Good night! How are you? — (arriving, in the evening)
> Good evening! How are you?

Play each row back. Listen as the neighbour. After the wrong version, ask out loud: "Do I want
to keep talking to this person?" After the right version, ask again. If the answer does not
change, make the wrong version flatter and shorter, and the right version warmer, and record
again.

Then the repair in one breath, the way it happens in real life when you hear yourself:

> Good. — Sorry, I mean: good, thanks. And you?

The greeting words were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[hello](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/hello),
[good evening](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/good-evening) and
[goodnight](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/goodnight), each with
a sound button.

## When it breaks

The break this part is about: on playback, the wrong and the right version sound the same to
you.

> Good. — Good, thanks. And you?

If both sound fine, your ear is still hearing the words and not the door. Play the wrong one
and count the silence after it. That silence is what the neighbour hears. Then play the right
one; there is no silence, because the question has handed the turn back.

A second break: your repair makes it worse.

> Good. — Good. Good, thanks.

You repeated the word instead of adding the return. The fix is not louder or longer. It is
"And you?" at the end.

If you cannot say which version in each row is the wrong one on playback, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody drops the return on purpose. But everyone whose first language greets with a nod or a
real answer drops it by habit, and never hears the door close, because the other person is too
polite to say. The neighbour who stops greeting you is the only signal, and it comes weeks
late. Your recording gives you the signal today.

A native listener forgives a missing return from a stranger in a hurry. From a neighbour, a
colleague or a shopkeeper they see daily, they read it as distance. The repair, "Sorry, and
you?", even a few seconds late, reopens the door every time.
