## One-line answer

This points at one thing near you and that points at one thing away from you, so the word tells the reader where to look before they know what they are looking at.

## The story

You are at the vegetable market with a list your mother wrote. It says "the tomatoes". Which
ones? There are tomatoes on this cart in front of you and tomatoes on that cart across the
lane, and they are not the same price. You send her a photo and a message: "This one or that
one?" She writes back at once: "That one." Two small words, and she has pointed across a
market she cannot see.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **point** — show where something is with a finger or a word.
- **near** — close to you.
- **far** — away from you.
- **cart** — a small table on wheels that sells things.
- **lane** — a narrow road.

This and that are **pointing words**. Each one points at a
[noun](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md), a word for a
person or a thing. This points at one thing near you. That points at one thing away from you.
Both are for **one** thing only, the
[singular](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md).

On paper there is no finger. The word is the finger. A reader who sees "this cart" looks near
the writer. A reader who sees "that cart" looks across the lane. That is why the word matters
on paper more than in speech: in speech your hand helps; on paper the word is alone.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every page you write from here has things in it, and you will point at them. On
[day 18](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed) you describe
your room on paper: this chair, that window. A reader who cannot tell near from far cannot
picture the room.

## The rule

| Word | How many | Where | Say it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| this | one | near me | [this](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/this_1) /ðɪs/ |
| that | one | away from me | [that](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/that_1) /ðæt/ |

The two pages were opened on 2026-09-22. The definitions read: this is "used to refer to a
particular person, thing or event that is close to you"; that is "used for referring to a
person or thing that is not near the speaker".

Two ways to use each word. Before a noun, as the finger on the noun:

> This cart has cheap tomatoes.
> That cart has better ones.

Or alone, standing for the thing, when the reader already knows what you mean:

> This is my cart. That is my brother's.

The rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Demonstratives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/demonstratives):
"We use this (singular) and these (plural) as pronouns to talk about people or things near
us", and "that (singular) and those (plural)" for "things that are not near us".

A small picture of the market lane, so you can see near and far:

```text
   you ──► [this cart]            ...lane...            [that cart]
             near                                          far
```

The trap: near and far are measured from the writer, not from the reader. When you write
"this cart" in a message, the reader looks near you, wherever you are.

## When it breaks

You write that for the thing right in front of you:

> That cart has cheap tomatoes.

and you mean the one you are standing at. The reader looks across the lane and sees the wrong
cart. The fix: this, for near.

You use this for something far away and already gone:

> I saw a nice cart yesterday. This cart had good onions.

The reader looks around for a cart near you now. The fix: "That cart had good onions." Far in
time is far too.

If your sentence has this for a far thing or that for a near one, that sentence is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers use this and that for more than carts. "That's a good idea" points at what you
just said. "This is my number" points at what is right here in the message. Both are the same
finger, near and far, in time as well as space. You do not need those uses today; you need to
see that the finger is the same.

In a quick message, a reader forgives a wrong pointing word if a photo is attached. In a note
with no photo, a form, or a list for someone else to shop from, the word is all they have, and
the wrong one sends them to the wrong cart.
