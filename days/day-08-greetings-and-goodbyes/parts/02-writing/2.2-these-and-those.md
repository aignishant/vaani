## One-line answer

These points at more than one thing near you and those at more than one thing away from you, and the noun after them takes its plural -s.

## The story

Back at the market. Now your mother's list says "onions, the small ones". There is a heap of
small onions at your feet and a heap of big ones on the far cart. You write: "These onions are
small. Those are big. Which?" She writes: "These." You buy the heap at your feet. Four onions
or forty, the words did the pointing, and the -s on onions told her you meant the heap, not
one onion.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **heap** — a pile of many things together.
- **more than one** — two, three, or many.
- **match** — go together correctly.
- **pair** — two things that belong together.

In [part 2.1](2.1-this-and-that.md) you learned this and that: one thing, near or far. These
and those are the same two fingers for **more than one** thing, the
[plural](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md). These is near.
Those is far.

The pointing word and the noun must match. One thing: this cart, that cart. More than one:
these carts, those carts. The -s on the noun and the word before it say the same thing twice,
and a reader trusts a page where the two agree.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) about you has plural
things in it: your books, your brothers, your shoes. When you point at them, the pointing word
has to be plural too, or the reader stops to work out how many you mean.

## The rule

The four words together:

| | one | more than one |
| --- | --- | --- |
| near | this | these |
| far | that | those |

Say them. [these](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/these) is
/ðiːz/, with the long vowel and a buzzing z at the end;
[those](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/those) is /ðəʊz/. Both
pages were opened on 2026-09-22 and carry a sound button. On paper, the difference from this
and that is the letters; in the ear, it is the vowel and the z.

Before a plural noun:

> These onions are small.
> Those onions are big.

Or alone, standing for the heap:

> These are small. Those are big.

The rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Demonstratives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/demonstratives):
"this (singular) and these (plural)" for things near us; "that (singular) and those (plural)"
for things not near us.

Now the check that makes it yours. Take each sentence you write and put a finger on the
pointing word and a finger on the noun. Ask: do both say one, or do both say many? This onion.
These onions. Never this onions. Never these onion.

The trap: some nouns from [day 4](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.2-the-ones-that-break-it.md)
do not take -s for many: people, children, men. The pointing word still changes. These people.
Those children. The noun broke the -s rule; the pointing word did not.

## When it breaks

You write this with a plural noun:

> This onions are small.

The reader sees one finger and many onions, and does not know which you meant. The fix: these
onions.

You write these with one thing:

> These is my cart.

Now the reader expects a heap of carts and gets one. The fix: this is my cart.

If a pointing word and its noun disagree on how many, that sentence is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers make these and those agree without thinking, because they hear the -s and the
pointing word as one sound. Beginners whose first language has one pointing word for one and
many have to look twice. The two-finger check is slow today and gone in a week.

A friend reading "this onions" understands you. A shop list, a note to a flatmate, or an order
to a supplier does not have a friend reading it. "Send this boxes" gets you a question back
and a delay. "Send these boxes" gets you boxes.
