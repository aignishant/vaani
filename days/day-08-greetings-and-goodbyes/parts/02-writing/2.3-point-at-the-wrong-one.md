## One-line answer

Write a short shopping note with three pointing words wrong on purpose, read it as the person who has to shop from it, mark each wrong finger, then fix only those.

## The story

Now you write the list. Your brother is going to the market for you, and he does not know the
carts. You write: "Buy this tomatoes from the near cart. That onions on the far cart are good.
Not this one, the other." He comes back with the wrong tomatoes and one onion. He read every
word. The words pointed the wrong way.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong version is deliberate. It rests on what beginners
commonly write, not on a mistake found in your own page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **note** — a short piece of writing for one person.
- **shopper** — the person who goes and buys.
- **read back** — read your own page again, slowly, as if it were someone else's, from
  [day 2](../../../day-02-long-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.3-a-form-nobody-can-use.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the four words from [part 2.1](2.1-this-and-that.md) and
[part 2.2](2.2-these-and-those.md). Now you plant three wrong ones in a note: one that says
one where you mean many, one that says many where you mean one, one that says near where you
mean far. Then you read as your brother, who has only the note.

## Why Vaani needs it

On [day 18](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed) you
describe your room on paper for a reader who has never seen it. Every wrong pointing word puts
a thing in the wrong place. If your read-back cannot catch three planted wrong fingers today,
it will not catch one real one then.

## The rule

Under your correct sentences from parts 2.1 and 2.2:

1. Write a note of four or five sentences for someone shopping for you, pointing at real
   things in a real shop or market you know. Use all four words, correctly.
2. Copy it with three deliberate breaks: swap one this for these or the other way round; swap
   one that for those or the other way round; swap one near word for a far word.
3. Cover the correct note.
4. Read the broken note out loud as the shopper. At each pointing word, stop and ask two
   things: "One or many?" and "Near the writer or far?" Then look at the noun and the sentence.
   Do they agree?
5. Mark each wrong finger RED. There should be three.
6. Uncover. Fix only the three, on a new line, and mark it GREEN.

The model, broken on purpose:

> Buy this tomatoes from the near cart. That onions on the far cart are good. Take those bag
> by the door.

Three RED marks: this tomatoes should be these tomatoes; that onions should be those onions;
those bag should be that bag, or the bag by the door has become many.

The rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Demonstratives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/demonstratives).

## When it breaks

You read the broken note back and mark it GREEN:

> Buy this tomatoes from the near cart. That onions on the far cart are good. — GREEN

If your first language has one pointing word for one and many, "this tomatoes" reads fine to
you. The check itself failed. Read again with a finger on the pointing word and a finger on
the noun, asking "one or many?" out loud.

Another break: you fix the finger and break the noun.

> Buy this tomato from the near cart.

The words now agree, but you wanted many tomatoes. You fixed the wrong half. When the noun is
right, change the pointing word, not the noun.

If any planted break was marked GREEN, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody plants a wrong pointing word. But everyone whose first language has fewer of them
writes "this" for everything, and readers quietly fix it in their heads, or they don't and
come back with one onion. The two-question stop at each pointing word is the habit. It is slow
today. In a week, "this tomatoes" will look wrong before you finish writing it.

A friend at the market calls you and asks. A supplier, a delivery person or a form does not
call; they act on the note. "Send this boxes to that address" with the wrong words sends the
wrong number of boxes to the wrong place. Keep the broken note on your practice page as proof
that your read-back can go RED.
