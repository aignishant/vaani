# Hesitating out loud

`FL-04`, `FN-01` · Day 9

## The one-line answer

Fluent speakers do not think faster than you — they think **out loud, in English, while still
holding the floor**, and that is the entire trick.

## The model

Here is a myth worth dropping today: that fluent speakers produce sentences smoothly because they
have already worked them out. They have not. Listen to anyone speaking their own language
unscripted and it is full of stalling — restarts, *sort of*, *I mean*, *what's the word*, whole
seconds of nothing being said.

The difference is not the amount of hesitation. It is **what the hesitation is made of.**

Your hesitation is currently silence, and silence is ambiguous. To a listener, a silence in the
middle of your turn reads as *finished, or in trouble* — and either way they start preparing to
take the floor. You then have to fight your way back into a conversation you had not meant to
leave.

Filled hesitation is unambiguous. It says: *still going, give me a second.* Same delay, opposite
signal.

There are three kinds and they do different jobs.

**Stalling phrases** buy several words of time while sounding like content: *that's a good
question, actually.* Cheap, effective, slightly overused — but nobody has ever been penalised
for one.

**Thinking aloud** buys the most time and costs the least, because it is honest: *I've never
really thought about that.* Now you are visibly thinking, and the listener waits, because you
have told them what the pause is for.

**Rephrasing forward** is the advanced one and the most useful. You do not stop; you say the same
thing again, differently, and use the second attempt to find where you were going: *it's more
of a — well, it's not exactly a problem, it's more that…*

What you should mostly avoid is not the sound *um*. It is *um* **stretched to fill the whole
gap**. A short *um* is normal in every language and every speaker. Four seconds of *ummmm* is a
turn being handed away.

> That's a good question, actually.
> I've never really thought about that.
> How do I put this…
> It's more of a — well, it's not exactly that, it's more that…
> Give me a second, I'll get there.
> Sorry, what's the word — you know when you…

That last one is the best line in this document. **"You know when you…"** buys you unlimited time
because the listener is now engaged in helping you find the word, and helping is the opposite of
waiting.

*how do I put this* and *the thing is* are already there from Days 1 and 4.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| That's a good question, actually. | You need three or four words of runway |
| I've never really thought about that. | Honest, and buys the most time of any line here |

## Say it now

Say each line **at normal conversational speed.** Filled hesitation said slowly and carefully
defeats itself — the whole point is that it sounds unremarkable.

1. *That's a good question, actually — I've never really thought about that.*
2. *It's more of a — well, it's not exactly a problem, it's more that it's slow.*
3. *Sorry, what's the word — you know when you leave something for too long and…*
4. *Bear with me. Give me a second, I'll get there.*

Now the drill that makes it stick. Say line 3 **and then keep going**, inventing the rest of the
sentence as you say it. Three times, with a different ending each time. The skill is not the
phrase; it is arriving at the far side of it still talking.

The rule for this rep, and it is the only rule:

**No silence longer than a breath.** If you do not know what to say, say that you do not know

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** they are instructed to start talking the moment you leave a silence. That
  makes the cost immediate and real.

## The deliberate failure

Record thirty more seconds on the same question with **all filled hesitation banned** — no
chunks, no *um*, no rephrasing. When you do not know what to say, you must be silent.

**What breaking sounds like:** the silences are not longer than usual, but they feel enormous
from the inside, and on playback they sound like the recording has stopped. You will also notice
that after each one you restart the sentence from the beginning rather than continuing it.

That restart is the hidden cost of silent hesitation, and it is why silent hesitation is slower
than filled hesitation even though it looks like it should be faster.

## When it breaks in the wild

Someone asks you something unexpected in a meeting. You pause to think. Two seconds in, somebody
else answers — not rudely, they simply read the silence as *finished* — and your answer, which
was good, never gets said.

**The repair:**

> That's a good question, actually. Give me a second — bear with me.

Said immediately, before the thinking starts. It costs one second and buys you as many as you
need, because you have relabelled the pause: it is no longer an empty space, it is a stated
intention to answer.
