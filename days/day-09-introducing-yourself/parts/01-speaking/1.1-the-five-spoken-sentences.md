## One-line answer

A spoken self-introduction is five short sentences in a fixed order, name, place, work, people, one like, each with a pause after it so the listener can catch up.

## The story

It is your first day at a new job. The manager gathers the team and says, "Let's go round.
Say a bit about yourself." The person before you speaks for a minute and you catch nothing
except a laugh at the end. Then it is you. You say your name and stop. Everyone waits. You
say your city. Silence. Nobody knows if you are finished. The manager says "Great!" too early
and moves on. You said true things. You did not give them a shape they could follow.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **introduce** — tell people who you are.
- **fixed** — always the same.
- **pause** — a short silence between sentences.
- **catch up** — understand what was just said before the next thing comes.
- **go round** — each person speaks in turn.

A **self-introduction**, from
[day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.2-your-name-aloud.md), is a message
that tells someone who you are. Spoken, it is five sentences, always in the same order:

1. your name
2. where you live or where you are from
3. what you do
4. who you live with, or how many people are in your family
5. one thing you like

Five is the number a stranger can hold. The order is the order they expect: big to small. And
a pause after each sentence is part of the sentence. It tells the listener "that one is done;
here comes the next."

## The rule

The five shapes, and a model for a person who is not you:

| Sentence | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | I'm + name. / My name is + name. |
| 2 | I'm from + place. / I live in + place. |
| 3 | I'm a + job. / I work in + place. / I'm a student. |
| 4 | I live with + people. / I have + number + family word. |
| 5 | I like + thing. |

> I'm Arjun. I'm from Nagpur. I work in a bank. I live with my wife and my son. I like old
> songs.

Say it at the mirror. After each full stop, close your mouth and count one beat. The pause is
where the listener puts your fact away. Without it, sentence two arrives while they are still
holding sentence one.

Use the short forms, I'm, not I am, in speech. They are what fluent speakers say, and they
were confirmed on 2026-09-22 at
[Present simple: 'to be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-be),
which gives I'm, you're, he's. The greeting shape "Hi, I'm Yuna" appears at
[Meeting new people](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/meeting-new-people),
opened the same day. Two of the words above carry a sound button:
[work](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/work_1) /wɜːk/ and
[wife](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/wife) /waɪf/, both opened
on 2026-09-22.

Now write your own five, one per line, and say them at the mirror, with the beat after each.
Your name, your city, your work, your people, your like.

The trap: sentence three when you do not have a job. "I'm a student" is a full sentence.
"I'm looking for work" is a full sentence. "I stay at home with my children" is a full
sentence. Nobody has an empty line three.

## When it breaks

You say sentence one and wait for a reaction before going on:

> I'm Arjun. ... ... ...

The listener does not know whether you are finished. The fix: one beat, then sentence two.
The pause is one beat, not a question.

You drop I'm and start with the fact:

> Arjun. Nagpur. Bank.

Three words, no sentences. The listener hears a form being read out. The fix: put I'm and I
back. They are what turn a list into a person.

If, on playback, you cannot count five sentences with a clear pause after each, the check is
RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers add colour: "I'm Arjun, and before you ask, yes, the cricketer's name." But
under the colour, the five are still there, in this order. Listen to any introduction round
and count. Under pressure, a big room, a boss watching, fluent speakers drop the colour and
say the five plainly. That is what you are learning first.

A native listener forgives a slow introduction. They lose a fast one with no pauses, and they
do not ask you to repeat; they smile and remember nothing. The pause is your friend in that
room.
