## One-line answer

Have is the verb for owning, and it takes the form has with one other person or thing, he, she, it, and have with everyone else.

## The story

You move into a hostel room with two others. On the first night you write a list on the wall so
nobody fights over things: who owns the kettle, who owns the fan, who has a charger to lend.
You write "Ravi have a kettle." Ravi reads it and says, "Ravi has." You ask what the
difference is. He says, "You have. I have. He has." You write it again, and the list goes up.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **own** — it is yours; it belongs to you.
- **lend** — let someone use your thing for a while.
- **hostel** — a building where students or workers rent a bed.
- **kettle** — a pot that heats water.
- **share** — use one thing between several people.

**Have** is a [verb](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md), a
word that says what someone does or is. Have says what someone owns. Like the verb
[be](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md), it changes its
[form](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md) with the person.
But have is simpler: there are only two forms. **Has** for one other person or thing, he, she,
it, or one name. **Have** for everyone else: I, you, we, they, or more than one name.

On paper the difference is one letter, s for ve. A reader sees it before they finish the word,
and it tells them how many people you are writing about.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) about you has your

and that day is about family, not about verbs.

## The rule

| Person | Form |
| --- | --- |
| I, you, we, they | have |
| he, she, it, one name | has |

> I have a kettle. You have a fan.
> Ravi has a charger. She has a lamp.
> We have one table. They have two chairs.

The forms were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got):
"I/you/we/they have got", "he/she/it has got", and the page says that "in many situations,
have and have got mean the same thing", with have "often used in writing". This course writes
have and has, without got. The word
[have](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/have_1) was opened the
same day, and its first meaning reads "to own, hold or possess something".

The test for every sentence: put a finger on the person at the start. One other person or
thing, not I, not you? Then has. Everyone else? Then have.

The trap: has looks like is, and both go with he and she. But they are different verbs. Is
says what someone is; has says what someone owns. "Ravi is a kettle" is a different sentence
from "Ravi has a kettle", and only one of them is true.

## When it breaks

You write have with one other person:

> Ravi have a kettle.

The reader sees one person and a verb for many, and stops. The fix: Ravi has a kettle.

You write has with I:

> I has a fan.

Beginners do this because has "sounds more correct". It is not. I always takes have. The fix: I
have a fan.

If a sentence has has after I, you, we or they, or have after he, she, it or one name, that
sentence is RED.

## In real life

In speech, fluent speakers often say "I've got" and "she's got", and the page above says so.
On paper, especially on anything formal, a form, a message to a landlord, a note on a wall,
"have" and "has" are the forms readers expect. Write those.

A friend reading "Ravi have a kettle" understands you. A landlord's list of what is in the
room, or a form asking what you have, is read by someone who does not know you, and the wrong
form is the first thing they notice about your English, before the kettle.
