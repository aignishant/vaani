## One-line answer

Have and has also say what a person is like and who they have, brown eyes, a cold, two brothers, and the form still follows the person at the start.

## The story

The hostel warden asks each of you to write three lines about yourself for the noticeboard, so
the others know who is in which room. Ravi writes: "I have short hair and glasses. I have a
brother in the city. I have a cold this week, sorry." You read it and know him at once: what
he looks like, who is near, why he is coughing. None of these are things he owns in a shop.
All of them take have.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **be like** — how a person looks or is.
- **glasses** — the two small lenses some people wear to see.
- **cold** — a small illness with a runny nose and a cough.
- **noticeboard** — a board on a wall where messages are pinned.
- **describe** — say what something is like.

In [part 2.1](2.1-have-and-has.md) have and has said what someone owns. The same two words
also say three other things: **what a person looks like** (brown eyes, long hair, a beard),
**who a person has** (two brothers, a small daughter, many friends), and **what is wrong with
a person today** (a cold, a headache, a fever). None of these are owned, but English uses have
for all of them.

The form rule does not change. Finger on the person at the start: one other person or a name
takes has; I, you, we, they take have.

## The rule

Three jobs, one verb:

| Job | Shape | Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| what someone looks like | person + have/has + a feature | He has short hair. |
| who someone has | person + have/has + number + family word | I have two brothers. |
| what is wrong today | person + have/has + a + illness | She has a cold. |

Three true lines about one person, the way Ravi wrote them:

> I have short hair and glasses.
> I have a brother in the city.
> I have a cold this week.

The forms were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got),
which shows have got with "I/you/we/they" and has got with "he/she/it", and says have and have
got mean the same in most situations. The word
[have](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/have_1) was opened the
same day; among its meanings is "to show a quality or feature". The family words
[brother](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/brother) and
[sister](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/sister) each carry a
sound button, opened the same day.

Now the shape for more than one. Two brothers, not two brother. The
[plural -s](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md) still applies
after a number. And the small word a goes before one illness: a cold, a headache, a fever.

The trap: in many languages you say "to me is a cold" or "my hair is short" and there is no
have. Both are fine English in their own way, but the have shape is the one that turns up on
forms and in descriptions, and it is the one a reader expects on a noticeboard.

## When it breaks

You write is where have belongs:

> I am a cold.

The reader thinks you are describing your character. The fix: I have a cold.

You forget the -s after a number:

> I have two brother.

The reader sees "two" and then one brother, and stops. The fix: I have two brothers.

You lose the form when the sentence gets longer:

> My sister have long hair and a small daughter.

Two facts, one verb, and the verb is wrong for one sister. The fix: My sister has long hair
and a small daughter. The person at the start decides, however long the sentence.

If any line has the wrong form, a missing -s after a number, or no a before an illness, that
line is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers use have for all three jobs without noticing they are the same verb. "I've got
a cold" and "she's got two kids" in a chat; "I have a cold" and "she has two children" in a
message to an office. The verb is the same; only the got comes and goes.

A friend reading "I am a cold" laughs and understands. A doctor's form, a school form, a
landlord's list does not laugh. "I have a cold" on a sick note says one thing; "I am cold"
says another, and the reader may act on the wrong one.
