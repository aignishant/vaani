## One-line answer

Copy your noticeboard lines with three forms wrong on purpose, has after I and have after a name and am where have belongs, read them as the warden, mark all three, then fix only those.

## The story

The warden pins the noticeboard lines up and walks along reading them. He stops at yours.
"I has a brother?" he says. "Who is I? Two people?" He moves on. "Ravi have a kettle. Which
Ravis?" He is not being unkind. He reads a hundred forms a week, and the wrong form is the
first thing his eye finds, before the kettle, before the brother, before you.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong version is deliberate. It rests on what beginners
commonly write, not on a mistake found in your own page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **swap** — put one where the other was.
- **warden** — the person in charge of a hostel.
- **read back** — read your own page again, slowly, as if it were someone else's, from
  [day 2](../../../day-02-long-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.3-a-form-nobody-can-use.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have have and has for owning from [part 2.1](2.1-have-and-has.md) and for what you are
like from [part 2.2](2.2-have-for-what-you-are-like.md). Now you break your lines the three
ways beginners really do: has after I, have after one name, and am or is where have belongs.
You read as the warden, whose eye goes straight to the verb. Then you fix only what he found.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) has have or has in
several of its ten sentences. A read-back that cannot find three planted wrong forms today
will not find the real one there, and the real one is what a stranger sees first.

## The rule

Under your correct lines from parts 2.1 and 2.2:

1. Copy your six lines with three deliberate breaks: put has after I in one; put have after
   one name in one; put am or is where have belongs in one.
2. Cover the correct lines.
3. Read the broken lines out loud as the warden. At every verb, stop. Finger on the person at
   the start. Ask: "One other person, or not?" Then: "Is this have, has, or the wrong verb
   altogether?"
4. Mark each wrong verb RED. There should be three.
5. Uncover. Fix only the three, on a new line, and mark it GREEN.

The model, broken on purpose:

> I has short hair. Ravi have a kettle. I am a cold this week.

Three RED marks: I has should be I have; Ravi have should be Ravi has; I am a cold should be I
have a cold.

The forms were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got).

## When it breaks

You read the broken lines back and mark them GREEN:

> I has short hair. Ravi have a kettle. I am a cold this week. — GREEN

"I has" slips past because has feels more careful, and "I am a cold" slips past because in
your first language the shape may be "I am with cold". The check itself failed. Read again,
finger on the person, asking the two questions out loud at every verb.

Another break: you fix the verb and change the person.

> We have short hair.

You made I into we so that have would be right. The person was never wrong. Fix the verb, not
the person.

If any planted break was marked GREEN, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody plants a wrong form. But everyone whose first language does not change the verb by
person writes have everywhere, or has everywhere because it looks more formal. Readers who
know you fix it in their heads. Readers who do not, a warden, an office, a landlord, see it
first and read the rest through it.

A friend forgives "Ravi have a kettle". A hostel rule sheet, a form asking what you have, or a
sick note saying "I am a cold" is read by someone acting on it, and the wrong verb can mean
the wrong action. Keep the broken lines on your practice page as proof that your read-back can
go RED.
