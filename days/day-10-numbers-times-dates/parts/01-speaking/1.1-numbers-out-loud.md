## One-line answer

Numbers from one to a hundred are built from a few pieces, and the teens, thirteen to nineteen, are told apart from the tens, thirty to ninety, by where the loud syllable falls.

## The story

You are at the clinic desk. The nurse asks your age and your phone number. You say them. She
writes and reads back: "Thirty. Nine eight one three…" You are not thirty. You said thirteen
for the last digits of your number and she wrote thirty. Every number was in your mouth. The
teens and the tens came out with the same shape, and she heard the tens.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **teens** — the numbers thirteen to nineteen.
- **tens** — the numbers twenty, thirty, forty and so on to ninety.
- **digit** — one figure, 0 to 9.
- **read back** — say a number again so the other person can check it.
- **clinic** — a small place where a doctor sees patients.

Numbers out loud are pieces. One to twelve are each their own word. Thirteen to nineteen end
in -teen. Twenty to ninety end in -ty. After that, you join a ten and a unit: twenty-one,
forty-five. A hundred is one more word.

**Stress**, from [day 7](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-loud-syllable.md),
is the extra force on one **syllable**, one piece of a word. It is the whole difference between
thirteen and thirty out loud. In thirTEEN the loud piece is the last one, and the -teen is long.
In THIRty the loud piece is the first, and the -ty is short and quiet.

## The rule

The pairs that get confused, with the loud syllable in CAPITALS:

| Teen | Ten |
| --- | --- |
| thirTEEN | THIRty |
| fourTEEN | FORty |
| fifTEEN | FIFty |
| sixTEEN | SIXty |
| sevenTEEN | SEVenty |
| eighTEEN | EIGHty |
| nineTEEN | NINEty |

Say each pair. Tap the table: teen gets the hard tap at the end; ten gets it at the start.
Make the -teen long, /tiːn/, and the -ty short, /ti/.

> thirTEEN — THIRty
> fifTEEN — FIFty

Now numbers above twenty. Say the ten, then the unit, joined:

> twenty-one, twenty-two … forty-five … ninety-nine, a hundred

Phone numbers are said digit by digit, and zero is said "oh" or "zero". Two of the same digit
together can be "double": double seven.

The trap: the th at the start of thirteen and thirty is the
[tongue-between-the-teeth sound from day 3](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/01-speaking/1.1-tongue-between-the-teeth.md).
Say it with the tongue in place, or "thirty" becomes "dirty", which the nurse will also hear.

## When it breaks

You say thirteen with the stress at the front:

> THIRteen

The listener hears the shape of a ten, and writes thirty. The fix: hit the -teen, make it long.

You say a phone number in a rush, with no gaps:

> nineeightonethreefourtwo

The listener catches the first two digits and loses the rest. The fix: say the digits in
groups of two or three, with a small pause between groups, and let the last digit of each
group finish.

If, on playback, you cannot hear which of thirteen and thirty is which, the check is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers know the teens and tens are a trap, and they often say both: "Thirteen. One
three." Or they ask: "Thirty or thirteen?" That is not a sign of weak English. It is what
careful people do with numbers, because a wrong number costs more than a wrong word.

At a desk, on the phone, at a counter, the read-back is the habit. You say the number; they
say it back; you say "yes" or fix it. A native listener does this too, and will thank you for
the "one three" after the thirteen.
