## One-line answer

A time is said as an hour word plus o'clock, or as a part past or to the hour; a date is said with the, an -th number, and of before the month.

## The story

The nurse gives you your next appointment: "Half past four on the third of March." You say
"Yes" and write down 4.30 and 3 March. At home your sister asks when, and you say "Four
thirty, three March." She understands, but she smiles. You said the numbers. You did not say
the time or the date the way people say them, and it sounded like reading a ticket.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **o'clock** — the word after the hour when there are no minutes: three o'clock.
- **past** — after the hour: ten past three.
- **to** — before the next hour: ten to four.
- **quarter** — one of four equal parts; a quarter of an hour.
- **appointment** — a fixed time to meet someone, such as a doctor.

In [part 1.1](1.1-numbers-out-loud.md) you said numbers. A time and a date are numbers with
a few extra words around them, and the extra words are what make them sound like speech. You
can say a time two ways, and both are right. The digit way, "four thirty", is what a screen
says. The clock way, "half past four", is what a person says. Learn both; use the clock way
when you speak to a person.

A date out loud has three small words the written date does not: the, -th, and of. The third
of March. The twenty-second of September.

## Why Vaani needs it

On [day 15](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed) you say
your daily routine, and every sentence has a time in it: I get up at half past six. If your
times come out as digits, the routine sounds like a timetable. A stranger follows a person
better than a timetable.

## The rule

**Times.** The hour word, then one of these:

| The clock shows | Say |
| --- | --- |
| 3.00 | three o'clock |
| 3.05 | five past three |
| 3.15 | a quarter past three |
| 3.30 | half past three |
| 3.45 | a quarter to four |
| 3.50 | ten to four |

Up to the half hour you say past the hour you are in. After the half hour you say to the next
hour. The chart was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Telling the Time](https://www.englishclub.com/vocabulary/time-telling-time.php), which gives
"half past three", "a quarter to four" and "ten to four" beside the digit forms "three thirty"
and "three fifty". The words [o'clock](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/o-clock),
[quarter](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/quarter_1) and
[half](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/half_1) each carry a sound
button, opened the same day. Half is /hɑːf/; the l is silent.

Morning or evening: add "in the morning", "in the afternoon", "in the evening", or a.m. and
p.m.

> The appointment is at half past four in the afternoon.

**Dates.** The, then the number with its -th ending, then of, then the month:

> the third of March
> the twenty-second of September

The number endings are first, second, third, then -th: fourth, fifth, sixth … twentieth,
twenty-first, twenty-second, twenty-third, twenty-fourth. Checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Ordinal Numbers](https://www.englishclub.com/vocabulary/numbers-ordinal.php) and at
[first](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/first_1),
[second](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/second_1) and
[third](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/third_1), each with a
sound button. The spoken form "the Fourteenth of March" is given at
[Writing the Date](https://www.englishclub.com/vocabulary/time-date.php).

Years are said in two halves: nineteen ninety-nine; twenty twenty-six.

The trap: the -th ending is the
[tongue-between-the-teeth sound](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/01-speaking/1.1-tongue-between-the-teeth.md)
at the end of a word. Fourth, fifth, sixth: finish them with the tongue out, or they become
"fort", "fift", "sikt".

## When it breaks

You say the time as bare digits to a person:

> Four thirty.

It is not wrong, but it is a screen talking. The listener may ask "morning or afternoon?" The
fix: "half past four in the afternoon".

You say the date as the numbers on the paper:

> Three March.

The listener hears a list. The fix: "the third of March". Three small words.

You mix past and to:

> a quarter past four

when the clock shows 3.45. The listener comes at 4.15 and you have gone. The fix: after the
half hour, count to the next hour: a quarter to four.

If your recorded time has no o'clock, past, to or half, or your recorded date has no the, -th
or of, the check is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers mix the two ways freely: "It's at four thirty — half four." Some drop the
"past": "half four" means half past four. You will hear that; you do not have to say it. On
the phone, where nothing can be seen, careful speakers say the time twice in two ways for
safety, and say the day with the date: "Tuesday the third of March".

A native listener understands "four thirty". They understand "three March". They hear the
difference between someone reading and someone speaking, and the small words are that
difference.
