## One-line answer

Record a teen with the stress at the front and a time with past where to belongs, on purpose, play them back as the nurse with a pen, write down what you heard, then record the right ones and the read-back repair.

## The story

You go back to the clinic desk with a plan. You give your phone number and, at the end, you say
"Thirteen. One three." The nurse writes 13 and does not look up. Then you say "Half past four
— four thirty." She nods. Nothing was asked twice. You did what careful speakers do: you gave
the number two ways. Now you will build that habit with the recorder as the nurse.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong versions are deliberate. They rest on what beginners
commonly do, not on a mistake found in your own recordings.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **pen** — the thing the nurse writes with; here, the listener who writes down what they
  hear.
- **two ways** — the number as a word and then digit by digit.
- **repair** — a small correction that helps someone understand, from
  [day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.3-change-one-sound.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the teens and tens from [part 1.1](1.1-numbers-out-loud.md) and the times and dates
from [part 1.2](1.2-times-and-dates-out-loud.md). Now you say them wrong on purpose and
listen as the nurse: a person with a pen who writes exactly what she hears and asks nothing.
What she writes is what you said. Then you record the right version, and the read-back repair
that careful speakers use.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), the stranger writes
down what they hear. A wrong-shaped thirteen becomes thirty on their page and there is no
nurse to ask. A learner who has played the nurse for their own recording knows which of their
numbers is at risk, and gives that one two ways.

## The rule

Three rows. Recorder on. Wrong first, then right, with a gap between.

**Row 1 — the teen with the wrong shape.**

> THIRteen — thirTEEN

**Row 2 — past where to belongs.** The clock shows 3.45.

> a quarter past three — a quarter to four

**Row 3 — the date as bare numbers.**

> three March — the third of March

Play each row. Be the nurse. Write down what you heard from the wrong version, exactly, before
you listen to the right one. Row 1: did you write 30 or 13? Row 2: which time did you write?
Row 3: did you write a date or two words?

Then the repair, the two-ways habit, in one breath, for your own phone number:

> … thirteen. One three.

And for the time:

> Half past four. Four thirty.

[thirteen](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/thirteen)
/ˌθɜːˈtiːn/ and [thirty](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/thirty)

## When it breaks

The break this part is about: as the nurse, you wrote the right thing from the wrong version.

> THIRteen → 13

If you wrote 13, you knew what you meant and your ear filled it in. That is not what a
stranger does. Play it again and listen only to where the loud piece is. Front means thirty.
If it is at the front and you still hear thirteen, your wrong version was not wrong enough.
Say it again with the whole force on THIR and a short quiet -teen.

A second break: your repair says the same thing twice.

> thirteen. thirteen.

Louder is not another way. The listener has the same shape twice. The fix: word, then digits.
"Thirteen. One three."

If the nurse wrote the same thing from the wrong and the right versions in any row, that row
is RED.

## In real life

Nobody says thirteen wrong on purpose. But the teen–ten pair is the one number mistake fluent
speakers also make in noise, on the phone, in a hurry, and they have the same fix: "Thirteen.
One three." At a bank, a clinic, a booking desk, saying the number two ways is normal, and
staff do it back to you.

A native listener will not notice a wrong stress in a word. They will notice a wrong number
when the appointment is missed, and by then the cost is a day. The two-ways habit is cheap and
it is the professional one.
