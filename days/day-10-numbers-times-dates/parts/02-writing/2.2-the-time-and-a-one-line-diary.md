## One-line answer

Write a time as hour, a dot or colon, two figures for the minutes, then a.m. or p.m., and a diary line is the date, the time and one true sentence about that day.

## The story

Your diary has a date at the top now. Under it you write "Got up late. Bus." A month later you
read it and remember nothing. Late was when? Which bus? On the next page you try again: "7.30
a.m. I got up late and missed the first bus." That one you will remember in a year, because it
has a time and a sentence, and the sentence has a verb.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **a.m.** — the hours from midnight to midday, the morning.
- **p.m.** — the hours from midday to midnight, the afternoon and evening.
- **entry** — one day's writing in a diary.
- **colon** — the mark : with two dots.
- **remember** — bring back to your mind.

In [part 2.1](2.1-writing-the-date.md) you wrote the date. A time on paper is the hour, a
dot or a colon, two figures for the minutes, and then a.m. or p.m. so the reader knows morning
from evening: 7.30 a.m. A **diary entry** is the date, a time if one matters, and at least one
full [sentence](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md),
with its capital letter and its full stop, about something that really happened.

One line is enough. The line must be a sentence, not a list of words, or it will mean nothing
to you later.

From today, every writing day can start with a diary line, dated and timed, and by

## The rule

**Writing the hour and the minutes.**

| Written | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 7.30 a.m. | half past seven in the morning |
| 12.00 p.m. | midday |
| 4.15 p.m. | a quarter past four in the afternoon |
| 10.00 p.m. | ten at night |

Hour, dot, two figures for the minutes. Always two figures: 7.05, not 7.5. Then a space and
a.m. or p.m. with their dots. A colon, 7:30, is also fine; pick one and keep it. The a.m. and
p.m. forms with lowercase letters and dots, "3 p.m.", were seen on 2026-09-22 at
[An email to confirm an appointment](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/email-confirm-appointment).
The written forms "8.00 a.m." and "10:00 p.m." beside routine sentences were seen at
[Daily Routine Vocabulary](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/vocabulary/a1-a2/daily-routine-vocabulary-a1-beginner-english-vocabulary-lesson),
opened the same day, and the digit forms against the clock words at
[Telling the Time](https://www.englishclub.com/vocabulary/time-telling-time.php).

**The diary line.** Date on its own line. Then the time, then one sentence:

> 9 October 2026
> 7.30 a.m. I got up late and missed the first bus.

The sentence is about you, is true, and has a verb. It may be small: "I had tea with my
mother." Small and true beats big and vague.

The trap: a diary line is not a note to yourself in shorthand. "Late. Bus." is what you will
write when tired. It is not a sentence, and in a month it is not a memory either.

## When it breaks

You write the time without a.m. or p.m.:

> 7.30 I got up late.

Today you know it was morning. In the diary of a month ago, 7.30 could be either, and "got up"
in the evening reads as a strange day. The fix: 7.30 a.m.

You write the minutes with one figure:

> 7.5 p.m.

The reader sees seven and a half, or five past seven, or does not know. The fix: 7.05 p.m.

You write words instead of a sentence:

> 7.30 a.m. Late. Bus.

No capital, no verb, no full stop where a sentence should be. The fix: "I got up late and
missed the bus." A sentence, with a verb, that a stranger could read.

If your line has a time with no a.m. or p.m., minutes with one figure, or a sentence with no
verb, that line is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers in a hurry write "7.30 late, missed bus" in their own diary, and they get away
with it because they will remember. You will not, yet, so write the sentence. In a message to
someone else, they write the full form: "I'll be there at 7.30 a.m." because the reader cannot
ask the writer's memory.

A friend reading "7.30" guesses morning from the rest of the message. An office, a clinic or a
train booking does not guess. "Your appointment is at 7.30" without a.m. or p.m. is the kind
of line that makes people arrive twelve hours wrong.
