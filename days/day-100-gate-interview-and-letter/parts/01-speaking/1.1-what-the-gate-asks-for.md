## One-line answer

The phase 5 speaking gate is a recorded interview role-play answering five standard questions in full
sentences. This part teaches nothing new, because a gate that teaches isn't a gate. It sets up the
recording so that when you press record, there's nothing left to decide except your answers.

## The story

A letter comes on a Thursday. You've been shortlisted, and the interview is on Monday. A panel of two
people will ask you questions for the job you really want. They'll ask *Tell us about yourself*. They'll
ask why you want this job, what you're good at, what you're not good at yet, and about a time something
went wrong. They're listening for what you've done, and for whether you can say it clearly, in whole
sentences, to strangers.

That's today. Nineteen days of this folder have taught the pieces: formal and informal, the
introduction at work, *tell me about yourself*, the standard questions, a confident voice, a complaint,
a meeting, an update, the opening and close of a talk, a chart, stress in long words, a deal, a *no*,
an office, an angry customer, rules, numbers and codes, a technical explanation, and a full talk. None
of it is repeated here, and none of it is made easier.

**This is a rehearsal, and it says so.** This day was written before your error ledger had rows in it.
Until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked, the checks below are the general ones
rather than your own recurring mistakes, and the gate is not yet a gate.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate**: a check at the end of a phase that the work has to pass before the phase is finished, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-the-gate-asks.md).
- **take**: one recording attempt, from start to stop, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/01-speaking/1.2-record-the-introduction.md).
- **transcript**: every word of a recording, written down as it was said, from
  [day 60](../../../day-60-gate-story-from-life/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-the-gate-asks-for.md).
- **standard question**, **shape (of an answer)**, **STAR**: a question that comes up in most
  interviews, the pattern an answer follows, and Situation, Task, Action, Result for *a time when*
  answers, from
  [day 84](../../../day-84-standard-interview-questions/parts/01-speaking/1.1-four-questions-four-shapes.md).
- **mock interview**: a practice interview with someone playing the interviewer, from
  [day 84](../../../day-84-standard-interview-questions/parts/01-speaking/1.2-five-answers-for-one-job.md).
- **full sentence (spoken)**: a sentence with someone doing something in it, a subject and a verb: *I
  plan my week on Sunday evening*, not *Planning, mostly*.
- **fragment**: a piece of a sentence said as if it were an answer: *Hard-working. Team player.*

The gate wording is in the plan's §7, and it's copied here without a word changed:

> Speaking: a recorded interview role-play answering five standard questions in full sentences.

Four conditions, and all four can be checked.

| Condition | What it means | How it's checked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| recorded | one take, kept, not edited together | the file exists, with a `RECORDINGS.md` row |
| an interview role-play | someone else asks, you answer, for one real job | both voices are on the recording; the job is named |
| five standard questions | five different questions from the standard set | the transcript shows five, each answered |
| in full sentences | every answer made of sentences with a subject and a verb | every sentence in your answers is marked full in the transcript |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the point of the phase. Nineteen days of formal speaking exist so that five real questions from
a stranger can be answered clearly, in whole sentences, without falling apart. Until it's recorded,
nobody knows whether they can be.

Phase 6 builds on it. An unscripted talk of four hundred words, the phase 6 gate, is five interview
answers with nobody asking the questions.

## The rule

There's no new rule today. What follows is the setup.

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to prepare for a job interview in English](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/level/improve-your-english-level/how-prepare-job-interview-english)
lists "'Why do you want this job?'" and "'What are your strengths and weaknesses?'" among the common
questions, and says "Your answers will be more persuasive if you include real examples of your work."
It also says: "It's a good idea to do a practice interview – ask a friend to ask you some questions and
practise responding in English."

**Choose one real job.** A job you'd really apply for, or the job you have and might interview for again.
Have the advert, or write down what it asks for.

**Choose five standard questions.** One from each row:

| Kind | The question | My answer's shape, in a few words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| about you | *Tell me about yourself.* | `TODO(me)` |
| why | *Why do you want this job?* | `TODO(me)` |
| strength | *What are your main strengths?* | `TODO(me)` |
| weakness | *What's one weakness, and what are you doing about it?* | `TODO(me)` |
| a time when | *Tell me about a time you …* | `TODO(me)` |

**What must be in the recording**, because these are what the phase taught:

- a formal greeting and a thank-you at the start and the end;
- every answer in full sentences, with *I* and a verb, not a list of words;
- a real example in at least three answers, with a name, a place or a number;
- the *time when* answer in STAR order;
- a steady voice that falls at the end of each answer.

**One take, straight through.** Mistakes stay in. If you stumble, say the sentence again and keep
going, the way you would in a real room.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the fragment answer**: *Strengths? Punctual. Hard-working. Good with
people.* Three fragments and no sentence. The interviewer hears a list, not a person.

The second is **the memorised answer**, read in a flat voice. It's in full sentences, but a stranger can
hear that it's recited.

The third is **the job that isn't real**, so the *why this job* answer could be for any job. Choose a
real one.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate recording. This day was written before your
own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Real interviewers usually ask a follow-up question: *Can you give me an example?* or *What did you learn
from that?* A friend playing the interviewer can ask one. It's fair, and it's what the real room does.

Under pressure, answers shrink into fragments, because a fragment feels safe. A full sentence says who
did what, and that's what the interviewer is listening for.

What a native listener notices first is whether you sound like you're talking to them or reading to
them. Notes, not a script.
