## One-line answer

Record two of your five answers, the strength and the *time when*, in fragments on purpose, as take L,
the list. Then play take L and the same two answers from your gate take to two strangers, one each. Ask
each one: *What's this person's strength, and what's the evidence?* and *Would you want to meet them
again? One to five.* The list should lose the evidence. The gate take should carry both.

## The story

Your cousin helped at interviews for a shop job. Afterwards she told you about two people:

> The first one, I asked about strengths, and he said *Honest. Reliable. Quick learner.* Then he
> stopped and smiled. I wrote *honest, reliable* and I couldn't think of anything else to write.
>
> The second one said *I learn quickly. In my last job, I learned the new billing system in two days,
> and after that I trained the others on it.* I wrote *learns fast, trained the team on billing*. I
> remembered her that evening.

Both said they were quick learners. Only one of them was believed, and it was the one who said it in a
sentence, with a story in it.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **full sentence (spoken)**, **fragment**: from [part 1.1](1.1-what-the-gate-asks-for.md).
- **F / X marking**, **point-of-each-answer test**: from [part 1.2](1.2-record-the-interview.md).
- **evidence**: the real example that shows a claim is true.
- **evidence test**: a stranger says the claim in an answer and the evidence for it, and scores from one
  to five whether they'd want to meet the speaker again.

| Take | What's in it | What a stranger should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L, list (deliberate) | the strength and *time when* answers as fragments: *Punctual. Team player.* | a claim with no evidence; a score of 1 to 3 |
| G, gate take | the same two answers in full sentences with a real example | the claim and its evidence; a score of 4 or 5 |

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate asks for full sentences because fragments throw away the part that persuades: who did what,
where and when. This test lets you hear that loss from a stranger's side before a real panel hears it.

## The rule

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take L that turns back into sentences** halfway,
because the habit is strong. That's good news for the gate, and bad for the test. Keep L short and
flat.

The second is **a stranger who's too kind** and gives everyone 5. Ask them: *If you could only meet one
of ten people again, would this be one?*

The third, in real life, is **the fragment answer to a follow-up**. *Can you give me an example?* —
*Yes, many.* That's a fragment. Give one.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate recording. This day was written before your
own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Interviewers hear the same claims all day: hard-working, reliable, team player. They can't choose
between claims. They choose between examples, and examples need sentences.

Under pressure, people drop into fragments because a sentence feels long. It isn't. *I learned the new
billing system in two days* is ten words.

A fragment can start an answer, as a headline, if a sentence follows it: *Patience. I'm patient with
customers, even when they're upset.* The fragment alone is where it goes wrong.
