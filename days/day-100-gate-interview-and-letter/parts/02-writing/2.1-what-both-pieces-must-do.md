## One-line answer

The phase 5 writing gate is two pieces: a formal email and a cover letter, both with the correct
greeting, sign-off and no contractions. This part teaches nothing new. It sets up the two pieces and
the three checks, so that when you start writing, the only thing left to decide is what you say.

## The story

The same job you'd interview for has an online application. It asks for two things: a short email to
the office to confirm you're sending your application, and a cover letter to go with your CV.

The person who opens them reads formal emails all day. Before they read a word of what you did, they
see how you greeted them, how you signed off, and whether it looks like a letter or a text message.

That's today. Nineteen days of this folder have taught the pieces: formal and informal on paper, the
formal frame, the CV, the cover letter, present perfect and past simple, the complaint, meeting notes,
the status update, formal joins, trends, the passive, the request, the decline, forms, the reply to a
complaint, written rules, instructions, a technical explanation, and an essay plan. None of it is
repeated here, and none of it is made easier.

**This is a rehearsal, and it says so.** This day was written before your error ledger had rows in it.
Until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked, the checks below are the general ones
rather than your own recurring mistakes, and the gate is not yet a gate.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate**: a check at the end of a phase that the work has to pass before the phase is finished, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-the-page-must-carry.md).
- **frame (of an email)**, **matching pair**: the greeting, reason line, paragraphs, closing line and
  sign-off, and a greeting and sign-off that belong together, from
  [day 82](../../../day-82-introducing-yourself-at-work/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-formal-frame-top-to-bottom.md).
- **cover letter**: a short formal letter or email that goes with a CV and asks the employer to consider
  you, from
  [day 84](../../../day-84-standard-interview-questions/parts/02-writing/2.1-four-paragraphs-three-jobs.md).
- **contraction**: a short form such as *I'll*, *can't*, *that's*, from
  [day 25](../../../day-25-on-the-phone/parts/02-writing/2.1-a-message-is-not-a-letter.md).
- **greeting**: the first line of a letter or email, before the message: *Dear Ms Rao,*.
- **sign-off**: the last line before your name: *Yours sincerely,*.

The gate wording is in the plan's §7, and it's copied here without a word changed:

> Writing: a formal email and a cover letter, both with the correct greeting, sign-off and no
> contractions.

Five conditions, and all five can be checked.

| Condition | What it means | How it's checked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a formal email | a real email to a real reader, in the formal frame | it exists in `lab/writing/` and has a real reader |
| a cover letter | a real cover letter for one real job | it exists in `lab/writing/` and names the job |
| the correct greeting | *Dear* + title and surname, or *Dear Sir or Madam*, with a comma | the greeting pass, one piece at a time |
| the correct sign-off | *Yours sincerely,* after a name; *Yours faithfully,* after *Dear Sir or Madam* | the sign-off pass, checked against the greeting |
| no contractions | *I am*, *I have*, *do not*, never *I'm*, *I've*, *don't* | the apostrophe pass: every ' circled and checked |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the point of the phase. Nineteen days of formal writing exist so that two real pieces can reach
a real reader looking like they came from someone who knows the form. Until they're written and sent,
nobody knows whether they can.

Phase 6 builds on it. Every essay there is formal writing without the frame, and the no-contractions
habit carries straight into it.

## The rule

There's no new rule today. What follows is the setup.

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Unit 4: Starting and finishing emails](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/english-emails/unit-4-starting-finishing-emails)
gives *Dear Mr Piper,* and *Dear Sir or Madam,*, *Yours sincerely,* and *Yours faithfully,*, and says "We
normally write a comma after the opening phrase" and "We normally write a comma after the closing
phrase".
[How to write a cover letter](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/covering-letter)
says: "It's a short letter, usually 3 to 5 paragraphs long." It also says: "If you know the name of the
person you're writing to, you should end the letter with 'Yours sincerely'." and "If you've addressed
the letter 'Dear Sir or Madam', you should end the letter with 'Yours faithfully'." And: "You can write
it as an email if you're applying online".

**Decide the greeting before you write.** Do you know the reader's name? Then *Dear* + title + surname,
and the sign-off is *Yours sincerely*. If not, *Dear Sir or Madam*, and the sign-off is *Yours
faithfully*. Write both lines first, top and bottom, and fill the middle after.

**The apostrophe rule for this gate.** An apostrophe that shows a missing letter is a contraction:
*I'm*, *don't*, *it's*. It fails the gate. An apostrophe that shows belonging is not a contraction:
*the company's website*. It's fine. Every apostrophe gets circled and checked.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the mismatched pair**: *Dear Sir or Madam* at the top and *Yours
sincerely* at the bottom. Each line is correct alone. Together, they fail.

The second is **the contraction that feels formal**: *I'd be grateful*, *I'm writing to apply*. They're
polite, and they're contractions.

The third is **the greeting with a first name only** to a stranger: *Dear Priya,*. For the gate, a
stranger gets a title and a surname.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate attempt. This day was written before your own
error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

You'll see *Kind regards* or *Best regards* at the end of many work emails, and they aren't wrong in
the world. The gate asks for the formal pair on purpose, because it's the one that's never wrong for a
stranger.

Under pressure, filling in an online form at the last minute, the greeting and sign-off are typed
fastest and checked least. Write them first, and check them last.

A formal reader sees the greeting before anything else, and the sign-off last. They're the first and
last impression of the piece.
