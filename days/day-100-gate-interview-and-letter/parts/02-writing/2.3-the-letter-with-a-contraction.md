## One-line answer

Make a copy of your cover letter that breaks the gate on purpose: three contractions, a mismatched
greeting and sign-off, and a missing comma. Give that copy and your fair copy to two readers, each with
the gate wording and a pen. Ask each one to mark every place the letter fails the gate. The broken copy
should come back marked. Your fair copy should come back clean.

## The story

Your uncle's office was hiring a clerk. He had forty cover letters on his desk and one afternoon to
choose five. He told you how he started:

> First I look at the top and the bottom. *Hi Sir* goes on the left pile. *Dear Sir or Madam* at the
> top and *Yours sincerely* at the bottom goes on the left pile too, which is unfair, maybe. Then I look
> for *I'm* and *don't*. A clerk has to write to our customers.

He got down to twelve before reading a single word about anyone's experience.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

You've read your own fair copy several times. Your eyes now see what you meant, not what's there. A
reader with the gate wording in their hand sees only what's there, like the person who opens your
application.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to write a cover letter](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/covering-letter):
"If you've addressed the letter 'Dear Sir or Madam', you should end the letter with 'Yours faithfully'."
[Unit 4: Starting and finishing emails](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/english-emails/unit-4-starting-finishing-emails):
"We normally write a comma after the opening phrase".

**Step one: make copy B.** Copy your cover letter and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Change three phrases to contractions: *I am* → *I'm*, *I would* → *I'd*, *do not* → *don't*.
2. Make the pair mismatch: if your greeting has a name, change it to *Dear Sir or Madam* and keep
   *Yours sincerely*. If it's *Sir or Madam*, change the sign-off to *Yours sincerely*.
3. Delete the comma after the greeting.

**Step two: the gate-check test.** Give each reader the gate wording on a slip:

> a formal email and a cover letter, both with the correct greeting, sign-off and no contractions

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who marks the possessive**: *the company's* is
circled as a contraction. It isn't. Tell them the apostrophe rule before they start.

The second is **a reader who marks style**, like a long sentence or a word they'd change. That's
helpful, but it isn't the gate. Ask them to mark only the three things in the gate wording.

The third, in real life, is **the email's subject line forgotten**. It isn't part of the gate wording,
but a real reader sees it first. Check it too.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate attempt. This day was written before your own
error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A formal reader forgives a lot in the middle of a letter. The top and the bottom they check first,
often before anything else. That's why the gate checks exactly those.

Under pressure, contractions come back in the sentences you care most about, because that's where you
write most naturally. Check your best sentence twice.

When a letter passes this test, the reader can get on to what matters: whether you're right for the
job.
