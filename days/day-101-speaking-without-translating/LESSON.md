## §1 Where we are

At a tea break, someone asks your friend about her weekend. She builds the whole answer in her first
language first, and every missing word is a long silence. The next week she starts with *The thing
is*, says one idea per sentence, and calls the ring ceremony *a big function*. Nobody waits. The
speaking folder is about fluency without translating: start from a chunk you own, one idea per
sentence, and go around a missing word instead of stopping. You draw topics from a jar and talk without
stopping, then build sentences in your first language on purpose and let listeners count the silences.

Your brother's essay about uniforms begins with a dictionary definition and ends *In this essay I will
discuss uniforms.* The teacher still doesn't know what he thinks. The writing folder is about the essay
introduction: a first line from real life, a sentence or two of context, and the thesis with its map as
the last sentence. You write a real introduction a reader can predict the essay from, then write one
that never answers on purpose.

Choose either folder first. Each stands on its own. Follow the parts in that folder's order.

## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** In [part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-sentence-built-in-your-first-language.md),
listeners count the long silences in a word-by-word take and an English-first take, and a third says
which was easier to listen to. RED is when the English-first take has as many silences, or is found
harder. GREEN is when it has fewer and is found easier.

**Writing:** In [part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-the-introduction-that-never-answered.md), two readers
underline the sentence that tells them what the writer thinks. RED is when your real introduction's
reader underlines something other than the thesis, writes *none*, or gets your answer wrong. GREEN is
when they underline the thesis and say your answer, and the copy's reader writes *none*.

Only record what you actually heard or saw. A deliberate exercise is labelled deliberate.

## §7 Traps

- The whole sentence built in your first language first.
- A long silence in the middle of a sentence.
- A first-language word dropped in.
- Stopping to find the perfect word.
- First-language word order in an English sentence.
- Reading the topic slip and then planning.
- A dictionary definition as a first line.
- *Since ancient times* and *In today's modern world*.
- *In this essay I will discuss …* in place of a thesis.
- A thesis nobody could disagree with.
- The thesis in the middle of the introduction.
- A map in a different order from the paragraphs.

## §9 Say it out loud

After your practice, tell a friend what you did. Use this only when it is true: "I learned to stop
building sentences in my first language before I speak. I start with a chunk I already own, say one
idea per sentence, and go around a word I don't know instead of stopping. I drew topics from a jar and
talked about each one without stopping, and counted my silences. Then I translated word by word on
purpose, and listeners heard more silences and found it harder to follow. In writing, I wrote an essay
introduction that starts from real life, gives a line of context, and ends with my answer and three
reasons. A reader could predict my essay from it. Then I wrote one that only announced the topic, and a
reader couldn't find what I thought." If you did only one folder, say only that part.
