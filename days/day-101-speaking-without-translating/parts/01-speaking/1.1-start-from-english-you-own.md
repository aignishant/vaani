## One-line answer

When you build a sentence in your first language and then change it into English word by word, you
stop in the middle of sentences and the word order comes out wrong. Instead, **start from English you
already own**, a marker or a chunk like *The thing is*, and say **one short idea per sentence**. When a
word is missing, **go around it** with a simpler word and keep going. You can hear the difference in
the silences.

## The story

At a tea break, a colleague asks your friend what she did at the weekend. You can see her thinking: the
whole answer is forming in her first language first. Then she starts:

> On Sunday, my … (long pause) … my husband's … elder brother's … the (pause) … engagement, no, the
> ring … function was there, so we … went.

Every pause is her looking for the English for a word she's already chosen in the other language. By
the end, the colleague has stopped waiting and is looking at his phone.

The next Monday, someone asks again. This time she starts with a chunk she owns:

> The thing is, it was a busy weekend. My brother-in-law got engaged. There was a big function on
> Sunday. We were there all day.

Four short sentences. No long silences. She didn't know the word for *ring ceremony*, so she said *a big
function*, and it was enough.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **marker**, **long turn**: a small word or phrase that tells the listener where you are, like *well*,
  *so*, *the thing is*, and a turn of several sentences where the listener waits, from
  [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/01-speaking/1.1-well-so-the-thing-is.md).
- **going around a word**: describing a thing when you don't know its name: *It's a kind of …*, *You
  use it to …*, from
  [day 75](../../../day-75-thing-without-name/parts/01-speaking/1.1-kind-of-used-for-looks-like.md).
- **filler**: a sound like *um* or *er* that fills a silence and means nothing, from
  [day 57](../../../day-57-pausing-right-place/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-lengths-of-silence.md).
- **fluency**: speaking in a way that keeps going, so the listener doesn't have to wait for you.
- **translating in your head**: building the sentence in your first language first, then changing it
  into English word by word.
- **chunk you own**: a group of English words you can say without thinking, like *The thing is*, *What
  happened was*, *I'm not sure, but*.
- **mid-sentence silence**: a long gap inside a sentence, while you look for a word.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Fluency](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/fluency) says: "Be very
clear about the message you want to communicate. That is your goal." And: "Don't worry about making
mistakes. The important thing is to communicate what you want to say." And: "Use little expressions like
'Er' or 'Erm' to fill the gaps while you are thinking about what to say."
[When you don't know an exact word](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/when-you-dont-know-exact-word)
says: "If you can't remember the word 'surgeon', you could say a more general word like 'doctor'." And:
"If you don't know the word 'bungalow', you could say 'It's a kind of house' or 'It's a sort of house'."

**Three habits.**

| Habit | What you do | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start from a chunk | begin with English you own, not with the first word of a translated sentence | *The thing is, …* / *What happened was, …* |
| One idea, one sentence | say each idea as its own short sentence | *My brother-in-law got engaged. There was a big function.* |
| Go around, don't stop | a general word, *a kind of*, or a short description | *a big function* for a word you don't have |

**Think in the message, not the sentence.** Before you speak, know *what* you want to say, the message,
in a few words. Don't build the whole sentence. Start with a chunk, and let the sentence come in
English.

**Where the silence goes.** A pause between sentences is fine. A long silence in the middle of a
sentence is the sound of translating. If you need time, put it before the sentence: *So … the thing is,
…* A short *erm* is better than a long silence.

**Name the trap.** Languages don't all put their words in the same order. If your first language puts
the verb at the end, a sentence changed word by word may come out as *Yesterday I to the market went*.
That line is an illustration of the pattern, not a sentence we have heard from you. The
listener has to hold every word until the verb arrives. Short sentences starting with *I* and a verb
avoid it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the long sentence built in advance**, as in the story. It's grammatical
in your head and falls apart in English, with silences at every word you don't have.

The second is **the first-language word dropped in** when the English one won't come. Sometimes the
listener knows it. Often they don't, and now they're guessing.

The third is **stopping to find the perfect word**. The listener would have understood the simple one.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers of a second language still don't know every word. What they have is a way to keep
going without it: a general word, *a kind of*, or a different sentence.

Under pressure, speaking to someone senior or in a meeting, the urge to build the perfect sentence
first is strongest. Start with a chunk anyway. The perfect sentence usually arrives too late to use.

A listener forgives a simple word much more easily than a long silence. They'll remember what you
meant, not which word you used.
