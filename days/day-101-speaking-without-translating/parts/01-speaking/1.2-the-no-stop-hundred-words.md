## One-line answer

Write six everyday topics on slips of paper and draw one without looking. Start recording at once, and
talk about it without stopping until you've said about a hundred words. Then write the transcript and
count three things: the words, the mid-sentence silences, and the places you went around a word. Draw a
second topic and do it again, trying to lower the silences.

## The story

Your cousin was preparing for a speaking exam. Her teacher gave her a game: a jar of paper slips,
each with an everyday topic. *Your street. Your favourite food. A bad day. A festival.* Draw one, and
talk. No planning, no stopping.

The first time, she drew *your favourite food*. She said forty words and stopped three times in the
middle of a sentence, looking for *fenugreek*. The fourth time, she drew *a festival*. When she didn't
know *rangoli*, she said *the coloured patterns on the floor at the door*, and kept going. She reached a
hundred words with one long silence.

She said the jar was the first thing that made her stop translating, because there was no time to.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **fluency**, **chunk you own**, **mid-sentence silence**, **translating in your head**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-start-from-english-you-own.md).
- **going around a word**: describing a thing when you don't know its name, from
  [day 75](../../../day-75-thing-without-name/parts/01-speaking/1.1-kind-of-used-for-looks-like.md).
- **topic slip**: a slip of paper with one everyday topic on it, drawn without looking.
- **no-stop talk**: talking about a drawn topic without planning and without stopping, until you've said
  about a hundred words.
- **the three counts**: the number of words, the number of mid-sentence silences, and the number of
  times you went around a word.

Drawing the topic takes away the chance to prepare sentences. That's the point. It makes you start from
English you already have.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is a small version of the phase 6 gate: a talk on a topic drawn at random. A hundred words is a
quarter of what the gate asks. The three counts give you a way to hear yourself getting more fluent
across the phase.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **reading the slip and then planning**. Start talking as soon as you've
read it, even if the first sentence is *Well, my street is quite ordinary.*

The second is **counting fillers as silences**. A short *erm* is not a mid-sentence silence. A gap where
you stop and search is.

The third is **choosing easy topics on purpose**. Mix in one or two you'd find harder, like *a bad
day* or *something you'd change*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers do the no-stop talk all day. It's called conversation. Someone asks *How was the
wedding?*, and there's no slip and no planning, just a chunk and the next sentence.

Under pressure, the first sentence is the hardest. Have three chunks ready that work for any topic, and
the second sentence comes more easily.

Nobody listening counts your words. They notice the silences. Fewer silences, even with simpler words,
sound more fluent than perfect words with gaps between them.
