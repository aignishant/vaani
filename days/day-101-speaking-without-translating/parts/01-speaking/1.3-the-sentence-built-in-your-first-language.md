## One-line answer

Record the same topic twice. In take W, translate on purpose: before each sentence, say it silently in
your first language, then change it into English word by word, keeping the first-language word order and
dropping in a first-language word where the English won't come. In take E, start from chunks you own,
with short sentences, and go around missing words. Play each to a different listener. Ask them to count
the long silences, and to say *Which speaker was easier to listen to?* W should have more silences and
be harder. E should be easier.

## The story

Your uncle was on the phone to a hotel abroad, booking a room for a family trip. He built every sentence
carefully in his head first. There were long gaps, and a word from his own language slipped in when the
English wouldn't come. After the third gap, the receptionist said *Sir? Are you still there?*

His daughter took the phone:

> Hi. We need two rooms, for four people. From the tenth to the twelfth of May. Is that possible?

Short sentences, no gaps. The booking took a moment.

Her English wasn't better than his. She'd just stopped trying to say his sentences in English, and said
her own.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **translating in your head**, **chunk you own**, **mid-sentence silence**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-start-from-english-you-own.md).
- **topic slip**, **no-stop talk**, **the three counts**: from
  [part 1.2](1.2-the-no-stop-hundred-words.md).
- **language switch**: dropping a word from your first language into an English sentence.
- **silence-count test**: a listener counts the long silences in a recording, and says which of two
  speakers was easier to listen to.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| W, word by word (deliberate) | each sentence built in your first language, then changed word by word; first-language word order; a first-language word where English won't come | more silences; harder to listen to |
| E, English first | chunks you own; one idea per sentence; going around missing words | fewer silences; easier to listen to |

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't hear yourself translate, because to you it feels like thinking. A listener hears it as
silence and odd word order. This is the only way to find out which one you're doing when you're not
paying attention.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Communication strategies in second-language acquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_strategies_in_second-language_acquisition)
describes one strategy this way: "Learners may insert a word from their first language into a sentence,
and hope that their interlocutor will understand." It describes circumlocution as "learners using
different words or phrases to express their intended meaning", with the example of *my father's father*
for *grandfather*.
[Fluency](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/fluency): "Be very clear
about the message you want to communicate. That is your goal."

**Step one: take W.** Write *deliberate* in your notebook. Draw a topic slip. For each sentence: say it
silently in your first language first, then change it into English word by word, in the same order. If an
English word won't come, use the first-language word. Record about six sentences.

**Step two: take E.** Same topic. Start with a chunk, one idea per sentence, and go around missing
words. About six sentences.

**Step three: the silence-count test.** Play W to one listener and E to another. Each counts the long
silences as they listen, making a mark for each. Then play both takes to a third listener and ask which
speaker was easier to listen to, and why.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take W that isn't really word by word**. It's hard
to do badly on purpose. Say each first-language sentence silently in full before you start the English.

The second is **a listener who speaks your first language** and understands the switched words. Use
someone who doesn't, if you can.

The third, in real life, is **translating only when nervous**. You may be fluent with friends and
translate in an interview. Try one no-stop talk in a formal voice, to hear which one you do.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

The aim isn't never to translate. It's to have a way out when a sentence gets stuck: shorter, simpler,
and starting from English.

Under pressure, the first sentence is the one most often translated. A chunk you own gets you past it.

A listener doesn't know what language you're thinking in. They only know whether they're waiting. Fewer
waits is what sounds fluent.
