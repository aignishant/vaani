## One-line answer

An essay introduction is one paragraph that moves from the reader's world to your answer. It opens with
a **topic sentence** that puts the question somewhere real. One or two sentences of **context** say why
it matters or what people disagree about. It ends with the **thesis**, your answer in one sentence, often
with the **map** of your three points in it. The last sentence is the one the reader remembers.

## The story

Your younger brother's teacher gave the class an essay: *Should students wear school uniforms?* He
showed you his introduction:

> The dictionary says a uniform is "a set of clothes worn by all members of a group." Since ancient
> times, people have worn clothes. Clothes are very important in our lives. In this essay I will
> discuss uniforms.

Four sentences, and the teacher still didn't know what he thought.

His friend's began:

> Every morning in our building, you can tell which school each child goes to by the colour of their
> shirt. Some parents say uniforms save money and arguments; others say children should be allowed to
> choose. I believe school uniforms should stay, because they save families money, stop competition
> over clothes, and make mornings simpler.

By the last line, the teacher knew the answer and the three reasons that were coming.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **essay**, **thesis**, **essay plan**: a piece of writing of several paragraphs that answers one
  question, your answer in one sentence, and the notes made before writing, from
  [day 99](../../../day-99-full-talk-structure/parts/02-writing/2.1-question-answer-three-points.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, saying the topic and the point, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **introduction**: the first paragraph of an essay.
- **context**: the one or two sentences that say why the question matters, or what people disagree
  about.
- **map (of an essay)**: the three points of the essay named briefly, usually inside the thesis.
- **announcement**: a sentence that says what the essay will do instead of what it thinks: *In this
  essay I will discuss …*

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Topic sentence | puts the question in the reader's world | *Every morning in our building, you can tell which school each child goes to.* |
| 2. Context | says why it matters or where people disagree | *Some parents say …; others say …* |
| 3. Thesis + map | gives your answer and the three reasons | *I believe …, because …, … and ….* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Thesis Statements](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/thesis-statements/), from a university
writing centre, says a thesis is "usually a single sentence near the beginning of your paper (most
often, at the end of the first paragraph)", that it "is a road map for the paper", that a good thesis
"Directly answers the question asked of you" and "Makes a claim that others might dispute", and that
"If your thesis simply states facts that no one would, or even could, disagree with, it's possible that
you are simply providing a summary, rather than making an argument."
[A for and against essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/against-essay)
gives the shape "paragraph 1 – introduction", and its model opens: "Reality shows are a very popular
form of entertainment on TV nowadays."

**The shape: wide at the top, narrow at the bottom.**

```text
   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  topic sentence: the question in real life   │   wide
   └──┬────────────────────────────────────────┬──┘
      │  context: why it matters / who disagrees│
      └──┬──────────────────────────────────┬──┘
         │  THESIS: my answer, because A, B, C│      narrow: the last sentence
         └──────────────────────────────────┘
```

**The topic sentence.** Something real the reader can picture or agree with. Not a dictionary
definition. Not *Since ancient times*. Not a question the essay won't answer.

**The context.** One or two sentences. If the essay argues one side, name the other side here fairly.

**The thesis, last.** One sentence that answers the question and could be disagreed with. Add the map
if you can: *because A, B and C*, in the order the paragraphs will come.

**No announcement.** *In this essay I will discuss …* tells the reader what you'll do. The thesis tells
them what you think.

**Name the trap.** A definition or *since ancient times* feels like a safe, formal start. It fills a
line and says nothing the reader didn't know.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the introduction with no thesis**, as in the story. It ends on an
announcement, and the reader still doesn't know the answer.

The second is **the thesis first and the context after**. The answer is there, but the paragraph then
drifts into background, and the last thing the reader sees isn't your answer.

The third is **the map in a different order** from the paragraphs. The reader expects *cost* second and
gets it third.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often write the introduction last, after the body, when they know exactly what the
essay argues. The thesis from the plan goes in, and the opening line is written to lead to it.

Under pressure, in an exam, a short introduction with a clear thesis is better than a long one with a
vague one. Three sentences can be enough.

A marker reading many essays looks for the thesis first. If they find it in the last line of the first
paragraph, they know what to check the rest of the essay against.
