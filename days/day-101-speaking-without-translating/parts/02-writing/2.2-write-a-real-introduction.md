## One-line answer

Write one real essay introduction of three to five sentences: a topic sentence from real life, one or
two sentences of context, and the thesis with its map as the last sentence. Then give only the
introduction to a reader and ask them to predict the essay: its answer, and its three body paragraphs
in order.

## The story

Your friend was applying for a college course. The form asked for a short essay: *What is one change
that would improve your town?* She'd already planned it: *more buses in the evening*, with three reasons:
safety, jobs and pollution.

She wrote the introduction three times. The first began with a definition of *public transport*. The
second began *Buses are important.* The third began with the bus stop outside her house at nine at
night, empty for an hour. It ended with her thesis and the three reasons in order.

She gave only that paragraph to her mother and asked *What's the rest going to say?* Her mother named all
three reasons, in order. *Then I'd better write them,* she said.

If you planned an essay on day 99, this is its first paragraph. If you didn't, write a quick plan first:
the question, the thesis and three points. An introduction written without a plan has nothing to lead
to.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Thesis Statements](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/thesis-statements/): a thesis "tells
the reader what to expect from the rest of the paper" and "Directly answers the question asked of you."
[A for and against essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/against-essay):
"Start each paragraph with a word or phrase so the reader knows what to expect".

**Step one: the plan, or a quick one.** Write down the question, your thesis and your three points in
order.

**Step two: three drafts of the first sentence.** Write three different topic sentences for the same
essay. One from something you've seen, one from something people say, and one from a fact you know is
true. Choose the one a reader can most easily picture.

**Step three: write the introduction.** Three to five sentences: the chosen topic sentence, one or two
of context, and the thesis with the map, last. No announcement. No contractions, because this is formal
writing.

**Step four: the predict-from-the-introduction test.** Give only the introduction to one reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **three topic sentences that are all definitions**. A definition is the
easiest first line to write and the least interesting to read.

The second is **context that argues**, so the reasons appear in the context and again in the thesis. The
context sets up the question. The thesis answers it.

The third is **a reader who reads the question first** and guesses from that. Give them the
introduction only.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers try more than one first sentence, just as you did in step two. The first one that comes
is rarely the best.

Under pressure, the context is what grows. Keep it to one or two sentences. The reader wants to reach
the thesis.

A reader who can predict your essay from its first paragraph reads the rest to check your reasons, not
to find out what you think. That's the reader you want.
