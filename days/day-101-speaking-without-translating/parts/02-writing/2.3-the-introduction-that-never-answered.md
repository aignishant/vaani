## One-line answer

Make a copy of your introduction that never answers, on purpose. It opens with a dictionary definition
or *since ancient times*, has a sentence of general truth, and ends with an announcement, *In this essay
I will discuss …*, instead of a thesis. Give that copy and your real introduction to two readers. Ask
each one: *What does the writer think? Underline the sentence that told you.* The copy's reader should
find nothing to underline. Your real introduction's reader should underline the thesis.

## The story

Your aunt teaches English at a secondary school. She keeps a sheet of first lines she's seen too often:

> The dictionary defines … as …
> Since the beginning of time, …
> In today's modern world, …
> In this essay I will discuss the advantages and disadvantages of …

> When I read one of those, she says, I start reading faster, because I know the answer won't be in
> this paragraph. Sometimes it isn't in the essay at all.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **introduction**, **context**, **announcement**: from [part 2.1](2.1-general-to-your-answer.md).
- **predict-from-the-introduction test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-introduction.md).
- **general truth**: a sentence nobody would disagree with, such as *Education is important.*
- **find-the-answer test**: a reader underlines the sentence that tells them what the writer thinks, or says
  there isn't one.

| N, no answer (deliberate) | a definition or *since ancient times*; a general truth; an announcement last | nothing to underline |

## Why Vaani needs it

An introduction with no answer can look formal and complete to the writer, because every sentence is
correct. Only a reader looking for your answer can tell you it isn't there.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Thesis Statements](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/thesis-statements/): "If your thesis
simply states facts that no one would, or even could, disagree with, it's possible that you are simply
providing a summary, rather than making an argument." And: "Thesis statements that are too vague often
do not have a strong argument."

**Step one: make copy N.** Copy your introduction and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Replace the topic sentence with a dictionary definition, or begin with *Since ancient times*.
2. Replace the context with one general truth.
3. Replace the thesis with *In this essay I will discuss the advantages and disadvantages of …*.

**Step two: the find-the-answer test.** Give N to one reader and R to another. Each reads once and underlines
the sentence that tells them what the writer thinks. If there isn't one, they write *none*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an N that still has an opinion hidden in it**: *In
this essay I will discuss why uniforms are good.* That's half a thesis. For the test, keep N neutral.

The second is **an R whose thesis is a general truth**: *Uniforms have advantages and disadvantages.*
Nobody would disagree, so there's nothing to argue. The reader writes *none*, and that's a real red.

The third, in real life, is **the announcement added to a real thesis**: *In this essay I will argue
that uniforms should stay.* It isn't wrong, but the first seven words do nothing. Start at *Uniforms*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Openings like *since ancient times* are what writers reach for when they don't know yet what they
think. If you find one in your draft, go back to the plan.

Under pressure, in an exam, a general-truth thesis feels safe because it can't be wrong. That's the
problem. It can't be argued either.

A reader of an essay is waiting for one sentence: your answer. The sooner and clearer it comes, the more
they trust what follows.
