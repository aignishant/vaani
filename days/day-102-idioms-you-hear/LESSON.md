## §1 Where we are

Your neighbour's son learned idioms from a list. On the stairs he wished your sick grandfather wouldn't
*kick the bucket*, and meant it kindly. In the lift, he said his exam was *a piece of cake*, and it was
perfect. One idiom he'd read; the other he'd heard. The speaking folder is about idioms you actually
hear: collect them from real people, check each one's exact words and label, and use it only in the
right room. You keep an idiom page, then break the checks on purpose and let listeners tell you what
sounded strange.

Your friend's practice essay came back with one paragraph marked in red and a question in the margin:
*Which reason is this paragraph proving?* The writing folder is about the body paragraph: a point from
your map, an explanation, a specific example, and a link back to your answer. You write your first body
paragraph, then write one that wanders on purpose and let a reader cross out what doesn't belong.

Choose either folder first. Each stands on its own. Follow the parts in that folder's order.

## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** In [part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-idiom-in-the-wrong-room.md), two listeners each
hear one take and say what you meant and what sounded strange. RED is when your plain take's listener
gets your meaning wrong or names something strange. GREEN is when they get your meaning and name
nothing, and the broken take's listener names at least one idiom.

**Writing:** In [part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-the-paragraph-that-wandered.md), two readers cross out
sentences that don't belong and say the paragraph's point. RED is when your real paragraph's reader
crosses anything out or says the wrong point. GREEN is when they cross out nothing and say your point,
and the wandering copy's reader crosses out at least one sentence.

Only record what you actually heard or saw. A deliberate exercise is labelled deliberate.

## §7 Traps

- An idiom learned from a list and never heard.
- The right idiom in the wrong room.
- A humorous idiom about something serious.
- One word changed inside an idiom.
- An idiom translated from your first language.
- Several idioms stacked in one answer.
- A pause before every idiom.
- A body paragraph that starts on one reason and ends on another.
- An example that's a general truth.
- A missing link sentence.
- A link sentence that copies the thesis word for word.
