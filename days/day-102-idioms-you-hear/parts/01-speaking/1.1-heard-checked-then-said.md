## One-line answer

An **idiom** is a group of words whose meaning you can't work out from the words: *a piece of cake*
means *easy*, not cake. Say an idiom only when it has passed three checks. **Heard:** a real person
around you has said it. **Checked:** you've opened a dictionary page and know its exact words. **Right
room:** you know its label, such as *informal* or *humorous*, and it fits the person in front of you.
An idiom that fails any check is one to leave alone, for now.

## The story

Your neighbour's son has just moved into the flat upstairs. He's learning English from a list of
"useful idioms" he found. On the stairs, you tell him your grandfather is in hospital. He wants to be
friendly and use his new English. He says:

> Oh no. I hope he doesn't kick the bucket.

He meant *I hope he's all right.* You heard a joke about dying. He didn't know that the phrase is
**humorous**, and that a joke was the last thing you wanted.

A week later, in the lift, someone asks him how his exam went. He's heard his office friends say this
one many times, and he says it with a smile:

> It was a piece of cake.

The same boy, the same list. The first idiom he'd only read. The second he'd heard people use.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **chunk you own**: a group of English words you can say without thinking, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/01-speaking/1.1-start-from-english-you-own.md).
- **register**: how formal or informal your words are for the person and the place, from
  [day 81](../../../day-81-formal-and-informal/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-request-ladder.md).
- **idiom**: a group of words with a meaning you can't work out from the single words. *Under the
  weather* means *a bit ill*.
- **label (in a dictionary)**: a small word in brackets beside a meaning, such as *(idiomatic)*,
  *(informal)*, *(humorous)*. It tells you where the phrase belongs.
- **literal meaning**: what the words would mean one by one. The literal meaning of *break the ice* is
  breaking frozen water.

An idiom is a chunk. You don't build it from its words; you take it whole. That's why a wrong word
inside it, or the wrong moment for it, is so easy for a listener to hear.

The phase 6 gate includes a recorded conversation. Real conversations are full of idioms, and you'll

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[English-language idioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-language_idioms) defines an idiom as
"a common word or phrase with a figurative, non-literal meaning that is understood culturally and
differs from what its composite words' denotations would suggest". The dictionary entries give the
meaning and the label of each one:

| Idiom | Meaning on the page | Label on the page | Checked at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *a piece of cake* | "A job, task or other activity that is pleasant or, by extension, easy or simple." | idiomatic | [piece of cake](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/piece_of_cake) |
| *under the weather* | "ill or gloomy, especially from a cold or flu" | — | [under the weather](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/under_the_weather) |
| *call it a day* | "To cease an activity." | idiomatic | [call it a day](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/call_it_a_day) |
| *break the ice* | "To start to get to know people to avoid social awkwardness and formality." | idiomatic | [break the ice](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/break_the_ice) |
| *kick the bucket* | "To die" | idiomatic, euphemistic, colloquial, humorous | [kick the bucket](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/kick_the_bucket) |

**Check one: heard.** You've heard a real person say it, in a real conversation, a call, a video or a
show. You heard *where* it went in the sentence and *how* they said it. A list tells you none of that.

**Check two: checked.** Open a dictionary page and read the exact words. Idioms don't bend. It's *a
piece of cake*, not *a piece of the cake*. It's *under the weather*, not *under the weathers*. One
changed word and the listener hears a mistake instead of an idiom.

**Check three: the right room.** Read the label. *Humorous* means it makes a thing lighter; never use
it for something serious. *Colloquial* or *informal* means friends and family, not an interview. If the
page has no label, listen to who you heard it from.

**Where an idiom goes.** Most idioms slot in where a plain word would go:

> I'm a bit under the weather today.
> Let's call it a day.
> The form was a piece of cake.

Say it at your normal speed, as one chunk. A pause before an idiom tells the listener you're reading
it from a list in your head.

**Name the trap.** Idiom lists are full of phrases the list-maker found colourful. Some of them you'll
hear every week; others you may never hear at all. The list can't tell you which. Your ears can.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the right idiom in the wrong room**, as in the story: *kick the
bucket* about a sick relative. The listener hears a joke at the worst moment.

The second is **the idiom with one word changed**:

> The test was a piece of the cake.

The listener understands, but they hear a learner who read the phrase rather than heard it.

The third is **an idiom translated from your first language**. It may be a lovely phrase at home. In
English, the listener hears only its literal meaning, and waits for it to make sense.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use idioms without noticing, and they don't need you to use any. Plain English is never
wrong. An idiom you've heard and checked is a bonus, not a requirement.

Under pressure, you'll reach for the idiom you learned most recently. That's usually the one you've
checked least. Reach for plain words instead.

A native listener forgives a plain word at once. A wrong idiom makes them stop and work out what you
meant, and sometimes they laugh. If they do, ask what you said. That's how you learn the idiom properly.
