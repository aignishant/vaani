## One-line answer

Start a page in your notebook for idioms you **hear**, not idioms you read. Write down five that real
people say around you, each with who said it and where. Check each one on a dictionary page. Keep the
ones that pass all three checks, and record each in two sentences about your own life: one where it
fits and one where you'd say it plainly instead.

## The story

Your neighbour's son kept his idiom list, but he changed how he used it. He drew a line down a page. On
the left, he wrote idioms he'd actually heard in the building: the security guard saying *Let's call it
a day* at the end of a long shift, a woman on the stairs saying her daughter was *under the weather*.
On the right, he wrote idioms he'd only read.

After a month, the left side had seven idioms, and he could say every one without thinking. The right
side had forty, and he'd used none of them well.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **idiom**, **label (in a dictionary)**, **literal meaning**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-heard-checked-then-said.md).
- **idiom page**: a page in your notebook where you write only idioms you've heard a real person use,
  with who said it and where.
- **plain version**: the same meaning said without the idiom. The plain version of *I'm under the
  weather* is *I'm a bit ill*.

| Column | What you write |
| --- | --- |
| Idiom | the exact words, copied from the dictionary page |
| Heard from | who said it: a colleague, a neighbour, a person in a show |
| Where | the room: at work, with friends, on the phone |
| Label | the dictionary's label, or *none* |
| Plain version | the same meaning in plain words |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **copying five idioms from a list** instead of hearing
them. The page then has the same problem as the list: nobody has shown you where they go.

The second is **checking the meaning but not the label**. You know what the idiom means but not where
it belongs.

The third is **a pause before every idiom** on the recording. Say the whole sentence again, with the
idiom at your normal speed.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers learn idioms the same way, by hearing them again and again. A short page of idioms you
own is worth more than a long list you've read.

Under pressure, the plain version is always there for you. Knowing it is part of owning the idiom.

A listener notices when an idiom comes out smoothly and in the right place. That's when it sounds like
your English, not like a book's.
