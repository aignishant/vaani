## One-line answer

Record two short answers to the same serious question. In take X, break the checks on purpose: use one
idiom in the wrong room, a humorous idiom for a serious thing, and one idiom with a word changed. In
take P, answer in plain words, with at most one idiom from your page, used where it fits. Play each to
a different listener and ask: *What did I mean, and did anything sound strange?* X should make its
listener stop or smile at the wrong moment. P should be understood and sound right.

## The story

Your neighbour's son had a harder test a month later. His father had a heart operation, and at the
building's gate an old neighbour asked how it went. He'd been practising idioms, and he answered:

> Oh, it was a piece of cake. The doctors were over the moon, and now he's back on his feet, touch
> wood.

Four idioms in one breath. The old man looked confused. *A piece of cake? It was a heart operation.*
The idioms weren't wrong, one by one. Together, and about something so serious, they sounded like a
joke.

That evening, the woman from the second floor asked. This time he said:

> It went well, thank God. He's home now and resting.

Nobody asked anything else. They'd understood.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **idiom**, **label (in a dictionary)**: from [part 1.1](1.1-heard-checked-then-said.md).
- **idiom page**, **plain version**: from [part 1.2](1.2-your-own-idiom-page.md).
- **the what-did-I-mean test**: a listener hears a recording once and says what the speaker meant, and
  whether anything sounded strange.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X, idioms broken (deliberate) | one idiom in the wrong room; one humorous idiom for a serious thing; one idiom with a word changed | a stop, a smile at the wrong moment, or a wrong guess at your meaning |
| P, plain first | plain words; at most one idiom from your page, where it fits | the right meaning; nothing strange |

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't hear your own idioms the way a listener does. To you they sound clever. This test lets a
listener tell you where one landed in the wrong place, before it happens in a real conversation.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[kick the bucket](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/kick_the_bucket) gives the meaning "To die" with the
labels idiomatic, euphemistic, colloquial and humorous.
[piece of cake](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/piece_of_cake): "A job, task or other activity that is
pleasant or, by extension, easy or simple."
[English-language idioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-language_idioms): an idiom has "a
figurative, non-literal meaning".

**Step one: choose a serious question.** Something real from your life: *How is your grandmother's
health?*, *How did the interview go?*, *What happened with the flat?*

**Step two: take X.** Write *deliberate* in your notebook. Answer the question in four or five
sentences, and break the checks on purpose:

1. Use one idiom whose label is *humorous* or *colloquial* about the serious thing.
2. Use one idiom with a word changed: *a piece of the cake*, *under the weathers*.
3. Use one idiom in the wrong room: something from your friends in a formal answer.

**Step three: take P.** Answer the same question in plain words. Use at most one idiom from your
[idiom page](1.2-your-own-idiom-page.md), only if it fits.

**Step four: the what-did-I-mean test.** Play X to one listener and P to another. Ask each:

> What did I mean? Did anything sound strange?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a listener who's too polite** to say anything sounded
strange. Ask again: *Which words would you change?*

The second is **a take P that still has three idioms**. One from your page is the most, and none is
fine.

The third, in real life, is **stacking idioms**, as in the story. Each one might be right; four
together sound like a performance.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use fewer idioms on serious subjects, not more. Illness, money trouble and bad news get
plain words.

Under pressure, you may reach for an idiom to sound relaxed. A plain sentence sounds more relaxed than a
wrong idiom does.

A native listener will almost always understand you. What they notice is the tone: whether you sound
kind, serious, or like you're joking at the wrong time.
