## One-line answer

A **body paragraph** proves one point of your thesis. It has four moves, in this order. The **point**:
a topic sentence that names one reason from your map. The **explanation**: one or two sentences saying
why that reason is true. The **example**: something specific, such as a real place, a number or a thing
that happened. The **link**: a last sentence that ties the point back to your answer. Every sentence in
the paragraph serves that one point.

## The story

Your friend was preparing for a college writing exam. Her tutor gave back her practice essay with one
paragraph marked in red. It began *Buses help people get to work.* Then it talked about her uncle's
scooter, the price of petrol, a new road near the station, and ended *There are many things to think
about.*

The tutor had written one question in the margin: *Which reason is this paragraph proving?*

She rewrote it. The new paragraph began with the reason from her thesis, *More evening buses would help
people keep their jobs.* It explained why, gave one real example, a neighbour who lost a night shift
because the last bus left too early, and ended by linking back to her answer. The tutor's margin note
the next week was one word: *Clear.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **essay**, **thesis**, **body paragraph**: a piece of several paragraphs answering one question,
  your answer in one sentence, and a middle paragraph making one point, from
  [day 99](../../../day-99-full-talk-structure/parts/02-writing/2.1-question-answer-three-points.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, saying the topic and the point, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **map (of an essay)**: the essay's points named briefly, usually inside the thesis, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/02-writing/2.1-general-to-your-answer.md).
- **evidence**: a fact or example that shows your reason is true, from
  [day 62](../../../day-62-agreeing-disagreeing/parts/02-writing/2.1-view-reason-example-other-side-close.md).
- **controlling idea**: the one point a paragraph is about. Every sentence in the paragraph serves it.
- **explanation**: the sentence or two after the topic sentence saying *why* the point is true.
- **link sentence**: the last sentence of a body paragraph, tying its point back to the thesis.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Point | names one reason from the map | *First, more evening buses would help people keep their jobs.* |
| 2. Explain | says why | *Many jobs in our town end after eight.* |
| 3. Example | shows it happening | *Last year, a neighbour …* |
| 4. Link | ties it back to the answer | *This is why the town needs later buses.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate essay has at least three body paragraphs, and the reader checks each one against the
map in your thesis. On day 103 you'll write the conclusion, which gathers the points these paragraphs
prove. A body paragraph that wanders leaves the conclusion nothing to gather.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/), from a university writing
centre, says a paragraph is "a sentence or group of sentences that support one main idea", which it
calls "the 'controlling idea,' because it controls what happens in the rest of the paragraph". It
says: "All of the sentences in a single paragraph should be related to a single controlling idea
(often expressed in the topic sentence of the paragraph)." And: "Every idea discussed in the paragraph
should be adequately explained and supported through evidence and details". It also says length alone
doesn't make a paragraph.
[Paragraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paragraph) notes that the topic sentence is "largely a
phenomenon of school-based writing". That's exactly where an essay is read, so this course uses it.

**The shape on the page.**

```text
  ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ POINT    First, [reason one from the map].            │  ← one sentence
  │ EXPLAIN  [why it is true].                            │  ← one or two
  │ EXAMPLE  For example, [a real place / number / event].│  ← specific
  │ LINK     [so this supports my answer].                │  ← one sentence
  └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        every line serves the POINT, and nothing else
```

**The point.** Take the first reason from your map, in the same order the map gives it. Write it as a
full sentence. A word like *First* or *The main reason* tells the reader where they are.

**The explanation.** Say why the point is true, in general. Don't give the example yet.

**The example.** Make it specific. *Many people* is not an example. *A neighbour who works night shifts
at the hospital* is. A number you know is true, a real place, a thing that happened. Start with *For
example* or *For instance* if it helps.

**The link.** Say how this point supports your thesis. Use different words from the thesis, not a copy.

**One paragraph, one point.** If a sentence is about a different reason, it belongs in a different
paragraph. If it's about nothing in the map, it doesn't belong in the essay.

**No contractions.** An essay is formal writing, so write *do not*, not *don't*. That's this course's
choice for formal pages.

**Name the trap.** A good example makes you think of a second one, and then a third about something
else. The paragraph drifts one sentence at a time, and each sentence seems to follow from the last.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the wandering paragraph**, as in the story. It starts on one reason
and ends somewhere else, with no link.

The second is **the paragraph with no example**, only general sentences:

> Buses are very useful. Many people need them. They are important for the town.

The reader agrees, and is not persuaded, because nothing has been shown.

The third is **the example with no point**: a story about a neighbour, with no topic sentence to say
what it proves.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers bend this shape. Sometimes the example comes before the explanation, and sometimes a
paragraph needs two examples. The one thing they keep is one point per paragraph.

Under pressure, in an exam, the link sentence is the one that gets dropped. Write it anyway. It's the
sentence the marker uses to see that you haven't forgotten the question.

A reader who can say the point of each body paragraph in a few words, and see how it proves your
answer, has read a good essay.
