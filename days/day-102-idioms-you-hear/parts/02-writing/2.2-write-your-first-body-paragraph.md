## One-line answer

Write the first body paragraph of your essay, four to seven sentences, in the order point, explain,
example, link. Then give it to a reader with your thesis and ask them to do two things: underline the
point, and circle the example. If they can't find both, or they underline the wrong sentence, the
paragraph isn't doing its job yet.

## The story

Your friend's tutor had a test for every paragraph. She gave the thesis and one paragraph to another
student and said: *Underline what this paragraph is proving. Circle the thing that shows it.*

For the old paragraph, the student underlined two different sentences and circled nothing. For the new
one, the student underlined the first sentence and circled the neighbour's story. It took a moment.

*That's how quickly an examiner does it,* the tutor said.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **an example that's a general truth**: *Many people have problems with
transport.* The reader has nothing to circle.

The second is **a point that isn't the first reason in the map**. The reader expects the map's order.

The third is **a link sentence that copies the thesis word for word**. Say how this reason supports it,
in new words.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often find the example first, from something they've seen, and then write the point it
proves. Either order works while you're drafting. On the page, the point comes first.

Under pressure, a paragraph gets longer, not clearer. Seven sentences are usually enough for one point.

A reader who can underline and circle in a moment trusts the next paragraph before they start it.
