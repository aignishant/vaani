## One-line answer

Make a copy of your body paragraph that wanders, on purpose. Add one sentence about a different reason,
swap the specific example for a general one, and drop the link sentence. Give the copy and your real
paragraph to two readers, each with the thesis. Ask each to cross out any sentence that doesn't belong,
and to say what the paragraph proves. The copy's reader should cross something out and struggle to say
the point. Your real paragraph's reader should cross out nothing.

## The story

Your friend's tutor kept one old paragraph from a past student, to show every new class. It began with
a clear point about buses and jobs. The third sentence was about the price of petrol. The fourth was
about a new road. The example was *Many people find it difficult.* There was no last line.

The tutor gave it out with a pencil and one instruction: *Cross out every sentence that isn't about the
first line.* Most students crossed out two sentences. Then she asked: *What did the paragraph prove?*
Nobody could say it in one sentence.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **body paragraph**, **controlling idea**, **link sentence**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-point-explain-example-link.md).
- **underline-and-circle test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-first-body-paragraph.md).
- **off-topic sentence**: a sentence in a paragraph that's about a different point.
- **cross-out test**: a reader, given the thesis and a paragraph, crosses out every sentence that
  doesn't serve the paragraph's point, and then says the point in one sentence.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your own paragraph always looks focused to you, because you know what you meant. A reader with a pencil
shows you where it drifts. The phase 6 gate essay will be read by someone with a pencil.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Paragraphs](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/paragraphs/): "All of the sentences in a single
paragraph should be related to a single controlling idea". Its word for this is unity.
[Paragraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paragraph): a paragraph has a topic sentence "and multiple
'supporting' or 'detail' sentences that explain or supply evidence".

**Step one: make copy W.** Copy your paragraph and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Add one sentence about a *different* reason from your map, in the middle.
2. Replace your specific example with a general one, such as *Many people find this difficult.*
3. Delete the link sentence.

**Step two: the cross-out test.** Give W and the thesis to one reader, and R and the thesis to a
different reader. Each crosses out any sentence that doesn't belong, then says:

> In one sentence, what does this paragraph prove?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if R's reader crosses out any sentence, or
says a point that isn't your reason. It's **GREEN** when R's reader crosses out nothing and says your
point, and W's reader crosses out at least one sentence.

**Step three: the repair.** If R went red, look at the sentence they crossed out. Move it to the
paragraph where it belongs, or delete it. If they said the wrong point, rewrite your first sentence to
name the reason plainly. Give R to a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who crosses out nothing in W** because every
sentence is about buses. Ask them: *Is every sentence about the first line, or just about the same
topic?* Same topic isn't enough.

The second is **an off-topic sentence you didn't add on purpose**, found in R. That's the real finding.
Fix it, and write it in your error ledger.

The third, in real writing, is **the second example that starts a new point**. One example is usually
enough. A second one must prove the same point.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers cut sentences they like, because they don't serve the paragraph. The sentence isn't
wasted; it often becomes part of another paragraph.

Under pressure, you'll add a sentence because it came to mind. Before you move on, read your first line
and your last line together. If the middle doesn't connect them, something has wandered in.

A marker doesn't cross out your sentences. They just give fewer marks for a paragraph whose point they
can't say.
