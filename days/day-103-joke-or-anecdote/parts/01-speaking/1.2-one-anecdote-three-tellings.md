## One-line answer

Take one true anecdote from your life and record it three times. After each telling, write down the
number of sentences in the set-up, and whether anything came after the punchline. Cut one thing each
time: a detail that doesn't help the ending, a set-up sentence, or a sentence after the punchline. The
third telling should be the shortest, with the punchline as the very last words.

## The story

Your cousin's samosa story wasn't always good. The first time she told it, at her office, it started
with the weather that morning. It had the name of the shop where she bought the samosas, and the price.
After the punchline she said *So yes, that was my Monday.*

By the time she told it at the family dinner, she'd told it five times. The weather was gone, and so was
the shop. The price had become *forty samosas*, because the number was funnier. The last words were *the
meeting was online*, and she'd learned to stop there.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **anecdote**, **set-up**, **build**, **punchline**, **landing**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-set-up-build-land.md).
- **telling**: one time you tell a story aloud.
- **tail**: anything you say after the punchline.
- **cut**: something you take out of the next telling.

## Why Vaani needs it

A story gets better when it's told again. In the phase 6 conversation, you won't have time to plan an
anecdote. You'll tell one you've told before, so this part gives you one that's ready.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **three tellings that grow**. Each time you remember
another detail and add it. Cut, don't add.

The second is **a punchline that changes words** each time. Once you've found the words that work, keep
them.

The third is **a tail you can't stop**. Close your mouth after the punchline, even if it feels rude.
The listener's reaction fills the silence.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent storytellers have a handful of anecdotes they tell again and again, polished by retelling. That's
not cheating. It's how stories work.

Under pressure, you'll forget the cuts and tell the long version. Telling it three times now makes the
short one the one your mouth remembers.

A native listener rarely notices a small grammar slip in an anecdote. They notice whether it got to the
point.
