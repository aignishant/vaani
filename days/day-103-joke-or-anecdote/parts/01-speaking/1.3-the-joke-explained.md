## One-line answer

Record your anecdote twice. In take E, break it on purpose: give the ending away in your first sentence,
and after the punchline, explain why it was funny. In take L, tell it as your third telling: set-up,
build, punchline last, then stop. Play each to a different listener, and ask: *Did you smile or laugh?
Where?* E should get less. L should get a smile at the punchline.

## The story

Your uncle likes telling jokes at family dinners, and he worries that people won't understand them. So
after every punchline, he explains it:

> … and the meeting was online! You see? Because she carried all the samosas, but nobody was there.
> Because it was online.

By the time he's finished explaining, the laugh has gone. His daughter tells the same stories with no
explanations, and the same table laughs.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **anecdote**, **set-up**, **punchline**, **landing**: from [part 1.1](1.1-set-up-build-land.md).
- **tail**, **telling**: from [part 1.2](1.2-one-anecdote-three-tellings.md).
- **giving it away**: saying the ending, or saying that something funny is coming, before the story.
- **the smile test**: a listener hears a recording and says whether, and where, they smiled or laughed.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E, explained (deliberate) | the ending in the first sentence; an explanation after the punchline | no smile, or a smile only at the start |
| L, landed | your third telling; punchline last; then stop | a smile at the punchline |

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't laugh at your own anecdote, so you can't tell whether it works. A listener can. This is the
only honest test of whether you're landing the punchline or explaining it away.

**Step one: take E.** Write *deliberate* in your notebook. Record your anecdote, and break it on purpose:

1. Start with the ending: *So the funny thing is, …*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a story that isn't funny to anyone but you**. Some
stories are funny because you were there. If two listeners don't smile, choose a different anecdote.

The second is **a listener who laughs at take E anyway**, because they're kind. Ask the second question:
*Where did you know how it would end?*

The third, in real life, is **the nervous laugh before the punchline**. If you laugh first, the listener
waits to see why. Say it straight.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A joke that isn't understood can be saved with one short line: *It was online. The whole meeting.* Say
it once, and move on. A long explanation is what kills it.

Under pressure, in a new language, you'll want to check that people understood. Watch their faces
instead. A smile answers the question.

Native listeners don't expect you to be funny. When you are, they remember it, and the conversation gets
warmer.
