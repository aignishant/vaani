## One-line answer

An essay **conclusion** is one short paragraph with three moves. **Answer again**: your thesis, in new
words, so the reader hears it once more. **Pull together**: one or two sentences showing how your points
fit together, not a list of them. **Why it matters**: a last sentence that looks beyond the essay, often
back at the real-life scene you opened with. A conclusion never brings in a new reason.

## The story

Your friend finished her practice essay about evening buses. Her last paragraph began *In conclusion*,
and then repeated her thesis word for word, and then listed her three reasons again, in the same words
as her topic sentences. It ended with a new idea: *Also, the buses should be electric.*

Her tutor wrote two notes in the margin. Beside the list: *I've just read these.* Beside the last line:
*Where is the paragraph about this?*

She rewrote it. The new conclusion said her answer in fresh words, showed how safety, jobs and pollution
all came back to the same empty bus stop, and ended at that bus stop, at nine at night, with a bus
arriving. The tutor wrote: *Now I'll remember it.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **essay**, **thesis**, **body paragraph**, **conclusion**: a piece of several paragraphs answering one
  question, your answer in one sentence, a middle paragraph making one point, and the last paragraph,
  from [day 99](../../../day-99-full-talk-structure/parts/02-writing/2.1-question-answer-three-points.md).
- **introduction**, **map (of an essay)**: the first paragraph, and the essay's points named inside the
  thesis, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/02-writing/2.1-general-to-your-answer.md).
- **link sentence**: the last sentence of a body paragraph, tying its point back to the thesis, from
  [day 102](../../../day-102-idioms-you-hear/parts/02-writing/2.1-point-explain-example-link.md).
- **restate**: say again in new words.
- **pull together**: show how several points connect to one answer, instead of listing them.
- **so-what line**: the last sentence of a conclusion, saying why the answer matters outside the essay.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Answer again | the thesis in new words | *Our town needs buses that run after dark.* |
| 2. Pull together | how the points connect | *Safety, jobs and cleaner air all start at the same empty bus stop.* |
| 3. So what | why it matters beyond the essay | *Until a bus stops there at nine, …* |

## Why Vaani needs it

This finishes the essay you started on day 101. The phase 6 gate is a five-paragraph essay of at least

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Conclusions](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/conclusions/), from a university writing
centre, says: "Your conclusion is your chance to have the last word on the subject." It suggests a
"So what" test: ask a friend to say "'So what?' or 'Why should anybody care?'" after each statement.
It describes a strategy that "brings the reader full circle": "if you begin by describing a scenario,
you can end with the same scenario". It says "Synthesize, don't summarize". And it lists things to
avoid, among them "Introducing a new idea or subtopic in your conclusion" and "Ending with a rephrased
thesis statement without any substantive changes."
[An opinion essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/opinion-essay)
tells learners to "Use phrases like *First of all*, *In addition* and *To sum up* to start each
paragraph".

**Answer again, in new words.** Read your thesis. Now say the same answer as if to someone who's just
walked in. Same answer, different words.

**Pull together, don't list.** A list repeats your topic sentences. Pulling together shows what they
have in common: one cause, one place, one kind of person they all affect.

**End beyond the essay.** Go back to the scene your introduction opened with, and show it changed. Or say
what would happen if nothing changed. That last sentence answers *So what?*

**No new reason.** If you think of a fourth reason now, either give it a body paragraph or leave it out.
A new idea in the conclusion is a question the essay never answers.

**About *In conclusion* and *To sum up*.** The two pages disagree. One lists *To sum up* as a useful way
to start the paragraph. The other calls *in conclusion* "unnecessary" and "overused". This course's
choice: you may use one, but the sentence after it must do the work. A conclusion that works without it
is stronger.

**Name the trap.** Copying your thesis feels safe, because you know it's right. The reader has already
read it. Saying it in new words shows you understand your own answer.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the thesis copied, then the points listed**, as in the story. The
reader reads the essay a second time, in short.

The second is **the new idea at the end**: *Also, the buses should be electric.* The reader wonders why
the essay didn't argue it.

The third is **the apology**: *This is only my opinion and I may be wrong.* It takes back the answer the
essay spent four paragraphs building.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often write the conclusion straight after the introduction, and fix both together. The
first line of the essay and the last line should feel like a pair.

Under pressure, in an exam, the conclusion is written in a hurry and becomes a copy. Three sentences are
enough, if they're the three moves.

A marker checks one thing first: does the conclusion give the same answer as the introduction? If it
does, in new words, and adds nothing new, the essay feels finished.
