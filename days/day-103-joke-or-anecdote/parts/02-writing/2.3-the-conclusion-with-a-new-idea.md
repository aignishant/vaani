## One-line answer

Make a copy of your conclusion that breaks it on purpose: the thesis copied word for word, the three
points listed, and a new reason at the end. Give the copy and your real conclusion to two readers, each
with your introduction. Ask each: *Which sentence in this conclusion did the essay not prove?* The copy's
reader should point at the new reason. Your real conclusion's reader should say *none*.

## The story

Your friend's tutor kept a stack of old conclusions for her classes. In one, a student had argued for
three paragraphs that schools should start later, and the conclusion ended: *Also, school uniforms
should be banned.*

She gave it to her class with the introduction and one question: *Which sentence in the conclusion did
the essay not prove?* Every student pointed at the uniforms. Then she asked what they thought of the
essay. Most of them talked about uniforms.

*One sentence,* she said, *and they've forgotten the other three paragraphs.*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **conclusion**, **restate**, **pull together**, **so-what line**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-answer-again-say-why-it-matters.md).
- **so-what test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-conclusion.md).
- **new idea (in a conclusion)**: a reason or topic the essay's body paragraphs never argued.
- **unproved-sentence test**: a reader, given the introduction and the conclusion, points at any
  sentence in the conclusion the essay did not prove, or says *none*.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate essay is judged as a whole. One new idea in the last paragraph can pull the reader's
attention away from everything you proved. This part shows you how quickly that happens.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Conclusions](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/conclusions/) lists, among things to avoid:
"Introducing a new idea or subtopic in your conclusion." And: "Ending with a rephrased thesis statement
without any substantive changes."
[An opinion essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/opinion-essay):
the model's conclusion gives the writer's opinion again, after the body paragraphs.

**Step one: make copy N.** Copy your conclusion and write *deliberate* at the top. Replace it with:

1. Your thesis, copied word for word.
2. Your three points, as a list: *…, … and ….*
3. One new reason the essay never argued, beginning *Also, …*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who only sees the conclusion**. Without the
introduction, they can't tell what the essay proved. Give them both.

The second is **a so-what line mistaken for a new idea**. A so-what line looks beyond the essay without
arguing something new. If the reader points at it, ask why. The answer tells you whether it went too
far.

The third, in real writing, is **the idea you had while writing**. It's often your best idea. It belongs
in the next essay.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers keep a note of good ideas that don't fit, instead of squeezing them into the last
paragraph.

Under pressure, the last paragraph is where a tired writer adds whatever comes to mind. Before you
finish, read your last sentence and ask: *Did my essay prove this?*

A marker notices a new idea in the conclusion at once, and it's the last thing they read before they
decide.
