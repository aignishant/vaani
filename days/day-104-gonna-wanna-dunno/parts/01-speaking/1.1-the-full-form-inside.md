## One-line answer

In fast, relaxed speech, some pairs of words melt into one: *going to* becomes **gonna**, *want to*
becomes **wanna**, *don't know* becomes **dunno**, and *have got to* becomes **gotta**. When you hear
one, rebuild the full form in your head. When you speak, you choose: the relaxed form with friends, the
full form when the room is formal. And *gonna* only replaces *going to* before a verb, never *going to*
a place.

## The story

Your friend sends you a voice note from the bus. You play it three times:

> Hey, I'm gonna be late. I dunno why, the traffic's crazy. You wanna start without me?

You catch *late* and *traffic*, but the rest sounds like one long word. Then you slow down and say what
each lump could be: *gonna*, *going to*. *Dunno*, *don't know*. *Wanna*, *want to*. Now it makes sense:
*I'm going to be late. I don't know why. Do you want to start without me?*

A week later, you leave a voice note for your landlord and hear yourself say *I'm gonna pay on Monday.*
It's not wrong. But you wouldn't write it that way to him, and you start to wonder whether you should
say it that way.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **contraction**: two words said and written as one shorter word, such as *I'm* for *I am*, from
  [day 35](../../../day-35-contractions-aloud/parts/01-speaking/1.1-two-words-one-beat.md).
- **weak form**: the quiet, short way a small word is said when it isn't stressed, often with a schwa,
  the lazy *uh* sound, from
  [day 30](../../../day-30-the-schwa/parts/01-speaking/1.2-the-small-words-go-weak.md).
- **register**: how formal or informal your words are for the person and the place, from
  [day 81](../../../day-81-formal-and-informal/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-request-ladder.md).
- **connected speech**: the way words join and change when they're said together at normal speed,
  instead of one by one.
- **relaxed form**: the melted version of two words, such as *gonna*. It's what you hear in fast,
  informal speech.
- **full form**: the two words said clearly, such as *going to*.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 conversation is with a real person at real speed, and relaxed forms are part of how people
talk. If you can't hear the full form inside *gonna*, you'll miss half a sentence. And if you use
*gonna* in the wrong room, you'll sound more casual than you meant to.

In these symbols, ˈ comes just before the loud syllable, and ə is the schwa.

**What the pages say.** The *gonna* page labels it colloquial, and says it "only occurs when *going to*
is a modal verb indicating a future tense": "one says *I'm gonna go now*, but *I'm gonna the mall* is not
observed." The *wanna* page says "The spoken contractions are widespread in everyday informal speech.
The written contractions are inappropriate outside informal writing or transcription of casual speech."
The *dunno* page labels it informal. The *gotta* page labels it colloquial.

**Hearing: rebuild the full form.** When you hear a lump you don't know, try each full form:

```text
   what you hear              what's inside
   I'm GONna be late     →    I'm going to be late
   you WANna come?       →    do you want to come?
   I dunNO               →    I don't know
   I GOTta go            →    I have got to go
```

The loud part stays loud. The small words, *to*, *do*, *have*, melt into it or disappear.

**Speaking: choose the room.** With friends and family, the relaxed forms are normal. In an interview,
on a formal call or with a customer, say the full form clearly. Both are English; they belong in
different rooms.

**The *gonna* limit.** *Gonna* replaces *going to* only before a verb: *I'm gonna call you.* When *going
to* means moving to a place, it stays: *I'm going to the market*, never *I'm gonna the market*.

**Name the trap.** You'll hear *gonna* and *wanna* so often in shows and songs that they can feel like
the normal words. They are normal, in relaxed speech. The trap is using them everywhere, or using
*gonna* before a place.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version, when listening, is **hearing a lump and giving up**: *I'm gonna* sounds
like one strange word, and you miss the plan it announces.

The second, when speaking, is **the relaxed form in a formal room**:

> Yeah, I dunno, I'm gonna check with my manager.

said to an interviewer. The listener understands, and hears someone who isn't taking the moment
seriously.

The third is ***gonna* before a place**: *I'm gonna the bank.* The listener hears a missing word.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers switch without thinking: *gonna* with friends, *going to* in a meeting, and often a
mixture. You don't need the relaxed forms to be understood. You do need to hear them.

Under pressure, at speed, you'll hear more relaxed forms, not fewer. If you miss something, ask: *Sorry,
you're going to what?* Saying the full form back is a quick way to check.

A native listener doesn't mind the full form in a relaxed chat; it just sounds careful. The relaxed form
in a formal room is the one they notice.
