## One-line answer

Record two answers to a formal question, such as a manager asking about your plans for the week. In
take B, break it on purpose: use *gonna*, *wanna* and *dunno* throughout, and put *gonna* before a place
once. In take C, use the full forms, clearly. Play each to a different listener and ask two questions:
*Who do you think I was talking to? Did any sentence sound wrong?* B's listener should guess a friend,
or name the *gonna* before a place. C's listener should guess someone formal and name nothing.

## The story

Your friend had a phone interview for a job at a hotel. The manager asked what he'd do on his first day.
He was nervous and spoke fast:

> Um, I dunno, I'm gonna the front desk first, I wanna learn the system, and then I'm gonna meet the
> team.

After the call, the manager told the agency he was friendly but "seemed very casual". He hadn't meant to
be casual. He'd meant to be quick.

For the second interview, he practised saying *going to* slowly. *I'm going to go to the front desk
first. I want to learn the system.* It felt stiff. On the call, it sounded careful.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the who-was-I-talking-to test**: a listener hears a recording and guesses who the speaker was
  talking to, and says whether any sentence sounded wrong.

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't hear your own register the way a stranger does, because you know who you meant to talk to. A
listener who guesses from the sound alone tells you what your voice is really saying.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[gonna](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/gonna): it "only occurs when *going to* is a modal verb indicating
a future tense": "one says *I'm gonna go now*, but *I'm gonna the mall* is not observed."
[wanna](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/wanna): "The written contractions are inappropriate outside
informal writing or transcription of casual speech."
[dunno](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/dunno): labelled informal.

**Step one: choose a formal question.** For example: *What are your plans for this week?* asked by a
manager, a teacher or an office.

**Step two: take B.** Write *deliberate* in your notebook. Answer in four or five sentences, and break it
on purpose:

1. Use *gonna*, *wanna* and *dunno* at least once each.
2. Put *gonna* before a place once: *I'm gonna the office.*

**Step three: take C.** Answer the same question with full forms, clearly: *going to*, *want to*, *don't
know*, *have to*.

**Step four: the who-was-I-talking-to test.** Play B to one listener and C to another. Don't tell them
who the question was from. Ask each:

> Who do you think I was talking to? Did any sentence sound wrong?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take C said so slowly that it sounds like reading**.
Full forms at a normal pace, not every word separate.

The second is **a listener who guesses from the words, not the sound**. Keep the facts the same in both
takes so only the forms change.

The third, in real life, is **relaxed forms coming back when nervous**, as in the story. Practise the
full forms for the questions you expect.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers in a formal room don't sound stiff when they use full forms. They sound careful, and
that's what the room wants.

Under pressure, speed and relaxed forms arrive together. Slowing down a little brings the full forms
back.

A native listener forgives *gonna* in almost any room. *Gonna* before a place is the one that sounds like
a mistake, in any room.
