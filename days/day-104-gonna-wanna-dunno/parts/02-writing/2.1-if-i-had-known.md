## One-line answer

To imagine a **different past**, write *If* + **had** + the third form of the verb, then **would have** +
the third form: *If I had left earlier, I would have caught the train.* It's about something that didn't
happen, and now can't change. When the past change leads to a result **now**, mix them: *If I had taken
that job, I would be in Pune now.* Both halves are about the past in the first; the second half moves to
the present in the mixed one.

## The story

Your cousin missed the last train home from a wedding. She'd stayed for one more photo. The next
morning she wrote in her diary, in English, for practice:

> If I didn't stay for the photo, I would catch the train.

She read it back and something felt wrong. It sounded like a plan for next time, not a story about last
night. Her brother read it and asked: *So are you going to catch it?*

She changed it:

> If I hadn't stayed for the photo, I would have caught the train.

Now it was clearly about last night, and clearly too late. That was exactly what she meant.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **clause**, **condition**, **result**: a group of words with its own subject and verb; the *if*
  half; and the other half, what happens, from
  [day 67](../../../day-67-suggestions-and-choosing/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-now-will-later.md).
- **first conditional**: *If* + present, *will* + verb, for a real possibility, from
  [day 67](../../../day-67-suggestions-and-choosing/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-now-will-later.md).
- **second conditional**: *If* + past, *would* + verb, for an imagined present or future, from
  [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-past-would.md).
- **past participle**: the third form of a verb, as in *go, went, gone*, from
  [day 63](../../../day-63-weekend-and-year-plans/parts/02-writing/2.1-have-or-has-and-the-third-form.md).
- **past perfect**: *had* + the past participle, as in *had left*, *had known*. It points further back
  than the past simple.
- **third conditional**: *If* + past perfect, *would have* + past participle. It imagines a different
  past.
- **mixed conditional**: an *if* sentence whose two halves are about different times, such as a past
  change with a present result.

| Kind | Condition | Result | Time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Third | *If I had left earlier,* | *I would have caught the train.* | past → past |
| Mixed | *If I had taken that job,* | *I would be in Pune now.* | past → now |
| Mixed | *If I weren't so careful,* | *I would have missed it.* | always → past |

## Why Vaani needs it

A narrative essay, which you'll write in this phase, often ends by looking back: what would have been
different. Regrets, lucky escapes and advice from experience all use this pattern. Without it, a
sentence about the past reads like a plan.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Conditionals: third and mixed](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/conditionals-third-mixed)
says: "The third conditional is used to imagine a different past. We imagine a change in a past
situation and the different result of that change." Its pattern is "If + past perfect >> would have +
past participle", with the example "If I had understood the instructions properly, I would have passed
the exam." For mixed conditionals it gives "If + past perfect >> would + infinitive", with "If I hadn't
got the job in Tokyo, I wouldn't be with my current partner.", and "If + past simple >> would have +
past participle", with "If it wasn't, I wouldn't have called you on your holiday." It adds: "The order
of the clauses does not change the meaning."
[Past perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/past-perfect) gives
the form "had + past participle".
[I'd](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/I%27d) gives two meanings: "Contraction of I + had" and
"Contraction of I + would or I + should."

**The third conditional on the page.**

```text
   If  +  had + past participle ,   would have + past participle
   If  I  had   left    earlier ,   I would have   caught  the train.
          ▲                   ▲            ▲
       two verbs          comma      three verbs
```

**The comma.** When the *if* clause comes first, a comma ends it. When the result comes first, no comma:
*I would have caught the train if I had left earlier.*

**Both halves change.** The condition takes *had*. The result takes *would have*. Change only one, and
the sentence points at the wrong time.

**The mixed conditional.** Keep the *if* half in the past, and let the result move to now: *If I had
learned to drive, I would drive to work now.* The word *now* or *today* often helps the reader see the
jump.

**Name the trap.** In the story, the writer used the second conditional, *If I didn't stay*, for a past
event. On the page, that looks like an imagined present or future, so the reader thinks the chance is
still open. And watch *I'd*: it can mean *I had* or *I would*. In *If I'd known, I'd have come*, the
first is *had* and the second is *would*. In formal writing, write them out.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the second conditional used for the past**, as in the story:

> If I knew about the meeting, I would come.

The reader asks: *So are you coming?*

The second is **would have in both halves**:

> If I would have known, I would have come.

The *if* half takes *had*, not *would have*.

The third is **the wrong third form**: *I would have went*, *I would have took*. The result takes the past
participle: *gone*, *taken*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers use the third conditional mostly for three jobs: regret (*If I had listened …*), relief
(*If the bus had been on time, I would have been in the accident*) and advice from experience (*If I had
known then what I know now …*).

Under pressure, the *if* clause is the half that breaks. Write *had* first, then check the result has
*would have*.

A formal reader notices *if I would have* at once. It's also common in careless native writing, which
is why a careful reader treats it as a mistake.
