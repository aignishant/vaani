## One-line answer

Make a copy of your paragraph with the conditionals broken on purpose: the second conditional in place
of the third (*If I left earlier, I would catch it*), one *if I would have*, and one wrong third form
(*would have went*). Give the copy and your real paragraph to two readers. Each runs the can-it-change
test and marks any verb that looks wrong. The copy's reader should answer *yes* at least once. Your real
paragraph's reader should answer *no* every time and mark nothing.

## The story

Your cousin sent a message to a friend who'd organised a trip she missed:

> If I knew about the trip, I would come. Next time tell me!

Her friend replied: *Great! We're going again on Saturday, come then.* But she'd meant *I'm sorry I
missed it.* The friend had read *If I knew … I would come* as a plan, and made one.

She wrote it the right way in her diary that night: *If I had known about the trip, I would have come.*
It was a small change on the page, and it meant something completely different.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

The wrong tense in a conditional doesn't look like a mistake to the writer, because they know it's about
the past. The reader doesn't know. This test shows you what a reader actually understands from the verbs
alone.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Conditionals: third and mixed](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/conditionals-third-mixed):
"The third conditional is used to imagine a different past."; "If + past perfect >> would have + past
participle".
[Past perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/past-perfect):
"had + past participle".

**Step one: make copy B.** Copy your paragraph and write *deliberate* at the top. Then change three
sentences:

1. One third conditional becomes a second conditional: *If I had left* → *If I left*; *would have caught*
   → *would catch*.
2. One *if* clause becomes *if I would have* + past participle.
3. One result gets a wrong third form: *would have went*, *would have took*, *would have ate*.

**Step two: the tests.** Give B to one reader and R to a different reader. For each conditional
sentence, they answer *Can this still change?* and circle any verb that looks wrong.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who knows the story**, and answers from what
they know, not from the verbs. Use a reader who doesn't know what happened.

The second is **a copy B where only one sentence changed**. Make all three changes, so the reader has
three chances to be misled.

The third, in real writing, is **the apology that sounds like a promise**, as in the story. *If I knew,
I would come* invites an answer you didn't mean to ask for.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers check the *if* clause first in any sentence about the past. It carries the time.

Under pressure, in a quick message, the second conditional slips in because it's shorter. Add the *had*.
It's one word, and it changes the meaning.

A reader won't write back *Your conditional is wrong*. They'll do what the friend did: answer the
sentence they read, not the one you meant.
