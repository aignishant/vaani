## One-line answer

In a debate, **answer** the other person's point before you make yours. Three moves. **Echo**: say
their point back in a few words, *So you're saying …*. **Respond**: either give them part of it, *I take
your point, but …*, or challenge it, *Have you considered …?*. **Your point**: your reason and one
example. The echo is what makes it an answer. Without it, you're just making a speech next to theirs.

## The story

At the tea stall after work, your friends are arguing about the new flyover near the market. Ravi says:

> The flyover's a good thing. Traffic will move faster.

Sunil answers straight away:

> No, no. The shops under it will lose customers. My uncle has a shop there.

Ravi repeats *Traffic will move faster*, louder. Sunil repeats *the shops*. They go round three times.
Neither has answered the other.

Then Ravi's sister joins in:

> So you're saying the shops will lose customers. I take your point, but people can't reach the shops
> now either, because the traffic's so bad. Last Sunday I gave up and went home.

Sunil stops. For the first time, someone has heard him and answered.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **opinion**, **reason**, **example**: what you think, why you think it, and something real that shows
  it, from [day 61](../../../day-61-giving-opinion/parts/01-speaking/1.1-opener-reason-example.md).
- **half agree**: agreeing with part of what someone said and not the rest, from
  [day 62](../../../day-62-agreeing-disagreeing/parts/01-speaking/1.1-agree-half-agree-disagree-softly.md).
- **debate**: a conversation where people argue for different answers to the same question.
- **echo**: saying the other person's point back to them in a few words, to show you heard it.
- **concede**: agree that the other person is right about part of it.
- **challenge**: question the other person's point politely, so they have to think about it again.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Echo | shows you heard their point | *So you're saying the shops will lose customers.* |
| Respond: concede | gives them part of it | *I take your point, but …* |
| Respond: challenge | questions it | *Have you considered the fact that …?* |
| Your point | your reason and an example | *People can't reach the shops now either. Last Sunday I …* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The recorded conversation at the end of phase 6 has two people in it. When the other person disagrees,
you need a way to answer, not just to repeat yourself. It's also the skill you'll use most in meetings
and family decisions.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Discussion](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/discussion) gives these
tips: "Say what you really think about the topic and explain why you think that." "Listen to what your
partner says and say if you agree or disagree." "Be polite if you disagree." It also warns: "Don't only
give your own opinion but also respond to your partner."
[Challenging someone's ideas](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b2/challenging-someones-ideas)
lists phrases including "I take your point, but", "Have you considered the fact that", "I see where
you're coming from." and "How exactly do you see this working?"
[Agreeing and disagreeing](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b1/agreeing-disagreeing)
lists "I see what you mean, but" and "OK, maybe you've got a point there."

**Echo first, in a few words.** Not their whole speech. The point:

> So you're saying the shops will lose customers.

It shows you listened, and it gives you a second to think.

**Respond: concede or challenge.** If they're partly right, say so, then turn: *I take your point, but …*
If you think they've missed something, ask about it: *Have you considered the fact that …?* or *How
exactly do you see that working?*

**Then your point, with a reason and an example.** One reason. One real example. Stop, and let them
answer you.

**Name the trap.** When you disagree strongly, you stop listening and wait for your turn. Then your turn
is a speech about your point, and the other person does the same. Nobody moves.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **repeating your point louder**, as in the story. It sounds like
certainty, but the other person hears that you didn't listen.

The second is **the echo that twists**:

> So you're saying the flyover is useless.

They didn't say that. Now you're arguing with something nobody said, and they'll correct you instead of
answering.

The third is ***but* with nothing before it**: *But the traffic …* without an echo. The listener hears
disagreement, not an answer.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers echo so quickly you barely notice it: *Right, the shops. But …* Even a few words count.

Under pressure, when you're angry or losing, the echo is the first thing to go. It's also the thing that
calms the other person down fastest.

A native listener hears the echo as respect. You can disagree completely and still sound fair, as long
as you've shown you heard them.
