## One-line answer

Choose a real question with two sides, and a partner who'll take the other side. Record a short debate
of three rounds: they make a point, you answer it with echo, respond and your point; then they answer
you. In each of your turns, use a different response: concede once, challenge once, and your choice once.
Then write down your three echoes and check each one against what your partner actually said.

## The story

Ravi's sister and Sunil kept talking about the flyover at the tea stall the next evening, and this time
they took turns properly. Sunil made his point. She echoed it, conceded part of it, and gave her reason.
Sunil echoed hers, and challenged it: *Have you considered that the shops will close while it's being
built?* She hadn't. She said so.

They didn't agree by the end. But each of them could say the other's point exactly, and both of them had
changed their mind about something small.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **debate**, **echo**, **concede**, **challenge**: from [part 1.1](1.1-their-point-before-yours.md).
- **round**: one turn from each speaker: they make a point, you answer.
- **partner**: the person who takes the other side for the exercise, whether or not they believe it.
- **fair echo**: an echo your partner agrees is what they said.

| Round | Their move | Your response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | a point for their side | echo, then **concede**, then your point |
| 2 | an answer to you | echo, then **challenge**, then your point |
| 3 | another answer | echo, then your choice, then your point |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a partner who agrees with you** to be kind. Ask them to
argue the other side as hard as they can. It's a game.

The second is **three concedes**. Conceding is polite, but if you concede every point, you've stopped
debating. Challenge at least once.

The third is **an echo that's longer than their point**. Keep it to a few words.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers don't win debates by speaking longer. They win by answering the strongest thing the
other person said.

Under pressure, you may not have an answer to a good challenge. It's fine to say so: *That's a good
point. I hadn't thought of that.* Then make your next point.

A native listener respects a speaker who concedes one point and holds the rest. It sounds honest.
