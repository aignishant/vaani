## One-line answer

Record your partner making one point, then record two answers to it. In take I, ignore their point on
purpose: no echo, no response, just your own point, said with confidence. In take A, answer properly:
echo, respond, your point. Play the partner's point plus take I to one listener, and the point plus take
A to another. Ask each: *Did the second speaker answer the first? What was the first speaker's point?*
I's listener should say *no*. A's listener should say *yes*, and name the point.

## The story

At the tea stall, the argument about the flyover went on for a week. One evening, an older man who'd
been listening quietly said to Sunil:

> Every day, you both talk. But you never answer each other. You just say your own thing again.

Sunil laughed, but he recognised it. He'd been waiting for his turn instead of listening. The next
evening, he started with *So you're saying the traffic's the real problem …*, and the older man nodded.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **debate**, **echo**, **concede**, **challenge**: from [part 1.1](1.1-their-point-before-yours.md).
- **round**, **fair echo**: from [part 1.2](1.2-three-rounds-with-a-partner.md).
- **parallel speech**: two people each making their own point, one after the other, without either
  answering the other.
- **the did-they-answer test**: a listener hears a point and a reply, and says whether the reply
  answered the point, and what the first point was.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I, ignored (deliberate) | no echo; no response; your own point, confidently | *no*, it didn't answer |
| A, answered | echo; concede or challenge; your point | *yes*, and the first speaker's point named |

## Why Vaani needs it

In your own head, every reply feels like an answer, because you know what you were thinking about. A
listener only hears the words. This test tells you whether your words answered.

**Step one: record your partner's point.** One or two sentences, on a real question.

**Step two: take I.** Write *deliberate* in your notebook. Record a reply that ignores the point: start
straight with your own point, confidently. No echo, no *I take your point*, no mention of what they said.

**Step three: take A.** Record a reply that answers: echo, concede or challenge, then your point.

**Step four: the did-they-answer test.** Play the partner's point followed by take I to one listener, and
the point followed by take A to another. Ask each:

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take I that answers by accident**, because your point
happens to be the opposite of theirs. Choose a point of yours that's about something else entirely.

The second is **a take A whose echo twists the point**. The listener says *yes*, but names a point the
partner never made. Check the echo against the recording.

The third, in real life, is **parallel speech in a meeting**, where everyone takes a turn and nobody
answers. One echo changes the whole meeting.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use the echo most when the argument is heated. It slows things down and makes the other
person feel heard.

Under pressure, the easiest thing to say is your own point again. The hardest, and the most useful, is
theirs.

A native listener watching a debate decides who's more reasonable by who answers. It's rarely the one
who talks the most.
