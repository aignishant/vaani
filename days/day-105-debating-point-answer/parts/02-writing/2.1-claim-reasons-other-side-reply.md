## One-line answer

An **argumentative essay** takes one side and defends it. It has five paragraphs. The introduction ends
with your **claim**. Two body paragraphs each prove one reason. A third body paragraph states the **other
side** fairly, in its strongest form, and then gives your **reply**: you concede part of it and show why
your answer still holds, or you show why it's mistaken. The conclusion answers again. The reply
paragraph is what makes it an argument and not an opinion.

## The story

A school near you ran an essay prize: *Should homework be banned for children under twelve?* The
headteacher read every entry. Most said *yes* or *no* and gave three reasons. They were fine.

The one that won said *no*, gave two reasons, and then wrote a whole paragraph beginning: *Many parents
say homework takes away time for play, and they are right that play matters.* It then showed how short,
regular homework could leave time for both.

The headteacher said later: *It was the only essay that talked to the people who disagreed with it.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **thesis**, **map (of an essay)**: your answer in one sentence, and its points named, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/02-writing/2.1-general-to-your-answer.md).
- **body paragraph**, **link sentence**: a middle paragraph making one point, and its last sentence tying
  back to the thesis, from
  [day 102](../../../day-102-idioms-you-hear/parts/02-writing/2.1-point-explain-example-link.md).
- **conclusion**: the last paragraph, which answers again, from
  [day 103](../../../day-103-joke-or-anecdote/parts/02-writing/2.1-answer-again-say-why-it-matters.md).
- **for and against**: two paragraphs, one for each side of a question, from
  [day 73](../../../day-73-reasons-for-and-against/parts/02-writing/2.1-two-paragraphs-two-topic-sentences.md).
- **argumentative essay**: an essay that takes one side of a question and defends it against the other.
- **claim**: the side you take, stated in the thesis.
- **counter-argument**: the strongest point on the other side.
- **reply**: your answer to the counter-argument. You either **concede** part of it and show your claim
  still holds, or **reject** it and show why it's mistaken.

| Paragraph | Job |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Introduction | the question in real life; context; the claim with its map |
| 2. Reason one | point, explain, example, link |
| 3. Reason two | point, explain, example, link |
| 4. The other side | the counter-argument, fairly; then your reply |
| 5. Conclusion | the claim again, in new words; why it matters |

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Argument](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/argument/), from a university writing centre,
says: "An argument is usually a main idea, often called a 'claim' or 'thesis statement,' backed up with
evidence that supports the idea." And: "By considering what someone who disagrees with your position
might have to say about your argument, you show that you have thought things through." It gives two
ways to reply: "Will you concede that your opponent has a point but explain why your audience should
nonetheless accept your argument? Will you reject the counterargument and explain why it is mistaken?"
And: "When you are summarizing opposing arguments, be charitable. Present each argument fairly and
objectively, rather than trying to make it look foolish."
[An opinion essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/opinion-essay):
"Mention other viewpoints with phrases like *Some people think*".

**The other-side paragraph, in two halves.**

```text
  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ THE OTHER SIDE   Some people argue that [their best point].│
  │                  [why a sensible person believes it].     │
  ├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ THE REPLY        concede:  They are right that …, but …   │
  │                  or reject: However, this overlooks …     │
  │                  [your evidence].                         │
  │ LINK             Therefore, [your claim still holds].     │
  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**State the other side at its strongest.** Choose the point a thoughtful opponent would really make, not
the weakest one. Give it a sentence of explanation, so the reader sees why sensible people believe it.

**Then reply, with evidence.** Concede if they're partly right; reject if they're wrong. Either way, give
a reason or an example, not just *I disagree*.

**Where it goes.** This course puts it in the fourth paragraph, after your own reasons, so the reader has
seen your case before they see it tested. Some writers put it earlier. Keep it in one place.

**No contractions.** An essay is formal, so write *do not*, not *don't*. That's this course's choice for
formal pages.

**Name the trap.** Mentioning the other side feels like weakening your own. It does the opposite: an
argument that has faced its strongest objection is harder to knock down.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **no other side at all**: three reasons for your claim, and nothing from
the people who disagree. The reader who disagrees finishes the essay still disagreeing.

The second is **the other side with no reply**: a fair paragraph about the opposing view that just ends.
Now the essay seems to agree with both sides.

The third is **the other side made foolish**:

> Some people think children should play all day and never learn anything.

Nobody thinks that. The reader who disagrees stops trusting the writer.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers find the other side by asking someone who disagrees, not by imagining them. The strongest
objection is usually one you didn't think of.

Under pressure, in an exam, the other-side paragraph is the first to be dropped. Keep it, even if it's
short. It's often the paragraph that lifts the mark.

A reader who disagrees with you will read your other-side paragraph more carefully than any other. If
they recognise their own view in it, they'll listen to your reply.
