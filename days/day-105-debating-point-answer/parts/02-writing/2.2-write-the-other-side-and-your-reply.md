## One-line answer

Plan a five-paragraph argumentative essay in notes, and write its fourth paragraph in full: the other
side at its strongest, then your reply with evidence, then a link to your claim. Before you write it,
ask one real person who disagrees with you for their best reason, and use theirs. Then show them the
paragraph and ask: *Is that what you think? Did I answer it?*

## The story

The girl who won the homework prize explained how she'd written the fourth paragraph. She'd asked her
own mother, who thought homework was too much, for her best reason. Her mother said: *Children need
time to play. Play is how they learn to get along with people.*

The girl wrote that down almost word for word. Then she showed her mother the paragraph, with her reply
underneath. Her mother read it and said: *Yes, that's what I think. And your answer is fair. I still
don't agree, but it's fair.*

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Argument](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/argument/): "When you are summarizing opposing
arguments, be charitable. Present each argument fairly and objectively, rather than trying to make it
look foolish." The two replies: concede "but explain why your audience should nonetheless accept your
argument", or "reject the counterargument and explain why it is mistaken".
[An opinion essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/opinion-essay):
"Mention other viewpoints with phrases like *Some people think*".

**Step two: ask a real person.** Find someone who disagrees with your claim. Ask: *What's your best
reason?* Write it down in their words.

**Step three: write paragraph four in full.** Five to seven sentences:

1. The other side, starting *Some people argue that …* or *Many … believe …*, in their best form.
2. One sentence on why a sensible person believes it.
3. Your reply: *They are right that …, but …* (concede) or *However, this overlooks …* (reject).
4. Your evidence: a reason or an example.
5. A link: *Therefore, …* and your claim, in new words.

No contractions.

**Step four: the fair-statement test.** Show the paragraph to the person you asked.

**Step five: fix.** If they said *no* to the first, rewrite the other side in their words. If they said
*no* to the second, your reply needs evidence, not just disagreement.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **inventing the other side** instead of asking someone.
It usually comes out weaker than the real one.

The second is **a reply longer than everything else**. Two or three sentences of reply are enough, if
they have evidence.

The third is **a person who agrees with you**, who can't tell you whether you've stated the other side
fairly. Find someone who really disagrees.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers test their arguments on people who disagree, before anyone else reads them. It's the
quickest way to find the weak spot.

Under pressure, you won't have anyone to ask. Imagine the person you know who'd disagree most, and write
their best reason.

A reader who disagrees and still says *that's fair* has given you the best review an argument can get.
