## One-line answer

Make a copy of your fourth paragraph that makes the other side look foolish, on purpose: a weak, extreme
version of their view, and a reply with no evidence, only mockery. Give the copy and your real paragraph
to two readers who hold the other view. Each scores it from one to five: *How fair is this to people like
me?* The copy should score low. Your real paragraph should score four or five.

## The story

One entry for the homework prize had a fourth paragraph that began: *Some lazy parents think children
should just play games all day and learn nothing.* Its reply was: *This is obviously silly.*

The headteacher showed it to a parent who was against homework, and asked what she thought. *He's not
talking about me,* she said. *Nobody thinks that. Why should I listen to the rest?*

She was the reader the essay most needed to persuade, and it had lost her in one sentence.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **counter-argument**, **reply**, **concede**, **reject**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-claim-reasons-other-side-reply.md).
- **fair-statement test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-the-other-side-and-your-reply.md).
- **straw man**: a weak or extreme version of the other side's view, made up so it's easy to knock
  down. Nobody actually holds it.
- **fairness score**: a reader who holds the other view scores your paragraph from one (not fair at all)
  to five (completely fair).

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't judge whether you've been fair to a view you don't hold. Only someone who holds it can. This
test gives them a number to say it with.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Argument](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/argument/): "When you are summarizing opposing
arguments, be charitable. Present each argument fairly and objectively, rather than trying to make it
look foolish." And: "Do not stop with having a point. You have to back up your point with evidence."
[An opinion essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/opinion-essay):
"Mention other viewpoints with phrases like *Some people think*".

**Step one: make copy S.** Copy your paragraph and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Replace the other side with an extreme version nobody holds, with an insulting word in it, such as
   *lazy* or *foolish*.
2. Replace your reply's evidence with *This is obviously wrong* or *This is silly.*

**Step two: the fairness score.** Give S to one reader who holds the other view, and R to a different
reader who holds it. Ask each:

> From one to five, how fair is this paragraph to people who think like you?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **readers who agree with you**. They'll score S high,
because they like seeing the other side knocked down. The test only works with readers who disagree.

The second is **an R that's fair but too soft**, conceding everything. It scores five, and the essay
no longer argues anything. Check that the reply still holds your claim.

The third, in real writing, is **one insulting word** in an otherwise fair paragraph: *Some parents,
without thinking, …* One word can drop the score.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers describe the other side the way that side would describe itself. It costs nothing, and
it's the fastest way to be taken seriously.

Under pressure, when you feel strongly, the insulting word comes first. Read your other-side sentence as
if you were one of them.

A marker who sees a straw man marks down the argument, because it answered a view nobody holds.
