## One-line answer

When you bring up a news story in conversation, give it four moves. **Source**: where you heard it and
how sure you are, *I read in the paper that …* or *Apparently …*. **Story**: what happened, in one or two
sentences. **View**: what you think about it, marked as yours, *I think …*. **Question**: hand it to the
other person, *What do you think?*. The source tells the listener how much to believe; the question
turns news into a conversation.

## The story

You're waiting at the barber's. The TV in the corner is showing the news, and the man in the next chair
says to the barber:

> Petrol's going up twenty rupees tomorrow. It's terrible.

The barber stops cutting. *Twenty? Where did you hear that?* The man isn't sure. A cousin, maybe, or
something on his phone. Now nobody knows whether to believe it, and the conversation turns into an
argument about whether it's true.

Later, a woman waiting with a newspaper says:

> I read in this morning's paper that the council's planning to close the old market for repairs. For
> six months, apparently. I think it's needed, but it'll be hard for the shopkeepers. Have you been
> there lately?

The barber answers her question, the man in the chair joins in, and they talk until it's her turn.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **headline**: the most important fact of a piece of news, said first, from
  [day 55](../../../day-55-passing-on-news/parts/01-speaking/1.1-did-you-hear-then-the-headline.md).
- **echo**: saying the other person's point back in a few words, from
  [day 105](../../../day-105-debating-point-answer/parts/01-speaking/1.1-their-point-before-yours.md).
- **source**: where a piece of information came from: a newspaper, the TV news, a friend.
- **current topic**: something in the news, or that people are talking about, right now.
- **hedge word (for news)**: a word that tells the listener you're not certain, such as *apparently*.
- **fact and view**: what happened, which could be checked; and what you think about it, which is yours.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | where you heard it; how sure you are | *I read in the paper that …* / *Apparently …* / *According to the news, …* |
| Story | what happened, in brief | *The council's planning to close the old market for repairs.* |
| View | what you think, marked as yours | *I think it's needed, but …* |
| Question | hands it over | *What do you think?* / *Have you heard about it?* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The recorded conversation at the end of phase 6 needs topics, and current events are the easiest topic
to start with anyone. They're also the easiest place to spread something wrong, or to lose the
conversation in an argument about what's true.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[apparently](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/apparently) gives the sense "According to what the speaker
has read or heard", with "Apparently you are quite a good dancer."
[according to](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/according_to) gives "Based on what is said or stated by;
on the authority of".
[Discussion](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/discussion): "Say what you
really think about the topic and explain why you think that." "Ask your partner what he/she thinks."
[Keeping a conversation going](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b1/keeping-conversation-going)
lists "How about you?" and "Really? I didn't know that!" among its phrases.

**Source first.** Before the story, say where it came from:

> I read in the paper that …
> I saw on the news that …
> Apparently, …

*Apparently* means you heard it and can't vouch for it. *According to* names who said it. *I read* or
*I saw* names how you know.

**The story in one or two sentences.** The headline, and one detail. Not the whole report.

**The view, marked.** *I think*, *In my view*, *For me*. It tells the listener: this part is mine, not
the news.

**The question.** *What do you think?*, *Have you heard about it?*, *Does it affect you?* Then listen,
and echo what they say.

**Name the trap.** When you've heard something many times, it starts to feel like a fact, and you say it
as one. The listener can't tell a rumour from a report unless you tell them.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **no source**, as in the story: *Petrol's going up tomorrow.* The listener
has to ask, and the conversation becomes about whether it's true.

The second is **the view said as fact**: *The new market plan is stupid.* The listener hears news, not
your opinion, and may repeat it as news.

The third is **no question**. You finish your story and wait. The other person says *Oh* and the topic
dies.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use *apparently* all the time in chat, and it does real work. It says *I'm passing this
on, not promising it.*

Under pressure, when the topic is something you feel strongly about, the view comes first and the
source disappears. Say where you heard it anyway.

A native listener asks *Where did you hear that?* when the source is missing. That question means you
should have said it.
