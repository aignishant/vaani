## One-line answer

Record your second-hand news story twice. In take F, tell it as a fact on purpose: no source, no hedge
word, your view mixed in as if it were news. In take S, tell it properly: source, hedge word, story,
view marked as yours. Play each to a different listener and ask: *Where did I hear this? How sure am I?
Which part is my opinion?* F's listener shouldn't be able to answer. S's listener should answer all
three correctly.

## The story

The man at the barber's told his petrol story to four more people that week, the same way: *Petrol's
going up twenty rupees tomorrow.* By Friday, a customer came in and told the barber the same thing, as a
fact he'd heard from *everyone*.

Petrol didn't go up. The man had heard it from his cousin, who'd guessed. Nobody along the way had said
*apparently*, so by the time it reached the barber, it sounded like news.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **source**, **hedge word (for news)**, **fact and view**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-source-story-view-question.md).
- **second-hand**: from [part 1.2](1.2-two-stories-from-this-week.md).
- **rumour**: a story passed from person to person that nobody has checked.
- **the three-questions test**: a listener hears a news story and answers: *Where did the speaker hear
  it? How sure are they? Which part is their opinion?*

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| F, as fact (deliberate) | no source; no hedge word; your view said as news | can't say the source; *very sure*; can't find the opinion |
| S, sourced | source; hedge word; story; view marked as yours | the source; *not sure*; the right opinion |

## Why Vaani needs it

You know where you heard something, so you forget to say it. A listener only has your words. This test
shows how much a listener can tell from the words alone.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take F that still says *I heard***. Habits are hard to
break on purpose. Say it like a newsreader.

The second is **a listener who knows the story already** and answers from what they know. Use someone
who hasn't heard it.

The third, in real life, is **passing on a rumour with a hedge, and hearing it come back without one**.
You can only control your own words. Keep the hedge.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers who are careful with news are trusted with it. A few words, *apparently*, *I'm not
sure*, cost nothing.

Under pressure, in a group, you'll want to sound informed. Sounding sure about a rumour is the fastest
way to sound uninformed later.

A native listener remembers who told them something that turned out wrong. They also remember who said
*I'm not sure*.
