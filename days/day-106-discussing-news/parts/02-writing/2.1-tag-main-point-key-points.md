## One-line answer

A **summary of an article** is a short paragraph, in your own words, that tells a reader what the
article says. It opens with a **tag line**: the article's title, where it appeared, and what kind of
piece it is. The next sentence gives the article's **main point**. Then come the two or three **key
points** that support it, in the article's order. Nothing of yours goes in: no opinion, no extra facts,
and no sentences copied from the article.

## The story

Your grandfather reads the newspaper every morning, but the print has got too small for his eyes. One
day he asks you to read him a long article about the new water scheme and tell him what it says.

The first time, you read him the first three paragraphs word for word. He stops you: *I don't need all
that. What's it about?* The second time, you tell him what *you* think of the scheme. He stops you
again: *Not what you think. What does the paper say?*

The third time, you say: *It's a report in today's paper about the water scheme. It says the city will
have water every day by next year. There are three reasons: a new pipe, a new tank, and repairs to the
old pipes.* He nods. *Good. Now tell me what you think.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **summary**: a short version of something, giving only the main points, from
  [day 77](../../../day-77-reporting-what-people-said/parts/02-writing/2.1-decided-who-does-what.md).
- **controlling idea**: the one point a paragraph is about, from
  [day 102](../../../day-102-idioms-you-hear/parts/02-writing/2.1-point-explain-example-link.md).
- **article**: a piece of writing in a newspaper, a magazine or on a news website.
- **tag line**: the opening of a summary, naming the article's title, where it appeared, and what kind of
  piece it is.
- **main point**: the one thing the whole article is saying.
- **key points**: the two or three supporting points the article gives for its main point.
- **paraphrase**: saying something in your own words, keeping the meaning.

| Sentence | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Tag line | title, where, what kind | *The report "…", in today's paper, describes …* |
| 2. Main point | what the whole article says | *It says the city will have water every day by next year.* |
| 3–5. Key points | the support, in order | *The first reason is …. The article also says …* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate writing portfolio shows range, and a summary is the one piece that isn't about you.
It's also what you'll write most often at work and in study: a short version of something long, for
someone who hasn't read it.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Summarizing in writing](https://writingcenter.uci.edu/2023/08/18/summarizing/), from a university
writing centre, says: "A summary of a text is a short overview of the main ideas written in your own
words." And: "Do not include your opinions on the topic or the author's ideas in your summary." It
suggests a "TAG line", which "stands for Title, Author, Genre". And: "There is no specified length for
writing summaries; they may be a few sentences or a few paragraphs depending on your writing project."
[Paraphrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraphrase) says: "A paraphrase or rephrase is a rewording of
a text that retains the original meaning." And: "A paraphrase is typically more detailed than a
summary."

**The tag line.** One sentence: the article's title in quotation marks, where it appeared (the paper,
the date), and what kind of piece it is: a report, an opinion column, an interview.

**The main point.** Ask: *If the article could say only one sentence, what would it be?* Write that, in
your own words.

**The key points, in order.** Two or three. Only the ones that support the main point. Leave out
examples, numbers you don't need, and side stories.

**The article's words, not yours.** Use reporting verbs that keep the article as the speaker: *The
article says*, *It reports*, *According to the article*. If the article gives an opinion, report it as
the article's: *The writer argues that …*

**Your own words.** Close the article before you write each sentence. If a phrase from the article is
essential, put it in quotation marks.

**No contractions.** A summary is formal writing. That's this course's choice for formal pages.

**Length.** The page gives no fixed length. This course's choice: under a hundred words for a newspaper
article.

**Name the trap.** Two things slip into a summary without your noticing: sentences copied from the
article, because they're already well written, and your own view, because you have one.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the first paragraphs retold**, as in the story. It's in order, and it
never reaches the main point.

The second is **the summary that argues**:

> The article says the city will have water every day, which is obviously a lie.

The reader can't tell where the article ends and the writer begins.

The third is **the copied summary**: each sentence lifted from the article, with a few words changed.
It's the article, shorter, not a summary.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers summarise from memory, with the article closed, and then check it against the article.
What they remember is usually the main point.

Under pressure, at work, a summary is often the only thing your manager reads. They'll decide from it,
so it has to be what the article says, not what you think.

A reader who later reads the article, and finds it says what your summary said, will trust your next
summary without checking.
