## One-line answer

Choose one real news article, read it twice, and close it. Write a summary of under a hundred words:
tag line, main point, two or three key points, in your own words, with nothing of yours. Then give the
summary and the article to a reader, and ask two questions: *Does the summary say what the article
says? Is anything missing that matters, or added that isn't there?*

## The story

Your grandfather liked the water-scheme summary so much that he asked for one every morning. You found a
routine. Read the article twice. Close the paper. Write the summary on a small card. Then open the paper
again and check each sentence against it.

On the third morning, the check found a sentence you'd added: a fact from the TV news, not from the
article. You crossed it out. Your grandfather never knew, but you did.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **article**, **tag line**, **main point**, **key points**, **paraphrase**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-tag-main-point-key-points.md).
- **reporting verb**: a verb that says who is speaking in a summary, such as *says*, *reports*,
  *argues*, *explains*.
- **check-against-the-article test**: a reader compares the summary with the article and says whether
  it matches, what's missing that matters, and what's added.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **writing with the article open**. The sentences come
out copied. Close it.

The second is **a summary of the first half**. The article's main point is sometimes in the middle or at
the end. Read the whole thing twice.

The third is **too many key points**. If you have five, two of them are examples. Keep the two or three
that support the main point.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers check a summary against its source every time, because something always slips in. The
check is part of the writing, not an extra.

Under pressure, a summary grows. The reader wanted it short. Count the words.

A reader who checks your summary against the article and finds nothing added is the reader who'll ask
you for the next one.
