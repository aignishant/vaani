## One-line answer

Make a copy of your summary with your opinion mixed in, on purpose: two sentences of your own view
written as if the article said them, and one judging word slipped into a key point. Give the copy and
your real summary to two readers who haven't seen the article. Each underlines every sentence they think
is the summary writer's opinion rather than the article's. The copy's reader may miss some; your real
summary should have nothing to underline, and they should underline nothing.

## The story

One morning the article was about a new parking rule near the market, and you didn't like the rule. The
card you wrote for your grandfather said: *The article explains the new parking rule, which will clearly
ruin the shops. Cars must park outside the market, a very foolish idea.*

At the park that evening, your grandfather told his friends that *the paper* said the rule would ruin the
shops. One of them had read the article. *It doesn't say that,* he said. *It says the shopkeepers asked
for it.* Your grandfather came home annoyed. *Was that the paper, or was that you?*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **main point**, **key points**, **tag line**: from [part 2.1](2.1-tag-main-point-key-points.md).
- **reporting verb**, **check-against-the-article test**: from
  [part 2.2](2.2-summarise-a-real-article.md).
- **judging word**: a word that shows the writer's opinion, such as *obviously*, *clearly*, *sadly*,
  *outdated*, *brilliant*.
- **opinion-hunt test**: a reader who hasn't seen the article underlines every sentence in a summary that
  they think is the summary writer's opinion.

## Why Vaani needs it

You know which sentences are yours, so you can't see them as a reader does. A reader who hasn't seen the
article shows you how easily your view reads as the article's, which is exactly what happened in the
story.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Summarizing in writing](https://writingcenter.uci.edu/2023/08/18/summarizing/): "Do not include your
opinions on the topic or the author's ideas in your summary."
[Paraphrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraphrase): "the (intentional or otherwise) reinterpretation
of a source to infer a meaning that is not explicitly evident in the source itself" is not a
paraphrase.

**Step one: make copy O.** Copy your summary and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Add two sentences of your own view, written as if the article said them: *The article shows that
   this is a mistake.*
2. Slip one judging word into a key point: *clearly*, *sadly*, *obviously*.

**Step two: the opinion-hunt test.** Give O to one reader and R to another. Neither sees the article.
Each underlines every sentence they think is the writer's opinion, not the article's.

**Step three: the repair.** If R went red, look at the underlined sentence. If it's your view, delete it.
If it's the article's view, make that plain with a reporting verb: *The writer argues that …* Give R to a
new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who has read the article**. They'll know what
it said, and find your opinions easily. Use someone who hasn't.

The second is **an R with a judging word you didn't notice**. That's the real finding. Delete it, and
write it in your error ledger.

The third, in real writing, is **the article's opinion reported as fact**. If the article is an opinion
column, say so: *The writer argues …*, not *It is true that …*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers keep their view for after the summary, in a separate paragraph, starting *In my view*.
The reader always knows which is which.

Under pressure, when you disagree with an article, the judging words creep in first. Read your summary
once, hunting only for them.

A reader who finds your opinion inside a summary stops trusting the summary, and often the writer too.
