## One-line answer

To say how you feel so that someone understands, give three things. **The exact word**: not *bad* or
*fine*, but *frustrated*, *disappointed*, *embarrassed* or *relieved*. **The cause**: what made you feel
it, with *because*, *about*, *with* or *that*. **The size**: *a bit*, *quite*, *really*. *I'm a bit
frustrated with the bank, because they lost my form again* tells the listener what happened inside you,
and why, and how much.

## The story

You call home in the evening. Your mother asks how your day was.

> It was bad.

She waits. *Bad how? Are you ill? Did something happen at work?* You don't want to explain everything,
so you say *No, just bad*, and she worries all night.

The next evening, the same question. This time you say:

> I'm really frustrated. The bank lost my form again, and I have to go back on Saturday. But I'm
> relieved about one thing: the landlord fixed the tap.

She laughs. *Poor you. At least the tap's working.* She knows exactly what kind of day it was, and she
isn't worried.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **how you feel (in your body)**: saying where it hurts and what kind of pain it is, from
  [day 28](../../../day-28-at-the-doctor/parts/01-speaking/1.1-where-it-hurts.md). Today is about
  feelings inside, not pain.
- **chunk you own**: a group of English words you can say without thinking, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/01-speaking/1.1-start-from-english-you-own.md).
- **feeling word**: a word for what's happening inside you, such as *frustrated* or *relieved*.
- **vague word**: a word that could mean many things, such as *bad*, *fine*, *okay*, *upset*.
- **cause**: the thing that made you feel it.
- **size word**: a word that says how strong a feeling is: *a bit*, *quite*, *really*.

| Feeling | What it means on the page | Often said with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| frustrated | "dissatisfied, agitated, discontent and/or slightly angry because one is unable to perform an action or fulfill a desire" | *with* someone or something |
| disappointed | "Defeated of expectations or hope; experiencing disappointment; let down." | *with*, *in*, *that*, *to* |
| embarrassed | "Having a feeling of shameful discomfort." | *about* |
| relieved | "Experiencing or exhibiting relief; freed from stress or discomfort." | *that*, *when* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 conversation will ask about your life, and a life has feelings in it. *It was good* ends a
topic. *I was relieved, because …* opens one.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[frustrated](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/frustrated) gives the sense quoted in the table, and the
example "She easily gets frustrated when it comes to financial matters."
[disappointed](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/disappointed) gives "Defeated of expectations or hope;
experiencing disappointment; let down.", with examples for each partner word: "She was disappointed in
you.", "She was disappointed with her performance.", "She was disappointed to be left out.", "She was
disappointed that you didn't go to her party."
[embarrassed](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/embarrassed) gives "Having a feeling of shameful
discomfort."
[relieved](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/relieved) gives "Experiencing or exhibiting relief; freed from
stress or discomfort.", with "She was extremely relieved when the lesson finished."

**The exact word.** Swap the vague word for the one that fits:

| Vague | Ask yourself | Maybe it's |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *bad* | Did something stop me doing what I wanted? | *frustrated* |
| *bad* | Did something not turn out as I hoped? | *disappointed* |
| *bad* | Did I look silly in front of people? | *embarrassed* |
| *fine* | Did a worry just end? | *relieved* |

**The cause.** Add what made you feel it:

> I'm frustrated with the bank.
> I was disappointed that the trip was cancelled.
> I was so embarrassed about my mistake.

**The size.** *A bit* for small, *quite* for medium, *really* or *so* for big. Put it just before the
feeling word: *a bit frustrated*, *really relieved*.

**Name the trap.** *I was embarrassed* and *I was embarrassing* sound almost the same, and mean
different things. The *-ed* word is how you felt. The *-ing* word is what you made other people feel.
All four feelings in the table are *-ed* words: *frustrated*, *disappointed*, *embarrassed*, *relieved*.
Say the *-ed* clearly at the end.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the vague word alone**, as in the story: *It was bad.* The listener has
to guess, and usually guesses worse than the truth.

The second is **the feeling with no cause**: *I'm really disappointed.* The listener asks *Why?* and you
have to start again.

The third is ***-ing* for a feeling about yourself**:

> I was so embarrassing.

The listener hears that *you* made other people feel embarrassed.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers still say *fine* and *not bad* when they don't want to talk. That's allowed. The exact
word is for when you do want to be understood.

Under pressure, upset, the vague word is what comes. Name the cause first, *The bank lost my form*, and
the feeling word often follows.

A native listener responds to the exact word with the right kind of sympathy: *Oh, how annoying* for
frustrated, *Oh no* for disappointed, *Thank goodness* for relieved.
