## One-line answer

Choose three real moments from your week that made you feel something different. Look up one feeling
word for each that you haven't used before, on a dictionary page, and check which small word goes with
it. Then record yourself telling someone about all three, as you would on a call home: the moment, the
exact word, the cause and the size. Finally, list every feeling word you said, and mark any vague ones.

## The story

After the call about the bank and the tap, you started noticing your feelings in English. On the bus,
waiting at the bank, lying awake. You found words you'd never needed before: *nervous* before the
interview, *proud* when your sister passed her exam, *homesick* on a Sunday evening.

On the next call home, you told your mother about all three. She said: *You sound different. You're
telling me what's really going on.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **feeling word**, **vague word**, **cause**, **size word**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-exact-word-cause-size.md).
- **partner word**: the small word that usually goes after a feeling word, such as *with* after
  *frustrated* or *about* after *embarrassed*. The dictionary page's examples show it.
- **feelings list**: every feeling word you said in a recording, written down, with any vague ones
  marked.

## Why Vaani needs it

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **three moments that all felt the same**. Choose moments
that felt different, or the new words will all be *happy* and *sad*.

The second is **a new word with the wrong partner word**: *disappointed about my exam results* when the
page shows *with* or *in*. Check the examples.

The third is **reading the new words from the table**. Say the moment first, then find the word as you
speak.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use a small set of feeling words very often, and a larger set now and then. You don't
need a hundred. You need the ten that fit your life.

Under pressure, when a feeling is strong, the word may not come. Say what happened instead, and let the
listener name the feeling: *So you were furious.* Then say *Yes, exactly.*

A native listener who hears the exact word feels closer to you. It's the difference between a report and
a conversation.
