## One-line answer

Record the same moment twice. In take V, use only vague words on purpose: *good*, *bad*, *fine*, *upset*,
no cause, no size. In take P, use the exact word, the cause and the size. Play each to a different
listener, and ask them to guess: *What was I feeling, and why?* V's listener should guess wrong or not
know. P's listener should name your feeling and its cause.

## The story

Your sister called one evening. You asked how her day was, and she said *Not good. I'm upset.* You spent
the whole call guessing. Was she ill? Angry with her husband? Worried about money? It turned out she'd
been passed over for a promotion at work. She was disappointed, and a bit embarrassed, because she'd told
everyone she'd get it.

If she'd said that at the start, you'd have known what to say. Instead, you'd spent the call asking the
wrong questions.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **feeling word**, **vague word**, **cause**, **size word**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-exact-word-cause-size.md).
- **partner word**, **feelings list**: from [part 1.2](1.2-three-moments-from-your-week.md).
- **the guess-my-feeling test**: a listener hears a recording and guesses what the speaker felt, and
  why.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V, vague (deliberate) | only *good*, *bad*, *fine*, *upset*; no cause; no size | a wrong guess, or *I don't know* |
| P, precise | the exact word; the cause; the size | your feeling and its cause, correctly |

## Why Vaani needs it

A vague word feels complete to you, because you know what's behind it. Only a listener can show you how
much it leaves out.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a listener who knows what happened**, and guesses from
that. Use someone who doesn't know about the moment.

The second is **a take V that tells the story in detail**, so the listener works out the feeling from the
facts. Keep V short and vague.

The third, in real life, is **two feelings at once**: disappointed *and* embarrassed, as in the story.
Name both. Real moments often have two.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers answer *How are you?* with *Fine* to strangers. With people who care, they give the
exact word, because it saves everyone the guessing.

Under pressure, when a feeling is strong, you may only manage *I'm upset.* Add one thing, the cause, and
the listener can help.

A native listener hears the exact feeling word as trust. It invites them in.
