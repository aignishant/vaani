## One-line answer

A **descriptive essay** makes a reader feel they've been somewhere. Its thesis is not an argument but a
**dominant impression**: the one feeling the place gives, such as *crowded and kind* or *quiet and a
little sad*. Each body paragraph shows that impression through **sense details**: what you see, hear,
smell, taste and touch there. It **shows** rather than tells: instead of *the kitchen was warm*, it gives
the steam on the window and the heat from the stove on your face.

## The story

A local library ran a writing competition: *A place I remember*. Your neighbour's daughter wrote about
her grandmother's kitchen. Her first draft said: *My grandmother's kitchen was very beautiful and nice. It
was a lovely place and I have many amazing memories there.*

The librarian who helped with entries read it and said: *I believe you. But I can't see it.* She asked
three questions: *What could you hear? What could you smell? What did you touch?*

The second draft began: *The first thing you noticed was the pressure cooker, whistling twice, and then
the smell of cumin reaching the door before you did.* The librarian read the whole essay without
stopping.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **adjective**: a word that tells you more about a noun, such as *old*, *warm*, *small*, from
  [day 28](../../../day-28-at-the-doctor/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-an-adjective-does.md).
- **one true detail**: a small, real thing that makes a place this place and no other, from
  [day 54](../../../day-54-your-town-or-village/parts/02-writing/2.1-where-what-and-one-true-detail.md).
- **controlling idea**, **body paragraph**: the one point a paragraph is about, and a middle paragraph
  of an essay, from
  [day 102](../../../day-102-idioms-you-hear/parts/02-writing/2.1-point-explain-example-link.md).
- **descriptive essay**: an essay whose job is to make a reader see, hear and feel a place, a person or
  a thing.
- **dominant impression**: the one feeling a place gives, which the whole essay shows. It takes the place
  of an argument in the thesis.
- **sense detail**: a detail from one of the five senses: sight, sound, smell, taste, touch.
- **show, don't tell**: giving the details that make a reader feel something, instead of naming the
  feeling.

| Sense | Ask yourself | For example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sight | What's the first thing your eye goes to? | *a calendar from three years ago, still on the wall* |
| Sound | What could you hear with your eyes shut? | *the pressure cooker whistling twice* |
| Smell | What reached you before you saw anything? | *cumin, and something burning slightly* |
| Taste | What did you eat or drink there? | *sweet, milky tea, too hot to drink* |
| Touch | What did your hands or skin feel? | *the cold stone floor under bare feet* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Show, don't tell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show,_don%27t_tell) describes a technique "to allow the
reader to experience the story through actions, words, subtext, thoughts, senses, and feelings rather
than through the author's exposition, summarization, and description". It also warns: "Sometimes a
writer tells as a shortcut", and "If you try to do it constantly, the parts that are supposed to stand
out won't, and your readers will get exhausted."
[Imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imagery) defines imagery as "the literary device of using
sensory language", and names five kinds, for sight, sound, smell, taste and touch: visual, auditory,
olfactory, gustatory and tactile.

**The thesis is the impression.** One sentence naming the place and the one feeling it gives:

> My grandmother's kitchen was the loudest, warmest room in our house.

**Each body paragraph shows it.** Its topic sentence names one area or one moment. Its details come from
more than one sense. Its last line ties back to the impression.

**Show, then tell once, if at all.** Give the details first. If you name the feeling, do it once, after
the reader has already felt it.

**Adjectives that show, not adjectives that judge.** *Beautiful*, *nice*, *amazing* and *lovely* tell
the reader your opinion. *Cracked*, *steaming*, *sticky*, *faded* tell them what's there.

**No contractions.** An essay is formal writing. That's this course's choice for formal pages.

**Name the trap.** When you love a place, the first words that come are *beautiful* and *amazing*.
They're true for you and invisible to the reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the essay of judging adjectives**, as in the story: *beautiful*, *nice*,
*lovely*, *amazing*. The reader believes you and sees nothing.

The second is **sight only**. Every detail is something you saw. The reader gets a photograph, not a
place.

The third is **details with no impression**: a list of things in the room, all true, adding up to
nothing. The reader asks: *What was it like?*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers choose a few strong details and leave the rest out. Three sharp details beat ten weak
ones.

Under pressure, write the senses in notes first, one line each. The paragraph builds itself from them.

A reader remembers one detail from a good description, often a sound or a smell, and it brings the whole
place back for them.
