## One-line answer

Write a short descriptive essay of three or four paragraphs about one real place you know well: an
introduction ending with the dominant impression, one or two body paragraphs of sense details, and a
short conclusion. Then give it to a reader and ask them to write down, from memory, three things they
could see, hear, smell, taste or touch, and the feeling of the place. If they can, the essay has shown
it.

## The story

The neighbour's daughter wrote her second draft from a table of senses. For each part of the kitchen,
the stove, the window, the floor, she wrote what she could see, hear, smell and touch there. Some boxes
stayed empty. Others filled up at once.

When she'd finished, she gave it to her father without saying anything, and took it back. *Tell me three
things from it,* she said. He said: *The pressure cooker whistling. The cold floor. The calendar that
nobody changed.* Then he said: *It was noisy, wasn't it? But you felt safe there.* That was her
impression, almost word for word.

  feeling of the place.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a sense table with only sight filled in**. Leave the
page and go and stand in the place, or remember it with your eyes shut.

The second is **every box used**. You don't need fifteen details. Choose the strongest.

The third is **a place you don't know well**, such as a famous building you've visited once. Choose
somewhere you've spent real time.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers describe places they know from inside. The small, odd detail, the calendar nobody changed,
is what makes a reader believe you were there.

Under pressure, a description grows adjectives. Replace each judging adjective with one thing a reader
could see or hear.

A reader who remembers three details after one reading has, in a small way, been to the place.
