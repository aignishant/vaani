## One-line answer

Make a copy of one body paragraph from your descriptive essay with every sense detail replaced by a
judging adjective, on purpose: *beautiful*, *nice*, *amazing*, *lovely*. Give the copy and your real
paragraph to two readers. Each reads it once, hands it back, and writes down everything they could see,
hear, smell, taste or touch. The copy's reader should write almost nothing. Your real paragraph's reader
should write at least three sense details.

## The story

The librarian kept the first draft of the kitchen essay, with permission, to show the next year's
entrants. She read it aloud to them: *My grandmother's kitchen was very beautiful and nice. It was a
lovely place and I have many amazing memories there.*

Then she asked: *What colour were the walls? What did it smell like? What could you hear?* Nobody could
say. Then she read the second draft, and asked the same questions. Every hand went up.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **dominant impression**, **sense detail**, **show, don't tell**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-one-impression-many-senses.md).
- **three-things test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-describe-one-real-place.md).
- **judging adjective**: an adjective that gives the writer's opinion, not a thing a reader could see or
  hear: *beautiful*, *nice*, *amazing*, *lovely*.
- **recall test**: a reader reads a paragraph once, hands it back, and writes down every sense detail
  they can remember.

## Why Vaani needs it

A judging adjective feels full of meaning to you, because you're remembering the place while you write
it. The recall test shows how much of that memory reaches a reader.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Show, don't tell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show,_don%27t_tell): the reader should "experience the
story through actions, words, subtext, thoughts, senses, and feelings rather than through the author's
exposition, summarization, and description".
[Imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imagery): "the literary device of using sensory language".

**Step one: make copy A.** Copy one body paragraph and write *deliberate* at the top. Replace every sense
detail with a judging adjective: *the pressure cooker whistling twice* becomes *the lovely sounds*; *the
cold stone floor* becomes *the nice floor*.

**Step two: the recall test.** Give A to one reader and R to a different reader. Each reads once, hands it
back, and writes down every sense detail they can remember.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who knows the place**, and writes what they
remember from being there. Use someone who's never been.

The second is **a reader who's allowed to look back**. The test is what stays after one reading. Take
the page away.

The third, in real writing, is **the judging adjective in the thesis**: *My grandmother's kitchen was the
most beautiful room.* Replace it with the impression: *the loudest, warmest room*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers use judging adjectives sparingly, and usually after the details, when the reader already
agrees.

Under pressure, a judging adjective is the quickest thing to write. It's also the quickest thing for a
reader to forget.

A reader who can recall your details can picture your place. That's the whole job of a description.
