## §1 Where we are

During a power cut, your brother tells a story that starts *I lost my passport, but I found it*, and your
nephew goes back to his phone. Your grandmother starts with *someone at the back door* the night before
her wedding, and nobody moves until the last word. The speaking folder is about suspense: plant the
question, hold back the answer, slow down and answer last. You tell a real story and stop just before
the end to ask for a guess, then give the ending away on purpose and let listeners raise a hand when
they know how it ends.

Your nephew's essay about the day his school bus broke down ended *And we got there just in time*, and
his teacher wrote one word: *And?* The writing folder is about the narrative essay: one small, true
story in the past tenses, a turning point, and a reflection that names what changed. You write your own,
then take the point out on purpose and let a reader try to say why you told it.

Choose either folder first. Each stands on its own. Follow the parts in that folder's order.

## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** In [part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-ending-in-the-first-line.md), two listeners each
raise a hand when they know how a story ends, then score how much they wanted to know. RED is when the
held-back take's listener raises a hand before the last two sentences or scores three or less. GREEN is
when they raise it only in the last two sentences and score four or five.

**Writing:** In [part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-the-story-with-no-point.md), two readers each say why the
writer told the story and whether it happened now or in the past. RED is when your real essay's reader
can't say why, gives a reason that doesn't match your reflection, or says *now*. GREEN is when they give
a matching reason and say *past*, and the copy's reader struggles with at least one.

Only record what you actually heard or saw. A deliberate exercise is labelled deliberate.

## §7 Traps

- The ending in the first sentence.
- No question planted.
- Details that don't add to the uncertainty.
- Rushing the answer.
- Story notes written in full sentences.
- A smile that gives the ending away.
- A narrative essay with no reflection.
- A moral stuck on in place of a change.
- A tense slip into the present in the exciting part.
- A story too big for four paragraphs.
- Direct speech with no quotation marks.

## §9 Say it out loud

After your practice, tell a friend what you did. Use this only when it is true: "I learned to tell a
story with suspense. I plant a question early, tell the steps without giving the answer away, slow down
at the end and give the answer last. I told a real story and stopped just before the end to ask my
listener what they thought had happened. Then I gave the ending away in the first line on purpose, and
my listener knew it at once and didn't care much. In writing, I wrote a narrative essay about a small
true story, in the past tenses, ending with what it changed for me. A reader could say what happened and
why I told it. Then I took the reflection out and slipped into the present, and a reader couldn't say
why I'd told it." If you did only one folder, say only that part.
