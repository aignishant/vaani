## One-line answer

**Suspense** is the listener wanting to know what happens next. You make it in three moves. **Plant the
question** early: something is wrong, missing, or coming. **Hold back the answer**: tell the steps in
order, each one making the question sharper, without giving the ending away. **Answer last**: slow down,
use shorter sentences, and give the answer in the final line. The listener should know the question
from the start and the answer only at the end.

## The story

There's a power cut one evening, and the family sits together by candlelight. Your little nephew asks
for a story. Your brother starts:

> Once I lost my passport at the airport, but I found it in my bag. So, I was at the airport …

Your nephew is already looking at his phone. He knows how it ends.

Your grandmother tells one next:

> The night before my wedding, I heard someone at the back door. It was late. Everyone was asleep. I
> took the lamp and went to look. The door was open. There were footsteps in the yard. I lifted the
> lamp. And there was my father, carrying a goat he'd bought as a surprise.

Your nephew doesn't move until the last word. Then everyone laughs.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **set-up**, **build**, **punchline**: the first sentences saying who and where, the middle where things
  happen in order, and the last line, from
  [day 103](../../../day-103-joke-or-anecdote/parts/01-speaking/1.1-set-up-build-land.md).
- **beginning, middle, end**: the three parts of a story a listener waits for, from
  [day 43](../../../day-43-story-with-three-parts/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-three-parts-a-listener-waits-for.md).
- **suspense**: the feeling of wanting to know what happens next, because something is uncertain.
- **the question (in a story)**: the thing the listener wants to know: *Who's at the door? Will she get
  there in time?*
- **holding back**: telling the steps of a story without giving away the answer.
- **slowing down**: saying the last part of a story in shorter sentences, with small pauses between them.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plant the question | something is wrong, missing or coming | *I heard someone at the back door.* |
| Hold back | each step makes the question sharper | *It was late. Everyone was asleep. The door was open.* |
| Answer last | slower, shorter, then the answer | *I lifted the lamp. And there was my father …* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate has an unscripted talk and a recorded conversation. A story told with suspense keeps a
listener with you through a long turn. It's also the difference between a story people wait for and one
they talk over.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Suspense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspense) defines it as "a state of anxiety or excitement
caused by mysteriousness, uncertainty, doubt, or undecidedness". In stories, it describes "the audience's
excited anticipation about the plot or conflict", and says it may be about "who, what, or how", or about
"when", where "the outcome is inevitable from foregoing events, and the suspense resides in the
audience's anxious or frightened anticipation".
[Punch line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punch_line): the punch line "follows the introductory framing
of the joke and the narrative which sets up the punch line".

**Plant the question in the first two sentences.** Something the listener wants answered: a sound, a
missing thing, a deadline, a stranger.

> The night before my wedding, I heard someone at the back door.

**Hold back.** Tell each step in order, in the past simple. Each one should make the question sharper,
not answer it. Details that add to the uncertainty stay: *It was late. Everyone was asleep.* Details that
don't, go.

**Slow down at the end.** Shorter sentences. A small pause between them. Your voice a little quieter, if
it suits the story. This is where the listener leans in.

**Answer in the last line.** Then stop, as with a punchline.

**Name the trap.** When you're nervous that a story is too long, you tell the ending first to be safe.
It makes the story shorter, and it removes the only reason to listen.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the ending in the first line**, as in the story: *I lost my passport,
but I found it.* There's no question left.

The second is **no question planted**: the story starts with the weather and the date, and the listener
doesn't know what they're waiting for.

The third is **rushing the end**: the steps are told slowly and the answer is thrown away fast, in the
middle of a long sentence.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent storytellers often start with a line like *You won't believe what happened on Sunday.* It plants
a question without giving anything away.

Under pressure, when people might interrupt, a planted question buys you the floor. They'll wait to hear
the answer.

A native listener shows suspense with their face and with small sounds: *No!*, *What?*, *And then?* When
you hear them, you're doing it right. Don't answer too quickly.
