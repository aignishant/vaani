## One-line answer

Choose one true story from your life with an uncertain moment in it. Before you record, write only three
things in notes: the question, the steps in order, and the answer. Record the story to a real listener.
Stop once in the middle, just before the answer, and ask: *What do you think happened?* If they're
guessing, the question worked. Then finish the story.

## The story

The next power cut, your nephew asked your grandmother how she'd learned to hold a story back. She said
she always knew three things before she started: what the listener would want to know, the steps to get
there, and the last line. Everything else she made up as she went.

Then she told him to try. He told the story of the day his school bus broke down on the way to an exam.
Halfway through, she stopped him: *Did you get there in time?* He grinned. *Wait and see.* She said:
*Good. Now you've got it.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **suspense**, **the question (in a story)**, **holding back**, **slowing down**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-hold-back-the-answer.md).
- **story notes**: three lines written before telling: the question, the steps, the answer.
- **the mid-story guess**: stopping just before the answer to ask the listener what they think happened.

## Why Vaani needs it

A story in the phase 6 conversation won't be planned in full. Three lines of notes, or three lines in your
head, are enough to keep suspense in an unplanned story.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **story notes that are the whole story**, written in full
sentences. You'll read them. Three lines only.

The second is **stopping too early** for the guess, before the question is sharp. Stop just before the
answer.

The third is **a listener who guesses right**. That's fine; suspense doesn't need a surprise. It needs a
question they wanted answered.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent storytellers do the mid-story stop without planning it: *And guess who was standing there?* It
pulls the listener in.

Under pressure, a story told in a group may be interrupted. If the question's been planted, someone will
say *So what happened?* and give you the floor back.

A native listener who asks *And then?* is telling you the suspense is working.
