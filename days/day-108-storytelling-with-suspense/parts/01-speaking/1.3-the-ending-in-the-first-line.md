## One-line answer

Record your story twice. In take E, give the ending away in the first sentence on purpose, and rush the
last part. In take H, plant the question, hold back, slow down and answer last. Play each to a different
listener. Ask each to raise a hand at the moment they knew how it would end, and afterwards to score from
one to five: *How much did you want to know what happened?* E's listener should raise a hand at once and
score low. H's listener should raise it near the end and score four or five.

## The story

At the end of the power cut, the lights came back on, and your brother asked for another turn. He told
the passport story again, the same way: *So I lost my passport, but it was in my bag the whole time.*
Nobody looked up.

Your grandmother said: *Tell it again. But this time, don't tell us you found it.* He started: *I was at
the check-in desk, and the woman asked for my passport. I opened my bag. It wasn't there.* Your nephew
put his phone down.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **suspense**, **the question (in a story)**, **holding back**, **slowing down**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-hold-back-the-answer.md).
- **story notes**: from [part 1.2](1.2-a-real-story-with-a-question-in-it.md).
- **the hand-up test**: a listener raises a hand at the moment they know how a story will end, and then
  scores from one to five how much they wanted to know what happened.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E, ending first (deliberate) | the ending in the first sentence; the last part rushed | hand up at once; a low score |
| H, held back | the question planted; steps held back; answer last, slowly | hand up near the end; four or five |

## Why Vaani needs it

You always know the ending, so you can't feel the suspense yourself. The hand-up test shows you exactly
where a listener stops wondering.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a listener who's heard the story before**. Use someone
new.

The second is **a take H that's twice as long as E**, padded to seem suspenseful. Suspense comes from
what you hold back, not from how much you add.

The third, in real life, is **the ending given away by your face**, smiling from the start of a story
with a happy ending. Keep a straight face until the answer.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent storytellers keep the question open even in short stories. One planted sentence is enough.

Under pressure, in an interview or a meeting, a short story with a question in it holds attention better
than a list of facts.

A native listener forgives a story with simple English. They don't forgive one that gave away its ending
and then kept going.
