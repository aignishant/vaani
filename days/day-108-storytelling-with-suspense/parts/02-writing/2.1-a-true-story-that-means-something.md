## One-line answer

A **narrative essay** tells one true story from your life, and then says what it meant. It opens with
the scene and a hint of why it matters. The body tells the events in time order, in the past tenses,
building to the **turning point**, the moment things changed. The last paragraph is the **reflection**:
what you understood, or how you changed, because of it. A story without the reflection is an anecdote.
The reflection is what makes it an essay.

## The story

Your nephew's school magazine asked for an essay: *A day I will not forget.* He wrote about the day his
school bus broke down on the way to an exam. It was a good story: the smoke from the engine, the phone
with no battery, the truck driver who gave three students a lift. It ended: *And we got there just in
time.*

His teacher wrote one question underneath: *And?*

He added a last paragraph. It said that until that day, he'd never asked a stranger for help, and that
he'd since learned that most people say yes. The teacher put the essay on the first page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **story in six sentences**: a short story with a setting, a problem, events and an ending, from
  [day 43](../../../day-43-story-with-three-parts/parts/02-writing/2.1-six-sentences-six-jobs.md).
- **past continuous**: *was* or *were* + a verb ending in *-ing*, for something in progress in the past,
  as in *I was waiting*, from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/02-writing/2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **past perfect**: *had* + the past participle, pointing further back than the past simple, as in *had
  left*, from [day 104](../../../day-104-gonna-wanna-dunno/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-i-had-known.md).
- **conclusion**: the last paragraph of an essay, from
  [day 103](../../../day-103-joke-or-anecdote/parts/02-writing/2.1-answer-again-say-why-it-matters.md).
- **narrative essay**: an essay that tells one true story from the writer's life and says what it meant.
- **turning point**: the moment in a story when things change.
- **reflection**: the part of a narrative essay that says what the writer understood or how they changed
  because of the story.

| Paragraph | Job | Main tense |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Opening | the scene; a hint of why it matters | past simple and past continuous |
| 2–3. The events | in time order, building to the turning point | past simple, with past continuous and past perfect where needed |
| 4. Reflection | what it meant; how you changed | present and past together: *Until that day, I had never … Now I …* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[A short story](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/short-story)
tells learners to use "the past simple, past continuous and the past perfect", with the examples "At half
past seven she was still waiting" and "Her friends had forgotten", as well as "adjectives and adverbs",
"direct speech with reporting verbs and punctuation", and words like "then, after that or in the end".
[Conclusions](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/conclusions/), from a university writing
centre, describes a conclusion that "brings the reader full circle": "if you begin by describing a
scenario, you can end with the same scenario".

**The three past tenses, each with a job.**

```text
   past perfect      past continuous          past simple
   (before)          (in progress)            (what happened next)
   ────────┼───────────────┼────────────────────────┼──────────▶
   The bus had       We were sitting at the     The engine
   left late.        back, revising.            started smoking.
```

**The opening.** Put the reader in the scene, and hint that it mattered: *It was the morning of my first
exam, and I had never been late for anything.*

**The events.** In time order. Each paragraph moves the story forward. Use time words: *then*, *after
that*, *a few minutes later*, *in the end*. Hold back the outcome until the turning point.

**The reflection.** Not a moral lesson stuck on the end, but a change you can name: what you did before,
what you do now, or what you understood. It can return to the opening scene.

**No contractions in the narration.** Speech in quotation marks can keep them, because that's how people
spoke. That's this course's choice for formal pages.

**Name the trap.** A story feels finished when the events end. The reader still wants to know why you
told it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the story with no reflection**, as in the story: it ends *And we got
there just in time.* The reader asks *And?*

The second is **the moral stuck on**: *This shows that we should always be kind to strangers.* It's a
general truth, not your change.

The third is **tenses that slip into the present** halfway through: *Then the driver stops and he
says …* The reader loses the time.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers choose a small story for a narrative essay: one morning, one conversation. A big story, a
whole year, has no room for a turning point.

Under pressure, the reflection gets one sentence. Make it a specific change: *Since then, I have asked
for help three times, and nobody has said no.*

A reader remembers the story and quotes the reflection. That's the paragraph that makes it yours.
