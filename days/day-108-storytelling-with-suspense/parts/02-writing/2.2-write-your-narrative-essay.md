## One-line answer

Write a narrative essay of four paragraphs about one small, true story from your life: the opening
scene, two paragraphs of events building to the turning point, and a reflection. Then mark every verb
with its tense, and check that nothing slips into the present outside the reflection. Finally, give it to
a reader and ask: *What happened, and what did it change for the writer?*

## The story

Your nephew rewrote his bus essay for a second competition. This time he planned it on one card: the
opening scene, the steps, the turning point, the change. He wrote it in one go, and then went through it
with three coloured pens, one for each past tense.

He found two sentences that had slipped into the present: *The driver stops. He says get in.* He fixed
them. Then he gave the essay to his grandmother. *You missed the bus,* she said, *and a stranger helped
you, and now you're not afraid to ask.* That was all of it.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **narrative essay**, **turning point**, **reflection**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-a-true-story-that-means-something.md).
- **past continuous**: *was* or *were* + *-ing*, from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/02-writing/2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **past perfect**: *had* + the past participle, from
  [day 104](../../../day-104-gonna-wanna-dunno/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-i-had-known.md).
- **tense marking**: underlining each verb and writing its tense above it.
- **what-and-change test**: a reader says in two sentences what happened in a narrative essay, and what
  it changed for the writer.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a story too big for four paragraphs**: a whole year, a
whole trip. Choose one morning, one evening, one conversation.

The second is **a reflection that's a moral**, not a change. Write what *you* did before and do now.

The third is **direct speech with no quotation marks**, so the reader can't tell what was said from what
was narrated.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers plan a narrative essay backwards: they know the change first, and choose the story that
shows it.

Under pressure, the events take all the space and the reflection is squeezed into a line. Plan it first,
so it's there.

A reader who can say what changed for you has read an essay, not just a story.
