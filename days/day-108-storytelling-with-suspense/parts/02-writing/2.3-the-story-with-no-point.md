## One-line answer

Make a copy of your narrative essay with the point taken out, on purpose: delete the reflection so it
ends when the events end, and change one paragraph of events into the present tense. Give the copy and
your real essay to two readers. Ask each: *Why did the writer tell me this story?* and *When did it
happen: now, or in the past?* The copy's reader should struggle with both. Your real essay's reader
should answer both clearly.

## The story

Your nephew's teacher kept a folder of first drafts. One was about a girl who got lost at a fair. It was
well told, with a crowd and a loudspeaker and a policeman. It ended: *Then my mother found me and we went
home.* Halfway through, it had slipped into the present: *I walk past the stalls. I see a man.*

The teacher gave it to the class with two questions: *Why did she tell us this?* and *Is it happening
now?* Nobody knew the answer to the first. Half the class said *now* to the second.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **narrative essay**, **turning point**, **reflection**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-a-true-story-that-means-something.md).
- **tense marking**, **what-and-change test**: from
  [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-narrative-essay.md).
- **tense slip**: a change of tense in the middle of a story, without a reason.
- **why-and-when test**: a reader says why the writer told the story, and whether it's happening now or
  in the past.

## Why Vaani needs it

You know why the story matters, and when it happened, so neither ever looks missing to you. A reader who
only has the page shows you whether the page says it.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[A short story](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/short-story): use
"the past simple, past continuous and the past perfect".
[Conclusions](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/conclusions/): a conclusion should "help your
readers make the transition back to their daily lives".

**Step one: make copy N.** Copy your essay and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Delete the reflection paragraph. The essay ends when the events end.
2. Change one paragraph of events into the present tense: *walked* → *walk*, *was sitting* → *am
   sitting*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who guesses a moral** for copy N: *Don't get
lost.* That's their idea, not yours. Ask: *Did the writer say that?*

The second is **a present-tense paragraph that reads as a style choice**. Some stories are told in the
present on purpose. In this course's narrative essay, keep the events in the past.

The third, in real writing, is **a reflection that's longer than the story**. The story shows it; the
reflection names it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often write the reflection first, then cut the story to fit it.

Under pressure, a tense slip happens when a story gets exciting: the writer is back in the moment, and
writes as if it's now. Check the most exciting paragraph first.

A reader who can say why you told the story, in words close to yours, has understood the essay.
