## One-line answer

A **phrasal verb** is a verb plus one or two small words, such as *put off*, *get over* or *run out of*,
and together they mean something new. *Put off* means *delay*. *Get over* means *recover from*. *Run out
of* means *use all of something*. In speech, the hard part is where *it*, *him* or *them* goes. With *put
off*, it goes in the middle: *put it off*. With *get over*, it goes after: *get over it*. With *run out
of*, it goes after both small words: *run out of it*.

## The story

Your flatmate comes home and finds the kitchen in a mess.

> We run out milk. And the meeting with the landlord, I put off it. Sorry, I have cold, I can't get it
> over.

You understand about half. Milk, something. The landlord, something. A cold. You ask him to say it again,
slowly, and it takes three tries.

The next week, he's practised:

> We've run out of milk. And I've put off the meeting with the landlord. I put it off till Friday. Sorry,
> I've still got this cold. I can't get over it.

Every sentence lands the first time.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **idiom**, **label (in a dictionary)**: a group of words with a meaning you can't work out from the
  single words, and the small word beside a meaning saying where it belongs, from
  [day 102](../../../day-102-idioms-you-hear/parts/01-speaking/1.1-heard-checked-then-said.md).
- **chunk you own**: a group of English words you can say without thinking, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/01-speaking/1.1-start-from-english-you-own.md).
- **phrasal verb**: a verb plus one or two small words that together mean something new.
- **particle**: the small word in a phrasal verb, such as *off*, *over*, *out*, *of*.
- **object**: the person or thing the verb acts on: *the meeting* in *put off the meeting*.
- **pronoun**: a short word that stands for a noun: *it*, *him*, *her*, *them*.

| Phrasal verb | Meaning on the page | With a pronoun |
| --- | --- | --- |
| put off | "To delay (a task, event, etc.)." | *put it off*, *put him off* |
| get over | "To recover (from)." / "To overcome." | *get over it* |
| run out of | used up, with nothing left | *run out of it* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Real conversation is full of phrasal verbs, and the phase 6 recorded conversation will be too. You'll
hear them before you say them, and when you say them, the pronoun in the wrong place is what a listener
notices.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Phrasal verbs](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/phrasal-verbs) says:
"Phrasal verbs are made up of a verb and a particle or, sometimes, two particles. The particle often
changes the meaning of the verb." For separable ones, "the verb and particle can be apart or together",
but they "must be separated when you use a personal pronoun", with "They've called it off". "Some phrasal
verbs cannot be separated", with "Who looks after her when you're at work?" And: "Phrasal verbs with two
particles are also inseparable. Even if you use a personal pronoun, you put it after the particles."
[put off](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/put_off) gives "To delay (a task, event, etc.)", with "The storm
put off the game by a week.", "The storm put the game off by a week." and "I'll have to put him off."
[get over](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/get_over) gives "To recover (from)", with "I'm having problems
getting over a bad cold.", and "To overcome", with "I'm trying to get over my fear of flying."
[run out of](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/run_out_of) gives "To use up or consume all", with "Get some
more beer out of the fridge before we run out."

**Put off: the pronoun goes in the middle.** A noun can go after or in the middle; a pronoun must go in
the middle.

> I put off the meeting. / I put the meeting off.
> I put it off.

**Get over: the object goes after.** Every example on the page puts it after *over*.

> I can't get over this cold.
> I can't get over it.

**Run out of: after both particles.** Two particles stay together, and the pronoun follows them.

> We've run out of milk.
> We've run out of it.

**Say the particle.** *Off*, *over*, *out of* carry the meaning. If you swallow them, the listener hears
only *put*, *get*, *run*, which mean something else.

**Name the trap.** Your first language may not have phrasal verbs, so you'll learn each as a verb and
forget the particle, or put the pronoun where your language would put it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the pronoun after a separable verb**:

> I put off it.

The listener hears the right words in the wrong order, and has to rebuild the sentence.

The second is **a missing particle**:

> We run out milk.

Without *of*, the listener has to guess how *milk* joins the sentence.

The third is **splitting one that doesn't split**: *I can't get it over* for *I can't get over it*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

The reference page says phrasal verbs "are very common in English, especially in more informal
contexts". In a kitchen, *put off* sounds natural; *postpone* sounds like a letter from an office.

Under pressure, the pronoun goes wrong first. If you're not sure, use the noun: *put off the meeting*
works both ways.

A native listener understands a wrong pronoun position, but notices it. A missing particle can change
what they understand.
