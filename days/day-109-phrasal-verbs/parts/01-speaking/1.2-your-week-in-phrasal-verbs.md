## One-line answer

Add three phrasal verbs of your own, ones you've heard real people use, to the three from part 1.1. Check
each on a dictionary page: its meaning, and whether a pronoun goes in the middle or after. Then record a
short account of your real week using all six, each once with a noun and once with a pronoun. Finally,

## The story

Your flatmate started a list on the fridge: phrasal verbs he'd heard at work that week. *Sort out*, from
his manager. *Look after*, from a colleague with a new baby. *Turn down*, from a friend who'd refused a
job. For each, he looked up whether *it* went in the middle or at the end, and wrote it beside the verb.

On Sunday, he told you about his week using all of them. You didn't have to ask him to repeat anything.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **phrasal verb**, **particle**, **object**, **pronoun**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-put-off-get-over-run-out-of.md).
- **separable**: a phrasal verb whose object can go between the verb and the particle, and whose pronoun
  must: *put it off*.
- **inseparable**: a phrasal verb whose object always goes after the particle or particles: *get over
  it*, *run out of it*.
- **pronoun check**: writing down each sentence where you used a pronoun with a phrasal verb, and
  checking its position against the dictionary page.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **three new verbs from a list**, not ones you've heard.
Choose from real speech around you.

The second is **guessing separable or inseparable** without checking the examples. Some look alike and
behave differently.

The third is **pronoun sentences that avoid the pronoun**, repeating the noun instead. Use *it*, *him*,
*them*: that's where the rule lives.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers learn phrasal verbs the way they learn idioms, as chunks heard many times. *Put it off*
is one chunk, not three words in an order.

Under pressure, stick to the verbs you've checked. A new phrasal verb in a nervous moment is where the
pronoun goes wrong.

A native listener who hears *sort it out* and *look after her* hears everyday English, spoken easily.
