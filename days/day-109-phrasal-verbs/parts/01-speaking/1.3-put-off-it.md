## One-line answer

Record four sentences about your week twice. In take W, break the phrasal verbs on purpose: one pronoun
after a separable verb (*put off it*), one inseparable verb split (*get it over*), and one particle
dropped (*run out milk*). In take R, say them right. Play each to a different listener, and ask them to
repeat each sentence back to you exactly, then say which ones sounded odd. W's listener should stumble
or name odd sentences. R's listener should repeat each one easily and name none.

## The story

Your flatmate's first message about the kitchen, *We run out milk. I put off it*, didn't just confuse
you. When you told a friend about it later, you couldn't remember what he'd said, only that it was
strange. The second version, a week later, you could repeat word for word.

That's the difference a listener feels: a sentence in the right order goes straight into memory. A
sentence in the wrong order has to be rebuilt first.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a listener who corrects as they repeat**, turning *put
off it* into *put it off* without noticing. That's a stumble; count it.

The second is **a take W said so hesitantly that it sounds wrong for another reason**. Say W at normal
speed, confidently.

The third, in real life, is **a new phrasal verb you haven't checked**, where you guess the pronoun's
place. Use the noun until you've looked it up.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers never think about separable and inseparable. They've heard *put it off* so often that
*put off it* sounds wrong to them. That's where your ear is going.

Under pressure, fall back on the noun: *put off the meeting*, *get over the cold*. It's always in the
right place.

A native listener rarely corrects a phrasal verb. They just take a moment longer to understand, and in a
fast conversation, that moment matters.
