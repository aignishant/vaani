## One-line answer

A **phrasal verb** is a verb plus one or two small words, such as *put off* or *get over*. They're right
in a message to a friend. In a formal email, a letter or an essay, a **single verb** with the same
meaning often reads better: *postpone* for *put off*, *recover from* for *get over*. On paper, the
choice is visible: a formal page full of phrasal verbs reads like speech written down. And when you do
write a phrasal verb, the pronoun's place is visible too: *put it off*, never *put off it*.

## The story

Your friend was applying for a college course, and she emailed the admissions office to ask for a later
interview date:

> Hi, I need to put off my interview because I'm still getting over a fever. Also I ran out of time to
> send the form, can I send it in next week?

Nothing in it was wrong. But the reply came from a senior person, and began *Dear Ms Rao*, and used
*postpone* and *recover* and *submit*. She felt her own email looked like a text message beside it.

She wrote her next email to the office like this:

> I am writing to ask whether I may postpone my interview, as I am still recovering from a fever. I have
> also not yet been able to submit the form. Would it be possible to submit it next week?

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **formal**, **informal**, **register**: careful writing for someone you don't know well, relaxed
  writing for friends and family, and which of the two a piece uses, from
  [day 81](../../../day-81-formal-and-informal/parts/02-writing/2.1-five-switches.md).
- **formal email**: an email with a subject line, a greeting, short paragraphs and a sign-off, from
  [day 82](../../../day-82-introducing-yourself-at-work/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-formal-frame-top-to-bottom.md).
- **phrasal verb**: a verb plus one or two small words that together mean something new.
- **particle**: the small word in a phrasal verb, such as *off*, *over*, *out*, *of*.
- **single verb**: one word with the same meaning as a phrasal verb, such as *postpone* for *put off*.
- **pronoun**: a short word that stands for a noun: *it*, *him*, *them*.

| Phrasal verb | Single verb, or formal phrase | Checked on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *put off* (a meeting) | *postpone* | [postpone](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/postpone): "To delay or put off an event, appointment, etc." |
| *get over* (an illness) | *recover from* | [recover](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/recover): "To get better, to regain health or prosperity." |
| *run out of* (time, money) | *have no … left*, *have used all of* | [run out of](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/run_out_of): "To use up or consume all" |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 writing portfolio holds formal pieces and informal ones. The choice of verb is one of the
quickest ways a reader decides which kind they're reading. Get it right, and the register holds.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Phrasal verbs](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/phrasal-verbs) says:
"Phrasal verbs are very common in English, especially in more informal contexts." It also gives the
pronoun rule: separable verbs "must be separated when you use a personal pronoun", with "They've called it
off", and "Phrasal verbs with two particles are also inseparable. Even if you use a personal pronoun, you
put it after the particles."
[postpone](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/postpone) gives "To delay or put off an event, appointment,
etc." and lists *put off* among its synonyms.
[put off](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/put_off) gives "To delay (a task, event, etc.)".
[get over](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/get_over) gives "To recover (from)".
[recover](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/recover) gives "To get better, to regain health or prosperity."

**Match the verb to the page.**

| Page | Verb choice |
| --- | --- |
| a message to a friend, a note on the fridge | phrasal verbs: *put off*, *get over*, *run out of* |
| a formal email, a letter, an essay, a report | a single verb where one fits: *postpone*, *recover from* |

**Change the whole sentence, not just the verb.** A single verb often brings a more formal sentence with
it: *I need to put off my interview* becomes *I am writing to ask whether I may postpone my interview.*

**Don't force it.** Some phrasal verbs have no plain single verb, or the single verb sounds stiff even in
a formal email. If you can't find one on a dictionary page, keep the phrasal verb and make the rest of
the sentence formal.

**The pronoun on the page.** When you write a phrasal verb with *it*, *him* or *them*, the pronoun's
place is visible: *put it off*, *get over it*, *run out of it*.

**Name the trap.** Phrasal verbs are the verbs you hear most, so they're the ones that come to your pen
first, even in a formal email.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the formal email that talks**, as in the story: *I need to put off my
interview.* Nothing's wrong, and it reads like a text message.

The second is **the single verb in a friend's message**:

> Can we postpone dinner? I am recovering from a cold.

The friend wonders why you're writing like an office.

The third is **the pronoun in the wrong place on paper**: *I will have to put off it.* A reader sees it at
once.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers switch between the two without thinking, and a single email can have both: a formal body
with one friendly phrasal verb in the last line.

Under pressure, in a hurry, you'll write the phrasal verb. In a formal piece, read it once looking only
at the verbs.

A formal reader rarely objects to one phrasal verb. A page of them tells them the writer didn't change
register.
