## One-line answer

Make a copy of your formal email with every single verb swapped back to a phrasal verb, on purpose, and
one pronoun in the wrong place. Give the copy and your real email to two readers. Each scores it from one
(like a text to a friend) to five (like a letter from an office), and circles any word that sounds like
talk. The copy should score lower and have circles. Your real email should score four or five, with no
circles.

## The story

Your friend's first email to the admissions office, the one with *put off* and *getting over* and *ran
out of*, stayed in her sent folder. Months later, she showed it to a cousin who worked in an office, next
to the second one, and asked which one she'd rather receive.

Her cousin pointed at the first. *This one's friendly. But if I got fifty of these, I'd answer the second
one first. It looks like it knows where it's going.*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

A phrasal verb doesn't look informal to you, because you hear it everywhere. A reader who scores the page
shows you how much the verbs alone shift the register.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Phrasal verbs](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/phrasal-verbs): "Phrasal
verbs are very common in English, especially in more informal contexts."; separable verbs "must be
separated when you use a personal pronoun".
[postpone](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/postpone): "To delay or put off an event, appointment, etc."

**Step one: make copy T.** Copy your email O and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Swap every single verb back to a phrasal verb: *postpone* → *put off*; *recover from* → *get over*;
   and any others you changed in [part 2.2](2.2-one-message-written-twice.md), back to what you had
   first.
2. Put one pronoun in the wrong place: *put off it*.

Change nothing else.

**Step two: the formality score.** Give T to one reader and R to a different reader. Ask each:

> From one (a text to a friend) to five (a letter from an office), how formal is this? Circle any word

**Step three: the repair.** If R went red, look at the circled word. If it's a phrasal verb, find its

R to a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a copy T with other changes too**. Change only the verbs
and one pronoun, so you see what the verbs alone do.

The second is **a reader who scores both the same**, because the greeting and sign-off are formal. Ask
them to look only at the middle paragraph.

The third, in real writing, is **the phrasal verb in the subject line**: *Putting off my interview*. The
subject line is the first thing a formal reader sees.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers check the verbs of a formal email last, after the content is right. It's a quick read,
and it catches most of the slips.

Under pressure, the phrasal verb is the one that arrives. Leave it in the draft, and swap it on the read
back.

A formal reader won't write back about your verbs. They'll decide how seriously to take the email, and
the verbs are part of that decision.
