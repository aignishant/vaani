## One-line answer

What asks about a thing, where asks about a place, who asks about a person, and a question that starts with one of them goes down at the end, not up.

## The story

You have moved to a new part of the city and you need the chemist. You stop a man and say,
"Chemist?" with your voice going up. He looks at you, unsure if you are asking him or telling
him. You try again: "Where is the chemist?" and your voice comes down at the end like a hand
pointing. He turns and points too: "Second left, next to the bank." The word where did the
asking, and the falling voice told him you were done.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **question word** — a word that starts a question and says what kind of answer you want.
- **tune** — the way your voice goes up and down in a sentence.
- **fall** — go down.
- **chemist** — the shop that sells medicine.
- **point** — show a direction with your hand or your voice.

A **question** is a sentence that asks for something. A **question word** at the front tells
the listener what kind of answer you want. Three of them cover most of what a new person in a
new place needs:

- **What** asks about a thing: What is this?
- **Where** asks about a place: Where is the bus stop?
- **Who** asks about a person: Who is the owner?

The **tune** is the second half of the rule. A question that starts with what, where or who
goes **down** at the end. The voice falls on the last word, like a hand coming down to point.
That fall is what tells the listener "I have asked; now it is your turn."

## The rule

The three words, the shape, and a model for each. The verb is is, because you are asking about
one thing, one place, one person:

| Word | Asks about | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What | a thing | What + is + this / that / the name of …? |
| Where | a place | Where + is + the + place? |
| Who | a person | Who + is + the + person? |

> What is this?
> Where is the chemist?
> Who is the owner?

Say each one at the mirror with your hand up on the first word and coming down on the last.
The hand and the voice fall together. That is the tune.

The three words were confirmed as question words on 2026-09-22 at
[Questions and negatives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/questions-negatives),
which says: "Wh-questions are questions which start with a word like what, when, where, which,
who, whose, why and how." The word order, question word first and then the verb, was confirmed
at [Question forms](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/question-forms):
"If there is a question word (why, what, where, how, etc.), it goes before the verb."

In fast speech, "what is" becomes "what's" and "where is" becomes "where's". Both are fine.
Say the full form today, so the tune has room.

The trap: wh at the start of what, where and who is not the
[w with round lips from day 4](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/01-speaking/1.2-w-round-lips-no-teeth.md)
for all three. What and where start with /w/, lips round, no teeth. Who starts with /h/, a
breath, and the w is silent. Say "hoo", not "woo".

## When it breaks

You ask with the tune going up:

> Where is the chemist? ↗

The listener hears surprise, or a question about your question, and may answer "Yes?" The
fix: let the voice fall on chemist, like a hand coming down.

You leave the question word out and use only the tune:

> The chemist? ↗

Now the listener does not know if you want the place, the person or the opening time. The
fix: "Where is the chemist?"

You say who with a w:

> Woo is the owner?

The listener hears a word that is not English. The fix: breathe the h. "Hoo."

If, on playback, your three questions rise at the end, the check is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers ask short: "Where's the chemist?" "What's this?" "Who's in charge?" The
question word and the fall are still there; only is has shrunk. Under pressure, lost, late, in
noise, they add "sorry" or "excuse me" at the front, and that word goes up a little before the
question comes down. "Excuse me, where's the chemist?"

A native listener answers the question word before anything else. Ask where and they point.
Ask what and they name. Ask who and they look for the person. Get the word right and the rest
of the sentence can be a little wrong; they will still answer.
