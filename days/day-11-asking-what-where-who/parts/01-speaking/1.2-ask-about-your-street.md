## One-line answer

Take the six things you need to know about where you live, turn each into a what, where or who question with the falling tune, and say them to the mirror and then to a real person.

## The story

Second week in the new area. You have a list in your head: where the water comes on, who
collects the rubbish, what the building is called, where the nearest bus stop is. You have
been guessing at all of them. Today you stop the neighbour on the ground floor and ask, one
at a time, with the word first and the voice falling. He answers all four. He also tells you
where the good tea is, because a person who asks well gets more than they asked for.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **actually** — really; in real life.
- **rubbish** — the waste you throw out.
- **collect** — come and take away.
- **nearest** — closest to you.
- **guess** — decide without knowing.

In [part 1.1](1.1-what-where-who.md) you learned three question words and the falling tune.
Now you use them on your own street. Not on a list from a book, on the things you have actually
been guessing about since you arrived. A question you need is a question you remember.

The shape does not change. Question word, then is, then the thing, the place or the person.
Voice falls on the last word.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every speaking day from here has a question in it, and by the
[day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) you should be able to ask
one back after your introduction: "And where are you from?" A person who only tells is a
recording. A person who asks is in a conversation.

## The rule

Write six things you do not know about where you live. Sort them into three groups:

- a **thing** you do not know the name of → What
- a **place** you do not know → Where
- a **person** you do not know → Who

Turn each into a question. The shape for one thing or person; the model is for a street that
is not yours:

> What is the name of this building?
> Where is the nearest bus stop?
> Who is the landlord?

For more than one thing, place or person, is becomes are:

> Where are the shops?

Say each question at the mirror three times with the hand falling on the last word. Then, if
you can, ask at least one of them to a real person today, and listen to the answer. The answer
is the point.

The question shape was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Question forms](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/question-forms),
which gives "Why are you late?" as the model for a question word before the verb, and at
[Questions and negatives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/questions-negatives),
which gives "Where are they working?" as a wh-question.

Two politeness words that go in front of any of these and cost nothing: "Excuse me," before a
stranger, "Sorry," before a neighbour you already know. They rise a little; then the question
falls.

> Excuse me, where is the nearest bus stop?

The trap: asking two questions in one breath because you are nervous. "Where is the bus stop
and what time is the bus?" The listener answers the second and forgets the first. One question,
wait for the answer, then the next.

## When it breaks

You ask the question with the place first and the word last:

> The bus stop is where?

The listener understands, but it sounds like you are testing them. In English the question
word comes first. The fix: "Where is the bus stop?"

You forget the verb:

> Where the bus stop?

Common for beginners whose first language does not need is here. The listener fills it in, but
it marks you as a beginner before you have said anything else. The fix: put is back.

If, on playback, any of your six questions has the question word anywhere but first, or is
missing is or are, that question is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers shorten to "Where's the bus stop?" and often drop the noun when pointing:
"What's that?" They still never move the question word from the front. Under pressure, they
add "sorry" more than once and the questions get shorter, not longer.

A native listener will answer a question with the word first even if the rest is broken.
"Where … bus?" with a falling tune gets a pointed finger. A perfect sentence with the word at
the end gets a puzzled pause. The word at the front is the part that must be right.
