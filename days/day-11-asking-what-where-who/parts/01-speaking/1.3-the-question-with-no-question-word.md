## One-line answer

Ask three questions with the word left out and the tune rising, on purpose, play them back as the stranger, notice what you would answer, then ask them right beside them.

## The story

You test it on the tea seller. You point at a packet and say "This?" with your voice going up.
He says "Yes." You wanted the name. You say "Bus stop?" going up. He says "Yes, yes," and waves
at the whole road. You wanted a direction. Then you say "What is this?" and "Where is the bus
stop?" with the voice falling, and you get a name and a pointed finger. Same man, same shop.
The word and the fall were the whole difference.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong versions are deliberate. They rest on what beginners
commonly do, not on a mistake found in your own recordings.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **tune alone** — using only the rise of the voice to ask, with no question word.
- **come back** — the answer you get.
- **repair** — a small correction that helps someone understand, from
  [day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.3-change-one-sound.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the three words from [part 1.1](1.1-what-where-who.md) and your six real questions
from [part 1.2](1.2-ask-about-your-street.md). Now you break them the way beginners really do
when they are shy: the question word goes, the verb goes, and a rising tune on one noun does
all the work. You listen as the stranger and notice what you would say back. It is almost
always "Yes" or a puzzled look. Then you record the repair beside it.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), and in every real
conversation after it, a question that gets "Yes" when you wanted a name is a conversation
that stalls. A learner who has heard their own one-word question from the outside puts the
word back before the stranger has to guess.

## The rule

Three rows. Recorder on. Wrong first, rising; then right, falling. A gap between.

**Row 1 — a thing.**

> This? ↗ — What is this? ↘

**Row 2 — a place.**

> Bus stop? ↗ — Where is the bus stop? ↘

**Row 3 — a person.**

> Owner? ↗ — Who is the owner? ↘

Play each row. Be the tea seller. After the wrong version, say out loud the first thing you
would answer. If it is "Yes", the wrong version worked as a wrong version. After the right
version, say what you would answer. If it is a name, a direction, a person, the right version
worked.

Then the repair in one breath, as it happens when you hear "Yes" and realise:

> Bus stop? — Sorry: where is the bus stop?

The question-word rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Question forms](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/question-forms):
"If there is a question word (why, what, where, how, etc.), it goes before the verb."

## When it breaks

The break this part is about: you answer your own wrong version with the right information.

> Bus stop? ↗ → "Second left."

You knew what you meant. The tea seller did not. Play it again and answer only what was asked.
One rising noun asks "is it a bus stop?" and the honest answer is "Yes." If you cannot make
yourself answer "Yes", your wrong version still had a question word hiding in your face or
your hand. Record it flat, no pointing.

A second break: the repair adds the word but keeps the rising tune.

> Sorry: where is the bus stop? ↗

The word is back, and the tune still says "I'm not sure I'm allowed to ask." The fix: the
voice falls on stop.

If you cannot tell the wrong version from the right one on playback by what you would answer,
the check is RED.

## In real life

Everyone drops to one rising noun when shy, tired or in a hurry, and fluent speakers do it too
with people they know: "Tea?" "Coffee?" It works when the answer is yes or no. It fails when
you need a name, a place or a person, and beginners use it exactly then, because a question
word feels like a risk. It is not. It is the part that gets you the answer.

A native listener will answer a rising noun with "Yes" every time, and mean no harm by it. They
will answer a falling what, where or who with the thing you actually wanted. The repair,
"Sorry, where is…", takes one breath and turns the "Yes" into a direction.
