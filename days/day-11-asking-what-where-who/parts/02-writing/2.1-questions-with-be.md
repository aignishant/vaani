## One-line answer

To ask a question with am, is or are, put the verb in front of the person or thing and end with a question mark instead of a full stop.

## The story

You are messaging a landlord about a room you saw online. You write: "The room is free." He
writes back: "Yes." You wait. Nothing else comes. You meant to ask. You wrote a sentence that
told him, and he agreed with it. You try again: "Is the room free?" and this time he sends the
rent, the deposit and a time to visit. Two words swapped and one mark changed, and a statement
became a question.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **statement** — a sentence that tells something.
- **question** — a sentence that asks something.
- **subject** — the person or thing a sentence is about; it usually comes first.
- **swap** — change places.
- **landlord** — the person who owns the room or house you rent.

A **statement** tells. A **question** asks. On paper they can hold the same words, and the
reader knows which is which from two things: the **order** of the words, and the mark at the
end.

The **subject** is the person or thing the sentence is about. In "The room is free", the
subject is the room. In a statement, the subject comes first and the
[verb](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md), here is, comes
after it. In a question with am, is or are, the verb **jumps in front** of the subject. Is the
room free? And the sentence ends with a **question mark**, ?, not a
[full stop](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md).

## Why Vaani needs it

Every message you write to a person you do not know, a landlord, an office, a shop, is mostly
questions. On [day 12](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed)
you write the negative, not, and it sits in the same place the verb sits today. Get the jump
right today and tomorrow's rule is one word longer.

## The rule

The swap, shown as a picture:

```text
Statement:   The room   is    free.
             subject    verb

Question:    Is   the room   free?
             verb  subject
```

The verb, am, is or are, moves to the front. Everything else stays in its place. The full stop
becomes a question mark.

| Statement | Question |
| --- | --- |
| The room is free. | Is the room free? |
| You are the owner. | Are you the owner? |
| The bills are included. | Are the bills included? |
| I am late. | Am I late? |

> Is the room free?
> Are you the owner?
> Are the bills included?

The rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Question forms](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/question-forms):
"To make questions, we often put the verb before the subject. This is called inversion", with
the example "Am I late?" The forms of be in questions, "Am I? Are you? Is he/she/it?", were
seen at [The verb 'be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/verb-be),
opened the same day.

The check for every question you write: put a finger on the first word. Is it am, is or are?
Put a finger on the last mark. Is it a question mark? Both yes, and the reader sees a question
before they read a word of it.

The trap: a question mark alone does not make a question. "The room is free?" with a question
mark is what many beginners write, because in speech the rising voice does the asking. On
paper there is no voice. The reader sees a statement with a strange mark, and answers "Yes" to
the statement.

## When it breaks

You keep the statement order and add a question mark:

> The room is free?

The landlord reads a statement and agrees with it. The fix: swap. "Is the room free?"

You swap the verb but keep the full stop:

> Is the room free.

The reader starts to read a question and hits a full stop. The sentence feels unfinished. The
fix: the question mark.

You move the wrong word:

> Room is the free?

Only the verb jumps. The subject and everything after it stay where they were. The fix: "Is the
room free?"

If a question you wrote starts with anything but am, is or are, or ends with anything but a
question mark, that question is RED.

## In real life

In quick chat messages, fluent writers do sometimes write "The room's still free?" and rely on
the reader knowing them. With a stranger, they swap. "Is the room still free?" It takes no
longer and it cannot be read as a statement. An office, a landlord or a form reads for the
first word, and if the first word is Is or Are, they know a question is coming and read it as
one.

A friend reading "The room is free?" answers your question anyway. A landlord with ten
messages answers the statement, "Yes", and moves on. The swap is what gets you the rent and the
visit.
