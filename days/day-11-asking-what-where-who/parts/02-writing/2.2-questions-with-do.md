## One-line answer

When the verb is not am, is or are, a question starts with do, or does for he, she and it, and the main verb stays in its plain form after the subject.

## The story

The landlord has answered your first questions. Now you need to know other things: does the
room have a window, do the neighbours make noise, does he allow cooking. You write "The room
has a window?" and he answers "Yes" again, to the statement. There is no am, is or are to jump.
So English brings in a helper: "Does the room have a window?" He sends a photo of the window.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **helper word** — a small word added to make a question, with no meaning of its own here.
- **main verb** — the verb that says what happens: have, allow, make, live.
- **plain form** — the verb with no -s and no change: have, not has.
- **allow** — let someone do something.
- **neighbours** — the people who live next to you.

In [part 2.1](2.1-questions-with-be.md) the verb jumped in front of the
[subject](2.1-questions-with-be.md). But only am, is and are can jump. Every other
[verb](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md), have, live, work,
allow, make, stays where it is. To ask, English puts a **helper word** at the front instead:
**do**, or **does** when the subject is he, she, it or one name.

The helper takes the job of showing the person. So the main verb goes back to its **plain
form**. Has becomes have. Lives becomes live. Does the room have a window, not does the room
has.

## Why Vaani needs it

Most of the questions you will write in real life are not about what something is; they are
about what it does or has. Does the shop open on Sunday? Do you deliver? On
[day 12](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed), not uses the
same helper: don't and doesn't. Today's do is tomorrow's don't.

## The rule

The shape, as a picture:

```text
Statement:   The room        has     a window.
             subject         verb

Question:    Does   the room   have    a window?
             helper subject    plain verb
```

| Subject | Helper | Statement | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I, you, we, they | do | You allow cooking. | Do you allow cooking? |
| he, she, it, one name | does | The room has a window. | Does the room have a window? |
| more than one | do | The neighbours make noise. | Do the neighbours make noise? |

> Does the room have a window?
> Do you allow cooking?
> Do the neighbours make noise?

The rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Question forms](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/question-forms):
"For other verbs in the present simple, we use the auxiliary verb do/does in the question",
with the example "Do you work at home?" and at
[Questions and negatives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/questions-negatives):
"For all verbs except be and have, we use do/does or did to make Yes/No questions", with "Does
he work hard?" The plain form after does, "Does he speak English?", was seen at
[Present simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple),
opened the same day.

Two fingers for every question: one on the first word, do or does, matching the subject; one
on the main verb, which must be plain, no -s.

The trap: have. In [day 9](../../../day-09-introducing-yourself/parts/02-writing/2.1-have-and-has.md)
you learned has for he, she, it. In a question with does, that -s moves into does and has
becomes have. Does the room have. Not does the room has. Not has the room.

## When it breaks

You leave the -s on the main verb:

> Does the room has a window?

Two words now show the person, does and has. The reader trips. The fix: does the room have.

You use do for one thing:

> Do the room have a window?

The helper does not match the subject. The fix: does, for one room.

You forget the helper and swap the main verb instead:

> Has the room a window?

Old-fashioned, and today's readers stop on it. Only am, is and are jump. The fix: "Does the
room have a window?"

If a question you wrote has an -s on the main verb after do or does, or do where does belongs,
that question is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers never think about do; it appears by habit. What they think about is which
questions to ask. In a message to a landlord, an office or a shop, they front-load the
practical ones: "Do you deliver? Does the price include tax?" Under pressure, writing fast,
even fluent writers slip an -s back onto the main verb, and a careful one reads back and takes
it off.

A friend forgives "Does the room has a window?" A landlord reads it, understands it, and files
you as a beginner before he has seen you. "Does the room have a window?" files you as someone
who asks clear questions, and clear questions get clear answers.
