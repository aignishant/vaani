# The vowel that isn't there

`PR-05` · Day 11

## The one-line answer

English ties three and four consonants into one tight knot with no vowel anywhere inside it, and
when your mouth loosens that knot by slipping a small vowel in, the word grows a beat it should
not have — which is a rhythm error, not a pronunciation one.

## The model

Say these out loud, now, one at a time.

> asked
> texts
> sixth
> strengths
> clothes

Somewhere in there your mouth almost certainly did something helpful and wrong. It put a tiny
vowel between two of the consonants. *Ask-ed. Tex-its. Six-ith. Strength-is.* Nobody taught you
that and you did not decide to do it. The knot was tight, and putting slack in it made the word
easier to get out.

Here is what it costs. **Say *asked* and tap the table once.** That works. Now say *ask-ed* and
try to tap once. You can't — your hand wants a second tap. That extra tap is the entire problem.
Every individual sound in *ask-ed* is a correct English sound, correctly made. The word is still
wrong, because it is now the wrong length.

That is worth sitting with. You can pronounce every sound in a word perfectly and still not be
understood, if the number of beats is wrong.

**Two tests, both of which work in your own mouth.**

*The chin test.* Put your hand flat under your jaw. Say *asked*. A vowel is a jaw dropping — so
if your jaw moves in the middle of the word, a vowel got in. Through a knot of consonants your
jaw should stay almost completely still.

*The tap test.* One tap per beat. *Asked* — one. *Texts* — one. *Sixth* — one. *Strengths* — one.
*Clothes* — one. If any of them needed two, you know exactly which one to work on.

**The fix is not speed.** Going faster does not remove the vowel, it just hides it. The fix is
that your jaw stays put and only your tongue and lips move. The consonants in a knot happen at
different places in your mouth, and you can change place without opening anything. Pull the knot
tight and move through it.

**And if the knot genuinely beats you, drop — never add.** Record *asked* two ways: once with the
middle consonant simply missing, and once with a vowel wedged in. Tap along to both. The dropped
version is still one beat. The wedged version is two. Keep the one that keeps the beat. Dropping
is a knot pulled so tight something disappeared inside it; adding is a knot coming undone, and
only one of those still sounds like the word.

**Today's phrases** — every one of them has a knot in it. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md`
tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I asked around. | Saying you checked with several people, not just one |
| I've asked twice already. | Chasing something without sounding rude about it |
| I've sent you a few texts. | Explaining that you already tried to reach someone |
| The texts didn't go through. | Excusing a silence that wasn't your fault |
| It's the sixth one this week. | Flagging that something keeps happening |
| That's not one of my strengths. | Turning something down honestly |
| I just threw some clothes on. | Explaining why you turned up looking unprepared |
| Next time, definitely. | Saying no now without closing the door |

## Say it now

Hand under your chin for all of this. Say each line three times, filling in your own content.
Nothing written down.

1. *I asked around about {{…}}, and nobody knew.*
2. *I've asked twice already, and I've sent you a few texts.*
3. *The texts didn't go through — it's the sixth one this week.*
4. *That's not one of my strengths, but {{…}}.*
5. *I just threw some clothes on. Next time, definitely.*

Then join all five into one continuous stretch, twice, without stopping between them. On the
second run, keep your hand under your chin the whole way. Every jaw drop you feel inside a word
is a vowel you did not mean to say.

## The deliberate failure

Record all eight of today's phrases twice through.

First pass: **wedge a vowel in on purpose.** Every place two consonants meet, force them apart.
*I ask-ed a-round. I've sent you a few tex-its. It's the six-ith one this week.* Go as slowly as
you like — slower makes it easier to do it wrong.

Second pass: the same eight, jaw still, knots pulled tight.

**What breaking sounds like:** the broken phrases are noticeably longer, and the words inside them
have turned into words you don't recognise. Tap along to *asked* in the first pass and you get
two taps. Then notice the thing that matters most: in the broken pass you did not mispronounce a
single sound. Every sound was a real English sound in the right order. The only thing wrong was
the count — and it was enough.

When the second pass restores it, listen for what came back. Not clarity. **Length.** The phrase
got shorter without getting faster, and that is what a knot pulled tight does.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are on a call with bad audio. You say *I asked them last week* and the person says "sorry?"
So you repeat it louder and slower — and it goes worse, not better, because the slower you go the
more room the extra vowel has to sit in. By the third attempt you are shouting a two-beat word at
someone who is listening for a one-beat one.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — I did ask. Twice, actually.

Rebuild the sentence so the knot isn't in it. *Asked* is a knot; *did ask* is two easy words. That
is not cheating or dodging — choosing the shape your mouth can actually deliver, in the moment,
is one of the most useful things a speaker does, and nobody hearing it knows you did anything at
all.
