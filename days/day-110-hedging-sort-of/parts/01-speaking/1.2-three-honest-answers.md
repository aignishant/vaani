## One-line answer

Ask a real person for three questions about things they know you have an opinion on, but can't be
fully sure about. Before each answer, decide silently which part you're sure of and which part you're
not. Record each answer: the plain part plainly, one hedge on the unsure part, and *it depends on …*
where it fits. Then play them back and, for each answer, write the plain part and the hedged part in two
columns.

## The story

Your cousin liked your answer about the shop so much that she started asking you everything. *Should I
open on Sundays? Should I hire a helper? Should I put up a sign in English?*

Before each answer you stopped and asked yourself one question: *What am I sure of here?* Sundays: sure
people shop then, unsure she'd have the energy. A helper: sure it'd help, unsure she could afford one.
You said the sure part first, every time, and hedged only the rest. She wrote your answers down.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **hedge**, **the unsure part**, **the plain part**, **depends on**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-soften-the-part-you-are-unsure-of.md).
- **sure-or-unsure split**: before answering, deciding silently which part of your answer you're sure
  of and which you're not.
- **two-column check**: writing each answer's plain part and hedged part in two columns, to see where
  your hedges went.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 conversation won't give you time to plan. The sure-or-unsure split takes a second and puts
your hedges in the right place without planning.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an empty plain column**: every part hedged. Find one
thing you're sure of and say it without softening.

The second is **an empty hedge column**: every part plain. If you really are sure of everything, choose a
harder question.

The third is **three hedges in the hedged part**. One is enough.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers make the sure-or-unsure split without thinking. You're training it to be automatic.

Under pressure, in a meeting, a manager may ask *Are you sure?* A plain part and one hedged part lets you
answer: *I'm sure about the cost. The timing depends on the supplier.*

A native listener who can repeat back what you were sure of has understood you exactly.
