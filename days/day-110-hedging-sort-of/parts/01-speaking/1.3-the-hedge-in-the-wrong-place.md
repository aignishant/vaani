## One-line answer

Take one of your three answers and record it twice. In take X, move the hedges on purpose: soften the
part you're sure of, say the unsure part as if certain, and add an *it depends* with no *on*. In take R,
say it as you did in part 1.2. Play each to a different listener, and ask: *Which part was the speaker
sure of? What does it depend on?* X's listener should name the wrong part or not know. R's listener
should name your plain part and your *depends on*.

## The story

Your cousin asked a neighbour's advice about the helper, too. The neighbour said:

> A helper would sort of be useful, maybe. But you definitely can't afford one. It depends.

In fact, the neighbour was sure a helper would be useful, and had no idea what your cousin could afford.
But your cousin heard the opposite: *not sure it's useful, sure it's too expensive.* She nearly dropped
the idea.

When she told you, you asked the neighbour yourself. *Oh, I'm sure it'd help,* the neighbour said. *I
just don't know her money.* The hedges had been on the wrong words.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

You know what you're sure of, so your hedges always sound right to you. A listener only has the hedges to
go on. This test shows whether they point where your doubt really is.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take X that sounds like a joke**, because the swap is
so obvious. Say it seriously, as if it were true.

The second is **a listener who knows your real opinion** and answers from that. Use someone who doesn't.

The third, in real life, is **politeness moving the hedge**: you soften the good news to be modest, and
state the bad news firmly. The listener hears it the other way round.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers read hedges carefully, even if they don't know they do. *Sort of* in the wrong place
changes what they believe about you.

Under pressure, giving bad news, you may want to hedge it more. Hedge only what you're really unsure of.
Soften bad news with kind words, not with false doubt.

A native listener who acts on your advice will act on the parts you said plainly. Make sure they're the
parts you meant.
