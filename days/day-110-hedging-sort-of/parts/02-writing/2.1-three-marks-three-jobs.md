## One-line answer

Each mark has its own jobs, and a reader sees them. A **comma** separates items in a list, ends an
opening part (*When you arrive, …*), and goes before *and*, *but* or *so* when they join two full
sentences. A **colon** comes after a **complete sentence** and points to what follows: a list or an
explanation. A **semicolon** joins two full sentences that belong together, and separates list items
that already contain commas. Put a mark in the wrong place and the reader counts the wrong number of
things, or reads the wrong meaning.

## The story

You're going away for a week, and your neighbour has offered to water your plants and let in the
plumber. You leave a note:

> The people coming are: Ravi the plumber Mrs Das from upstairs who has a key and my cousin Anil on
> Friday if you see a problem call me.

Your neighbour texts you: *How many people are coming? Is Mrs Das the plumber? Do I call you on Friday or
if there's a problem?*

You rewrite it:

> Three people may come: Ravi, the plumber; Mrs Das, from upstairs, who has a key; and my cousin Anil, on
> Friday. If you see a problem, call me.

Your neighbour texts back a thumbs-up.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **sentence**: a group of words with a subject and a verb that makes sense alone, starting with a
  capital and ending with a full stop, from
  [day 3](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md).
- **joining word**: a word that joins two ideas: *and*, *but*, *so*, *because*, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-four-joining-words.md).
- **clause**, **semicolon**, **comma splice**: a group of words with its own subject and verb; the mark
  *;*; and two full sentences joined by only a comma, which is a mistake, from
  [day 89](../../../day-89-presenting-opening-closing/parts/02-writing/2.1-three-formal-joins.md).
- **comma**: the mark *,*, a small pause inside a sentence.
- **colon**: the mark *:*, which points forward to a list or an explanation.
- **opening part**: words before the main sentence that set the scene: *When you arrive*, *On Friday*,
  *If you see a problem*.
- **list item**: one thing in a list.

| Mark | Job | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| comma | between list items | *milk, bread, eggs and rice* |
| comma | after an opening part | *If you see a problem, call me.* |
| comma | before *and*, *but*, *so* joining two full sentences | *The tap drips, so please turn it off firmly.* |
| colon | after a full sentence, before a list | *Three people may come: …* |
| colon | after a full sentence, before an explanation | *There is one rule: never leave the gate open.* |
| semicolon | between two full sentences that belong together | *The plumber comes on Monday; he has his own key.* |
| semicolon | between list items that contain commas | *Ravi, the plumber; Mrs Das, from upstairs; and Anil* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Commas](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/commas/), from a university writing centre, gives
the rules: "Put commas between items in a list."; introductory words, phrases or clauses are set off
with a comma, with "Generally, extraterrestrials are friendly and helpful."; "You should always have a
comma before FANBOYS that join two independent clauses", with "They smell really bad, so they will be
easy to find."; and "Two commas can be used to set off additional information".
[Colon (punctuation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_(punctuation)) says: "In modern English usage,
a complete sentence precedes a colon, while a list, description, explanation, or definition follows
it." On capitals: "While it is acceptable to capitalise the first letter after the colon in American
English, it is not the case in British English, except where a proper noun immediately follows a colon."
[Semicolon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicolon) says: "The most common use of the semicolon is to
join two independent clauses without using a conjunction such as the word *and*." And: "Semicolons can
also be used in place of commas to separate items in a list, particularly when the elements of the list
themselves have embedded commas", with "The people present were Jamie, a man from New Zealand; John, the
milkman's son; and George, a gaunt kind of man with no friends."
[Comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma) says the comma before the final *and* in a list, the serial
comma, "is one of the most disputed linguistic or stylistic questions in English".

**The colon test.** Cover everything after the colon. Is what's left a full sentence? *Three people may
come* is. *The people coming are* isn't, so it takes no colon: *The people coming are Ravi, Mrs Das and
Anil.*

**The semicolon test.** Put a full stop where the semicolon is. Are both halves full sentences? If yes,
the semicolon can stay. If not, it can't. In a list, it's allowed only when the items already have
commas inside them.

**The opening-part comma.** *If*, *When*, *After*, *On Friday*: when these come first, a comma ends
them.

**The serial comma.** *Milk, bread, eggs, and rice* or *milk, bread, eggs and rice*. The page says
writers disagree. This course's choice: leave it out, unless the list would be misread without it. Be
consistent on one page.

**Name the trap.** A colon looks formal, so it gets put after *are* and *include*, before any list. The
reader doesn't mind much, but a careful reader sees it at once.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the note with no marks inside**, as in the story. The reader can't tell
where one person ends and the next begins.

The second is **the colon after half a sentence**:

> The people coming are: Ravi, Mrs Das and Anil.

*The people coming are* isn't a full sentence.

The third is **a list of described people with only commas**:

> Ravi, the plumber, Mrs Das, from upstairs, and Anil.

Is that three people or five? The reader can't tell.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers use the semicolon rarely, and the colon mostly for lists and explanations. The comma does
most of the work.

Under pressure, in a quick message, commas get dropped first. The one that matters most is after an
opening *if* or *when*.

A formal reader reads your punctuation as care. A note where every list can be counted and every
instruction read once is a note they trust.
