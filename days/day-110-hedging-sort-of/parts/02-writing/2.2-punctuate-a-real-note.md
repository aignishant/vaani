## One-line answer

Write a real note of instructions for someone who'll look after something of yours: your home, your
plants, your child, your shop. Use each job at least once: a list with commas, an opening part with a
comma, *and*, *but* or *so* joining two full sentences with a comma, a colon after a full sentence, a
semicolon between two full sentences, and a list of described items with semicolons. Then give it to a
reader and ask three questions only the punctuation can answer.

## The story

After the plumber note, you started writing all your notes for your neighbour with care. When your
mother came to stay, you left her one about the house: who might ring, what to do about the gas, where
the keys were. You read each colon aloud and stopped, to check a full sentence came before it. You put a
full stop where each semicolon was, to check both halves stood alone.

Your mother read it once and did everything on it. She didn't phone you with a single question.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Commas](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/commas/): "Put commas between items in a list.";
introductory elements are set off with a comma; "You should always have a comma before FANBOYS that join
two independent clauses".
[Colon (punctuation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_(punctuation)): "a complete sentence precedes
a colon, while a list, description, explanation, or definition follows it."
[Semicolon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicolon): it joins "two independent clauses without using a
conjunction", and separates list items "when the elements of the list themselves have embedded commas".

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **forcing a semicolon** between two sentences that don't
belong together. Two separate sentences with a full stop are always correct.

The second is **questions the reader can answer without the punctuation**. Make each one depend on a
mark: a count, a who-is-who, or a when.

The third is **a serial comma in one list and not in the next**. Pick one way for the whole note.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers rarely use all six jobs in one short note. You're using them all here to practise; in
real notes, use what the note needs.

Under pressure, write short sentences with full stops. They're always right. Add colons and semicolons
when you check.

A reader who answers every question right after one reading has been given a note that works.
