## One-line answer

Make a copy of your note with the punctuation broken on purpose: the semicolons in your described list
changed to commas, a colon after half a sentence, and the comma after an opening part removed. Give the
copy and your real note to two readers, with your three punctuation questions. The copy's reader should
get at least one answer wrong, most often the count. Your real note's reader should get all three right.

## The story

Your neighbour kept your first note, the one with no marks inside, and showed it to her son as a puzzle.
*How many people are coming?* He counted *Ravi*, *the plumber*, *Mrs Das*, *from upstairs*, *who has a
key*, *my cousin Anil*, and said six. Then he read your second note and said three, straight away.

*Same words,* she said. *Different marks.*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

You know how many people are in your list, so you can't see the list that isn't there. A reader counting
from the marks alone shows you what your punctuation actually says.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Semicolon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicolon): list items are separated with semicolons
"particularly when the elements of the list themselves have embedded commas", with "The people present
were Jamie, a man from New Zealand; John, the milkman's son; and George, a gaunt kind of man with no
friends."
[Colon (punctuation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colon_(punctuation)): "a complete sentence precedes
a colon".
[Commas](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/commas/): introductory elements are set off with a
comma.

**Step one: make copy B.** Copy your note and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Change every semicolon in your described list to a comma.
2. Move one colon to after half a sentence: *The things to check are: …*
3. Remove the comma after one opening part: *If the tap drips call the plumber.*

**Step two: the count test.** Give B to one reader and R to a different reader. Ask each your three
punctuation questions from [part 2.2](2.2-punctuate-a-real-note.md).

opening-part comma. Give R to a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who counts right in copy B anyway**, because
the names are obviously people. Use descriptions that could also be people: *the plumber*, *my cousin*,
*the man from upstairs*.

The second is **a reader who asks you** what you meant. Don't answer. The note has to answer.

The third, in real writing, is **the colon after *are* or *include***. It's the most common place a colon
goes wrong, and the easiest to fix: take it out.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers avoid long described lists when they can, and use a new line for each item instead. When
they can't, the semicolon is what keeps the count right.

Under pressure, a list of people with descriptions is where punctuation fails. Count your own list from
the marks before you send it.

A reader who has to ask *How many?* has found a mark you left out. Better they don't have to ask.
