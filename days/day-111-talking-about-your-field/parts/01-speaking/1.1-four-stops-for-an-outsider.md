## One-line answer

To talk at length about your own **field** to someone outside it, make four stops. **What it is**: one
sentence an outsider could repeat. **Who it's for**: the people your work helps. **A real day**: one
concrete example of what you actually do. **Why it matters**: what would go wrong, or be missing, without
it, ideally in the listener's own world. Keep jargon out, or define a word once and keep it. The four
stops give a long turn a shape, so the listener always knows where they are.

## The story

At a cousin's wedding, an older relative asks your friend, a lab technician at a hospital, what she
actually does. She's been asked this before, and she answers the way she'd tell a colleague:

> I run CBCs and LFTs on the analyser, do the QC every morning, and flag anything out of range to the
> pathologist.

The relative nods politely and asks about the food.

At the next wedding, she tries four stops:

> I test blood in a hospital lab. The doctors send us samples, and we tell them what's in them. This
> morning, for example, a child came in with a fever, and our test showed an infection, so the doctor
> knew which medicine to give. Without the lab, doctors would be guessing.

The relative puts down his plate. *So when I had my blood test last month, that was you?* They talk for
the rest of the meal.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **what do you do**: answering with the name of the work, what you do in a day, and one thing about
  it, from [day 33](../../../day-33-your-job-or-studies/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-do-you-do.md).
- **jargon**: the words of your work that people outside it don't know, from
  [day 98](../../../day-98-explaining-technical-thing/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-word-on-the-screen.md).
- **chunk you own**: a group of English words you can say without thinking, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/01-speaking/1.1-start-from-english-you-own.md).
- **field**: the area of work or study you know best: nursing, accounts, farming, teaching, software,
  tailoring.
- **outsider**: someone who doesn't work or study in your field.
- **stop (in a long turn)**: one of the four parts of the talk: what, who for, a real day, why it
  matters.

| Stop | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | one sentence an outsider can repeat | *I test blood in a hospital lab.* |
| Who it's for | the people it helps | *The doctors send us samples, and we tell them what's in them.* |
| A real day | one concrete example | *This morning, a child came in with a fever …* |
| Why it matters | what's missing without it | *Without the lab, doctors would be guessing.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate includes an unscripted talk of at least four hundred spoken words, and a recorded
conversation. Your own field is the topic you know most about, so it's the one where you're most likely
to lose an outsider in detail. The four stops keep them with you.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Jargon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jargon) defines it as "the specialized terminology associated with
a particular field or area of activity". It says jargon "may not be well understood outside that
context", and that while it "allows greater efficiency in communication among those familiar with it,
jargon also raises the threshold of comprehensibility for outsiders."
[Fluency](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/fluency) says: "Be very
clear about the message you want to communicate. That is your goal."

**Stop one: what it is, in one sentence.** No job title, no jargon. A sentence the listener could repeat
to someone else tonight.

**Stop two: who it's for.** The people your work helps, or the thing it produces. This is how the
listener finds their way in.

**Stop three: a real day.** One example, from a real day, with a real person or thing in it. *This
morning*, *Last week*, *Yesterday*.

**Stop four: why it matters.** What would be missing without it. Best of all, in the listener's world:
*your blood test*, *your electricity bill*, *your child's school*.

**Jargon: swap it or define it once.** Swap a word for plain words if you can. If the listener will need
the word, because they'll hear it again, say it once, explain it, and keep using the same word.

**Signal each stop.** Short markers help: *So basically, …*, *For example, …*, *And the reason it
matters is …*

**Name the trap.** When you know a field well, the interesting part to you is the detail. For an
outsider, the interesting part is where it touches their life. Start there.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the colleague's answer**, as in the story: correct, full of jargon, and
closed to anyone outside.

The second is **the job title and stop**: *I'm a lab technician.* The listener has nothing to ask.

The third is **all detail, no why**: three examples of what you do, and never why it matters. The
listener follows, and doesn't care.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers who explain their work well usually have one example they always use, the one that
makes outsiders say *Oh!* Find yours.

Under pressure, at an interview or with someone senior from another department, the four stops still
work, in a formal voice.

A native listener who asks a follow-up question, *So that was you?*, has understood. That question is
the sign of a good long turn.
