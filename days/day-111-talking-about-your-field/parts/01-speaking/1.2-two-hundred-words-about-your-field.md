## One-line answer

Record a long turn of about two hundred spoken words about your own field, to a real outsider, with the
four stops in order. Then write out the transcript, count the words, mark each stop, and underline every
jargon word. Each underlined word must be either swapped for plain words or defined once and kept. Record
again if a stop is missing or a jargon word was never explained.

## The story

After the wedding, your friend decided to practise. She asked her younger brother, who studies history,
to listen while she talked about the lab until she ran out. She recorded it on her phone.

When she wrote it out, it was about two hundred words. She'd done three stops well and forgotten stop
two; she never said who the lab's work was for. She'd also used *analyser* four times without explaining
it. She recorded it again. Her brother said the second one was the first time he'd understood what she
did all day.

- **transcript**: every word of a recording, written down exactly as it was said.

  or defined.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a listener from your own field**. They'll understand
everything, and the jargon count will find nothing. Find an outsider.

The second is **a long turn that stops at a hundred words** because you've said the four stops once.
Add a second example to stop three, or say more about why it matters.

The third is **reading from notes**. The gate talk has no notes. Speak from the four stops in your head.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers can talk about their field for a long time because they have several good examples
ready. Each extra example is more talk the listener can follow.

Under pressure, the jargon comes back. The transcript shows you which words to watch for.

A native listener doesn't need your field's words. They need your field's reasons.
