## One-line answer

Record your long turn twice. In take D, leave out *who it's for* and *why it matters* on purpose, and
fill the space with more detail about how the work is done. In take W, use all four stops. Play each to a
different outsider, and ask two questions: *What does this person do, in one sentence? Why does it
matter?* D's listener may answer the first and should struggle with the second. W's listener should
answer both.

## The story

Your friend's colleague, a very good technician, was asked to talk to a group of school students about
working in a lab. He talked about the machines, the samples, the checks and the timings, in careful
detail. The students were quiet the whole time. At the end, a teacher asked them what they'd learned.
One said: *There are a lot of machines.*

Your friend spoke to the next group. She told them about the child with the fever, and what the doctor
would have done without the test. Three students asked how to become a lab technician.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **field**, **outsider**, **stop (in a long turn)**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-four-stops-for-an-outsider.md).
- **transcript**, **jargon count**: from
  [part 1.2](1.2-two-hundred-words-about-your-field.md).
- **process detail**: how the work is done, step by step, without why.
- **the what-and-why test**: an outsider hears a long turn and says, in one sentence each, what the
  speaker does and why it matters.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D, detail only (deliberate) | no *who it's for*; no *why it matters*; more process detail instead | *what*, maybe; *why*, no |
| W, whole | all four stops | both *what* and *why* |

## Why Vaani needs it

When you know a field well, the detail feels like the interesting part. An outsider shows you whether
they've understood why any of it matters, which is what makes them keep listening in a long talk.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take D that's much shorter than W**. Keep them about
the same length, so the only difference is what's in them.

The second is **a listener who guesses the why** from general knowledge: *Hospitals are important.*
That's not your why. Ask: *Did the speaker say that?*

The third, in real life, is **the why said once, too early**, and then twenty sentences of detail. Say
it again near the end.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers in every field keep coming back to why. It's what makes a long talk about accounts, or
soil, or code, one an outsider wants to hear.

Under pressure, in an interview, *why it matters* is the answer to a question they haven't asked yet:
*Why should we care about your experience?*

A native listener remembers the why and forgets the steps. Give them the why to remember.
