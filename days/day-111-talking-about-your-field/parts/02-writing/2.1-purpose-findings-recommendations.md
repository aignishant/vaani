## One-line answer

A **report** is written to help someone decide. It's set out under **headings**, not as an essay. A
short one has four sections. The **summary**: two or three sentences giving the purpose, the main
finding and the main recommendation. The **purpose**: why the report was written, and for whom. The
**findings**: what you checked and what you found, as facts. The **recommendations**: what should be
done, each one linked to a finding. Facts go in the findings; opinions go only in the recommendations.

## The story

The lift in your building kept breaking down, and the residents' committee asked your neighbour to look
into it. She wrote them a long letter: how annoyed everyone was, a story about her mother stuck on the
fourth floor, some thoughts about the company, and, near the end, an idea about a new contract.

The committee secretary rang her. *This is very heartfelt. But what did you find, and what do you want
us to do?*

She rewrote it as a report. Headings. A summary at the top. What she'd checked: the repair book, the
dates, the company's contract. What she'd found: eleven breakdowns in three months, most on weekends when
no engineer was on call. One recommendation for each finding. The committee voted on it at the next
meeting.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **summary**: a short version giving only the main points, in your own words, from
  [day 106](../../../day-106-discussing-news/parts/02-writing/2.1-tag-main-point-key-points.md).
- **status line**: one line at the top saying where things stand, from
  [day 88](../../../day-88-short-update-on-work/parts/02-writing/2.1-status-line-done-next-needs.md).
- **most important thing first**: putting the news before the background, from
  [day 55](../../../day-55-passing-on-news/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-most-important-thing-first.md).
- **report**: a document written to help someone decide something, set out under headings.
- **heading**: a short title above a section of a report, such as *Findings*.
- **purpose**: why a report was written, and for whom.
- **finding**: a fact you checked and found, which can be shown.
- **recommendation**: what the writer thinks should be done, based on a finding.

| Section | What it carries | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Summary | purpose, main finding, main recommendation, in two or three sentences | *This report looks at … . It finds that … . It recommends …* |
| Purpose | why, and for whom | *The committee asked for a report on …* |
| Findings | facts, each one checkable | *The repair book shows eleven breakdowns between …* |
| Recommendations | action, each one from a finding | *The committee should …, because (finding 2) …* |

## Why Vaani needs it

A report is the most useful formal piece in the phase 6 writing portfolio for work and study. It's also
the clearest test of keeping facts and opinions apart, which every essay needs too.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to write a report](https://www.citewrite.qut.edu.au/write/writing-well/report.html), from a
university's writing guide, says a report is organised "into sections with headings and subheadings
rather than paragraphs". It says the introduction should "define the purpose of the report". It
describes an executive summary as "a paragraph that provides the reader with a quick overview of the
entire report, including its purpose, context, methods, major findings, conclusions and
recommendations." And of recommendations: "These are subjective opinions about what action you think
could be followed. They must be realistic, achievable and clearly relate to the conclusion."
[Summarizing in writing](https://writingcenter.uci.edu/2023/08/18/summarizing/): "A summary of a text is
a short overview of the main ideas written in your own words."

The report page describes a full report, with methods, a discussion and a conclusion as well. The four
headings below are this course's short version of it, for a report of a page or less.

**The shape on the page.**

```text
  REPORT ON THE LIFT IN BLOCK B            ← a title that names the subject
  ─────────────────────────────
  Summary          2–3 sentences: purpose · main finding · main recommendation
  Purpose          why, for whom, what was checked
  Findings         1. fact   2. fact   3. fact          ← numbered, checkable
  Recommendations  1. action (from finding 1)
                   2. action (from finding 2)           ← each points back
```

**The summary goes first, and is written last.** A busy reader may read only this. It must stand alone.

**Findings are facts.** Numbers, dates, what a document says, what you saw. Each one could be checked by
someone else. No *I feel* and no *terrible*.

**Recommendations point back.** Each one names, or clearly follows from, a finding. The page says they
"must be realistic, achievable and clearly relate to the conclusion".

**Short numbered points under each heading.** A report is read to find things, not from start to finish.

**No contractions.** A report is formal writing. That's this course's choice for formal pages.

**Name the trap.** When you care about the problem, your feelings arrive before your facts, as in the
story. The reader has to dig for what you found.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the report written as a letter**, as in the story: heartfelt, in
paragraphs, with the recommendation buried at the end.

The second is **findings with opinions in them**:

> The company is useless and never comes on time.

That's a view. The finding is: *On eight of the eleven breakdowns, the engineer arrived the next day.*

The third is **a recommendation with no finding behind it**: *We should also repaint the stairs.* Nothing
in the findings mentions the stairs.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers of reports write the findings first, then the recommendations, then the summary last. The
summary is easy once the rest is done.

Under pressure, a report grows. The reader wants less. Three findings and three recommendations are
often enough.

A reader who can decide from your summary alone, and then check it against your findings, will trust
your next report.
