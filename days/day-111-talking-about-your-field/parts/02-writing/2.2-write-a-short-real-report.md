## One-line answer

Choose one real, small problem you can check yourself. Check it: count, look, read, ask. Then write a
short report under four headings: summary, purpose, findings and recommendations, with three findings
and one recommendation for each. Finally, give the summary alone to a reader and ask: *What did the
writer find, and what do they want done?* If they can answer from the summary, it stands alone.

## The story

Your neighbour's lift report worked, and the committee asked her for another, about the water tank. This
time she started by checking. She read the tank company's last three bills. She asked the watchman how
often the tank ran dry. She looked at the overflow pipe herself.

She wrote the findings first, three of them, each a fact. Then one recommendation for each. Then the
summary, two sentences. She gave the summary alone to her husband, and he said: *The tank runs dry
because the pump timer's wrong, and you want it reset and checked monthly.* That was it exactly.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **report**, **heading**, **purpose**, **finding**, **recommendation**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-purpose-findings-recommendations.md).
- **summary**: a short version giving only the main points, from
  [day 106](../../../day-106-discussing-news/parts/02-writing/2.1-tag-main-point-key-points.md).
- **checking**: finding facts yourself: counting, looking, reading a document, asking the person who
  knows.
- **summary-alone test**: a reader sees only the summary and says what the writer found and what they
  want done.

## The rule

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a problem you can't check yourself**, such as a
national issue. The findings become opinions. Choose something you can count or see.

The second is **three recommendations from one finding**, and two findings with none. Match them one to
one.

The third is **a summary that only says what the report is about**: *This report is about the tank.* The
reader wants what you found.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers of reports spend more time checking than writing. The writing is quick when the facts are
solid.

Under pressure, cut the report to the summary and the recommendations, and keep the findings in a list
below. That's often all a busy reader wants.

A reader who can act on your summary without asking you a question has been given a report that works.
