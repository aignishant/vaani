## One-line answer

Make a copy of your report that breaks the link between facts and actions, on purpose: add one
recommendation with no finding behind it, and change one finding into an opinion. Give the copy and your
real report to two readers. Each draws an arrow from every recommendation to the finding it comes from,
and circles any finding that's an opinion. The copy's reader should find a recommendation with no arrow,
and a circled finding. Your real report's reader should draw an arrow for every recommendation and
circle nothing.

## The story

At the committee meeting, another resident presented his own report on the lift. His findings said the
company was *hopeless* and the engineer *never bothered*. His recommendations were to change the company,
repaint the lift, and install a camera.

The secretary asked: *Where does the camera come from?* It didn't come from anything in his findings; he
just thought it was a good idea. *And what does "hopeless" mean, in numbers?* He didn't have any. The
committee voted for your neighbour's report instead.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

When you write a report, every recommendation feels justified, because you know your reasons. The arrow
test shows whether the reasons are on the page, where a reader can see them.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to write a report](https://www.citewrite.qut.edu.au/write/writing-well/report.html): recommendations
"are subjective opinions about what action you think could be followed. They must be realistic,
achievable and clearly relate to the conclusion."
[Summarizing in writing](https://writingcenter.uci.edu/2023/08/18/summarizing/): "Do not include your
opinions on the topic or the author's ideas in your summary."

**Step one: make copy O.** Copy your report and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Add one recommendation that none of your findings supports: a good idea from nowhere.
2. Change one finding into an opinion: *eleven breakdowns in three months* becomes *the lift is always
   breaking*.

**Step two: the arrow test.** Give O to one reader and R to a different reader. Ask each to draw an arrow
from every recommendation to the finding it comes from, and to circle any finding that's an opinion.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who draws arrows to the purpose** instead of the
findings. Tell them: arrows go to findings only.

The second is **a recommendation that follows from two findings**. Two arrows are fine. None is the
problem.

The third, in real writing, is **the recommendation you wanted before you checked**. If your findings
don't support it, the findings are telling you something.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent report writers draw these arrows themselves before they send anything. A recommendation with no
finding either gets a finding or gets cut.

Under pressure, the orphan recommendation is the one you care about most. Check it hardest.

A committee, a manager or a teacher reading your report will draw these arrows in their head. Give them
arrows to draw.
