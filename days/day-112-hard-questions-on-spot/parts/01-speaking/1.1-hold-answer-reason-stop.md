## One-line answer

When a question you didn't expect lands on you, answer it in four moves. **Hold**: one short line that
buys you a moment, said once. **Answer**: one sentence that actually answers, even if the answer is
*It depends* or *I'm not sure*. **One reason**: a *because* or a *for example*. **Stop**: let the other
person talk. The listener gets an answer in the first two sentences, and you get your thinking time
without a long silence.

## The story

Your friend works in a small office. On a Friday afternoon, the manager stops her in the corridor and
asks, without any warning:

> Do you think we should move the team to the new branch?

Nobody told her this was being discussed. She panics a little, and starts talking at once: the bus
routes, the old building, a colleague who lives far away, what someone said at lunch. After a while the
manager says: *So … is that a yes?* She doesn't know herself.

On Monday the manager asks someone else the same question. He says:

> Hmm, that's a tough one. Let me think. … Honestly, I'd say not yet, because half the team would need
> two buses to get there.

The manager nods and says: *Good point. I'll check the buses.* He had no more warning than she did. He
just answered in the second sentence.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **turn**: the time when it's one person's go to speak. **Well** and **so** are small words that start
  a turn and hold it, from [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/01-speaking/1.1-well-so-the-thing-is.md).
- **question shape**: the parts a good answer to a common question has, from
  [day 84](../../../day-84-standard-interview-questions/parts/01-speaking/1.1-four-questions-four-shapes.md).
  That day was about questions you could prepare for. This part is about the ones you couldn't.
- **hard question**: a question you have no ready answer for. It was unexpected, or it's awkward, or
  you really don't know.
- **on the spot**: straight away, with no time to prepare.
- **holding line**: one short line that tells the listener you heard the question and you're
  thinking, such as *Let me think.*
- **answer sentence**: the one sentence in your reply that a listener could repeat as your answer.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hold | buys a moment, once | *Hmm, that's a tough one. Let me think.* |
| Answer | one sentence that answers | *I'd say not yet.* / *It depends on the cost.* / *I'm honestly not sure.* |
| One reason | why, or one example | *because half the team would need two buses.* |
| Stop | hands the turn back | a falling voice on the last word, then silence |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate includes a recorded conversation, and a conversation is full of questions you didn't
choose. The gate's unscripted talk is on a topic drawn at random, so its first sentence is an answer to
a question you didn't see coming. If the first thing you do under surprise is talk around the question,
both recordings start badly.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Talk about yourself](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/talk-about-yourself)
lists, under things not to do: "Panic if you are asked an unexpected question. Take a few seconds to
think before you answer it." Under things to do, it says "Give complete answers in full sentences". It
also lists, as a thing not to do, "Just reply with 'yes' or 'no'."
[Taking turns](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/taking-turns) lists
"So …", "Erm …", "Well …" and "Let me think …" for avoiding silence and gaining thinking time. It says:
"In English-speaking cultures we avoid long silences or pauses."

**Move one: hold, once.** Choose one holding line and say it slowly. Then be quiet for a moment and
think. The line has made the silence polite. Three that work almost anywhere:

> Hmm, let me think.
>
> That's a good question.
>
> I haven't thought about that before.

Say one of them, not all three. One holding line sounds like thinking. Three in a row sounds like
hiding.

**Move two: answer in one sentence.** This is the move that gets skipped. The answer sentence comes
first, and the reasons come after it. If you really don't know, *I don't know* is an answer, and it's a
good one when you add what you do know:

> I'm honestly not sure, but I know the rent there is lower.

If the answer really is *it depends*, say what it depends on, in the same sentence: *It depends on the
buses.*

**Move three: one reason.** One *because*, or one *for example*. Not five. A second reason can wait
until they ask.

**Move four: stop.** Let your voice fall on the last word, and stop talking. The silence after an
answer is the other person's turn. It isn't a gap you need to fill.

**Name the trap.** Under surprise, most people start talking before they know what they think, and
hope an answer turns up on the way. It usually doesn't. The holding line exists so that you don't have
to start talking before you're ready.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the answer that never lands**, as in the story. It's ten sentences of
background, and the listener has to ask *So is that a yes?*

The second is **the frozen silence**: a long pause with nothing said, and then *I don't know* in a very
small voice. The listener can't tell whether you heard the question.

The third is **the stack of holding lines**: *That's a good question, that's a really good question,
um, let me think, well, you know …* The holding line has become the answer.

The fourth is **the bluff**: a confident answer to something you don't know. It works until the next
question, and then it doesn't.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use holding lines all the time, but they vary them. They also often answer with a
short sentence first, *Probably not*, and then give the reason. That's this part's shape at speed.

Under pressure, in an interview, a meeting or with an angry relative, the answer sentence matters more,
not less. A panel remembers what you answered, not how long you talked.

A native listener forgives a pause, a holding line and even *I don't know*. What they notice is when,
after all that, they still can't say what your answer was.
