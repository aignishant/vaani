## One-line answer

Ask a partner to write five hard questions about your life, work or town on slips of paper, without
showing you. Draw one at a time, start recording, and answer it in the four moves. Afterwards, write out
each answer, underline the answer sentence, and count how many sentences came before it. The goal is an
answer sentence in the first two sentences, every time.

## The story

Your friend from part 1.1 decided the corridor wasn't going to happen to her again. She asked her
younger brother to write five questions she'd hate to be asked, and to fold them up.

The first slip said: *If you could change one thing about your job, what would it be?* She said *Hmm,
let me think*, waited, and then answered in one sentence with a reason. The second slip said: *Why
haven't you learned to drive?* She talked for a long time about her father's car before she answered.

When she wrote out the second answer, the answer sentence was the ninth sentence. She drew the slip
again the next day, and it was the second sentence.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **hard question**, **holding line**, **answer sentence**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-hold-answer-reason-stop.md).
- **question slip**: a small piece of paper with one question on it, folded so you can't see it. This
  part calls them *slips* for short.
- **transcript**: your recording written out word for word, including the *um*s and the restarts.
- **sentences before the answer**: how many sentences you said before your answer sentence. The
  holding line doesn't count. Zero or one is the target.

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't prepare for a question you haven't seen. You *can* practise the moment of surprise itself, so
that the four moves come out without planning. That moment is the first thing the phase 6 gate's
unscripted talk and recorded conversation will test.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**

"Take a few seconds to think before you answer it." It also warns against memorising: "It's good to
have ideas ready, but it's better not to memorise long replies to typical questions."

**Step one: the slips.** Ask a partner to write five questions about your own life that you'd find hard.
They could be unexpected (*What's the worst advice you've ever been given?*), awkward (*Why did you
leave your last job?*) or ones you don't know the answer to (*What will your town look like in twenty
years?*). They fold them. You don't look.

**Step two: draw and record.** Draw one slip. Start recording before you read it aloud. Then answer it in
the four moves: hold once, answer in one sentence, one reason, stop. Do all five, one after another.
Don't stop the recorder between them, and don't re-record.

**Step three: the transcripts.** Write out each answer word for word. Underline the answer sentence.
Count the sentences before it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **reading the slip silently first**, then starting the
recorder. The surprise is the whole point. Record before you read.

The second is **questions that are too easy**, such as *What's your name?* Ask your partner for the
questions you'd avoid at a family dinner.

The third is **counting the holding line as the answer**. *That's a good question* isn't an answer
sentence. The underline goes on the sentence that says what you think.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers answer hard questions well mostly because they've been asked a lot of them. This
exercise gives you five in one sitting. Do it again with new slips whenever you can find a partner.

Under pressure, the question you fear most is usually one you already know. Put it on a slip.

A native listener asking a hard question is usually just curious. A clear short answer is often the end
of it. A long wandering one invites the next question.
