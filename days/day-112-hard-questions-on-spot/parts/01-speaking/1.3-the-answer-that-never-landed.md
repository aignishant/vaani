## One-line answer

Take one hard question from part 1.2 and record two answers to it. In take R, ramble on purpose: talk
around the question with background and side stories, and never say an answer sentence. In take A, use
the four moves. Play each take to a different listener and ask one question: *What was the answer, in
one sentence?* R's listener should struggle. A's listener should repeat your answer sentence almost word
for word.

## The story

Back to the corridor. The manager who asked about the new branch told your friend later what he'd
heard:

> You talked about buses and the old building and somebody's lunch. I honestly couldn't tell if you
> were for it or against it.

She'd been against it. It was clear in her head. It had never come out of her mouth as a sentence.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **hard question**, **holding line**, **answer sentence**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-hold-answer-reason-stop.md).
- **transcript**, **sentences before the answer**: from
  [part 1.2](1.2-five-questions-you-didnt-see-coming.md).
- **ramble**: to talk for a long time around a subject without getting to the point.
- **the one-sentence test**: a listener hears your reply and says, in one sentence, what your answer
  was.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R, ramble (deliberate) | background, side stories, reasons for and against, no answer sentence | *I'm not sure what they think.* |
| A, answer | hold once, answer sentence, one reason, stop | your answer sentence, in close to your words |

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't hear your own ramble while you're doing it, because you know what you meant. A listener who
doesn't know can. The one-sentence test turns *I think I answered* into something you can check.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take R that's obviously silly**. Make it sound like a
real ramble: every sentence true and related, just no answer.

The second is **a listener who's already heard A**. They'll give the answer from memory. Use two
different people.

The third, in real life, is **the answer said once, in the middle**, hidden between reasons. The listener
misses it. Put it first, and if the reply is long, say it again at the end.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers ramble too, when they're surprised. The difference is that they notice, stop, and say
*So, to answer your question: no.* That line rescues a ramble at any point. Keep it ready.

Under pressure, a listener who asks *So is that a yes?* is telling you that you've rambled. Answer that
question in one word, then your reason.

A native listener remembers your answer sentence and forgets nearly everything else. If there wasn't
one, they remember that too.
