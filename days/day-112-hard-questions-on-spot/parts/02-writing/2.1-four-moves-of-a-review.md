## One-line answer

A **review** helps a stranger decide whether to spend their money or their evening on something. It
makes four moves, usually one paragraph each. **What it is**: the name, what kind of thing it is, and
where or when. **What it is like**: a short description, without the ending. **Your judgement**: what
was good and what was not, each with a detail as the reason. **Who it is for**: your recommendation,
and who would not enjoy it. The last move is the one the reader came for.

## The story

A small new eating place opened near your neighbour's bus stop. Two people wrote about it in the
building's notice book.

The first wrote half a page. It described the owner's family, the history of the shop, the colour of the
walls and everything they ate, in order. It ended: *Then we went home.* Nobody could tell whether they
had liked it.

The second wrote five sentences:

> The new place near the bus stop serves home-style lunches at low prices. The dal was fresh and hot,
> and the rotis came to the table one at a time. It is small, so at one o'clock you may wait outside.

It ended: *Go early if you want a quick, cheap lunch. If you want a quiet place to sit and talk, it is
not for you.* By the weekend, three families in the building had tried it.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph**: a group of sentences about one idea, starting on a new line, from
  [day 57](../../../day-57-pausing-right-place/parts/02-writing/2.1-a-new-idea-a-new-paragraph.md).
- **recommendation**: what the writer thinks the reader should do, from
  [day 111](../../../day-111-talking-about-your-field/parts/02-writing/2.1-purpose-findings-recommendations.md).
  In a report it points back to a finding. In a review it points back to your judgement.
- **review**: a short piece of writing that describes something you have tried, such as a film, a
  book, a place to eat or a thing you bought, and says whether it is worth it.
- **judgement**: what you think of it: good, bad, or good in one way and bad in another.
- **detail as a reason**: a specific thing you saw, heard or tasted that shows *why* you think so.
- **spoiler**: telling the reader how a story ends, or its biggest surprise, before they have seen it.

| Move | What it answers for the reader | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | *What are we talking about?* | *The new place near the bus stop serves home-style lunches.* |
| What it is like | *What would I find there?* | *It is small, with six tables and a short menu.* |
| Your judgement | *Is it good, and how do you know?* | *The dal was fresh and hot.* |
| Who it is for | *Should I go?* | *Go early if you want a quick, cheap lunch.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Your review page goes into the writing portfolio, and the phase 6 gate indexes that portfolio in full.
A review is also one of the most common pieces of writing people actually post for strangers to read,
and a stranger decides quickly whether to trust it.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Film review](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/a2-writing/film-review) gives
these tips: "Start with the film's title." "Say the type of film and when it was made." "Explain the
film's story but don't explain the ending!" "Give your opinion of the film". "Should people go and watch
the film? Say what you think at the end."
[Spoiler (media)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoiler_(media)) defines a spoiler as something that
"reveals significant plot elements", so that "the experience of discovering the plot naturally" is
"robbed ('spoiled') of its full effect."

That page is about films. The same four moves work for a book, a show, a place to eat, a shop, a bus
route or a thing you bought. Only the details change.

**Move one: what it is.** The name, the kind of thing, and where or when. One or two sentences. A reader
who stops here knows what the review is about.

**Move two: what it is like.** A short description. For a story, say how it starts and what the problem
is, and stop there. For a place or a thing, say what you would see and use. **Never the ending.** Keep
this move short. It is the move that grows too long.

**Move three: your judgement, with details.** Say what was good and what was not. After every judging
word (*excellent*, *disappointing*, *slow*), put the detail that made you think it:

> The service was slow: two tables that came in after us were served first.

**Move four: who it is for.** Say who should try it and who should not. Say it at the end, where the
reader looks for it. Both halves are useful. *Not for you if …* is how a reader learns to trust you.

**Formal or relaxed.** Many published reviews use contractions. In this course, reviews leave them out,
as essays and reports do. It is this course's choice, and it keeps the portfolio in one voice.

**Name the trap.** When you liked or hated something, the story of it feels like the review. It is not.
The reader wants your judgement and your reasons, not a replay.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **all story, no judgement**: the whole film, or the whole meal, in order,
and then *It was good.* The reader has read a lot and learned only that.

The second is **the judging word with no detail**: *The food was amazing. The service was terrible.
Everyone must go.* The reader cannot tell whether your *amazing* is theirs.

The third is **the spoiler**: *In the end, the brother was the thief.* The reader now has no reason to
watch it.

The fourth is **no recommendation**: the review just stops, and the reader has to guess what you would
tell them to do.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent reviewers often put a one-line verdict at the very top as well as at the end: *Cheap, fast and
honest.* A busy reader may read only that line.

Under pressure, when you have only a few lines, keep move three and move four and cut move two to one
sentence.

A reader trusts a review that says what was bad about something the writer liked. A review with nothing
but praise reads like an advert.
