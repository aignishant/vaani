## One-line answer

Write a review of one real thing you have tried, in four short paragraphs, one for each move, with no
contractions. Every judging word gets a detail. Then give it to a reader who has not tried the thing and
ask two questions: *Did the writer like it?* and *Would you try it, and why?* If they can answer both and
point to a sentence for the *why*, the review works.

## The story

After the notice-book review, your neighbour decided to write one herself, about the tailoring shop
where she had her daughter's school uniform made.

She wrote the four moves. In the judgement paragraph she first wrote *The work was very good.* Then she
asked herself: *How do I know?* She added: *The hems were straight, and the buttons were still on after
a month of school.* In the last paragraph she wrote who it was for, and who it was not for: *It is not
the place if you need something in two days.*

She showed it to a friend who had never been there. The friend said: *She liked it, but it is slow. I
would go, because my son's trousers always lose buttons.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **review**, **judgement**, **detail as a reason**, **spoiler**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-four-moves-of-a-review.md).
- **judging word**: a word that gives an opinion, such as *good*, *terrible*, *excellent*, *boring*,
  *slow*.
- **the try-it test**: a reader who has not tried the thing reads your review and says whether you liked
  it, and whether they would try it and why.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a review of something you only half remember**. The
details come out vague. Choose something recent.

The second is **paragraph two taking over**: three sentences of judgement and ten of description. Count
the sentences in each paragraph.

The third is **a reader who has already tried it**. They answer from their own experience, not from your
review. Find someone who has not.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent reviewers keep notes while they watch, eat or use the thing. The details are the hard part to
remember afterwards, and the details are what make a review worth reading.

Under pressure, for example writing a quick review before you leave a shop, write move three and move
four only: one detail, one recommendation.

A reader who says *I would try it because …* and then quotes your detail back to you has been given a
review that works.
