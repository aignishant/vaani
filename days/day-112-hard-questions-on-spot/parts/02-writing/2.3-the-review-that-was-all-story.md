## One-line answer

Make a copy of your review that is all story, on purpose. Replace your judgement paragraph with more
description, take out who it is for, and end with one flat line such as *It was good.* Give your real
review to one reader and the copy to another. Each reader marks every sentence **S** for story or **J**
for judgement, then answers: *Should I try it, and why?* The copy's reader should struggle. Your real
review's reader should answer with one of your details.

## The story

The first notice-book review, the half page about the new eating place, was written by someone who had
loved it. They described the walls, the owner's family and every dish in order, because remembering it
was a pleasure.

A neighbour read it twice and asked the writer on the stairs: *So, is it any good?* The writer was
surprised. *I said it was good. At the end.* It was one sentence, after twenty sentences of story.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **review**, **judgement**, **detail as a reason**, **spoiler**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-four-moves-of-a-review.md).
- **judging word**, **the try-it test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-review.md).
- **story sentence**: a sentence that says what happened or what is there, with no opinion in it.
- **the S and J test**: a reader marks each sentence S for story or J for judgement, then says whether
  they would try the thing and why.

| Copy | How it is made | What a reader should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| T, all story (deliberate) | judgement paragraph replaced by description; no *who it is for*; one flat line at the end | mostly S; *I do not know if I should try it* |
| R, your real review | as written in part 2.2 | a J in most of paragraph three; *I would, because …* with your detail |

## Why Vaani needs it

A review that is all story is the easiest kind to write, because you only have to remember. It feels
full to you. A reader who does not know the thing shows you whether there was a judgement in it at all.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Film review](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/a2-writing/film-review):
"Give your opinion of the film" and "Should people go and watch the film? Say what you think at the
end."
[Spoiler (media)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spoiler_(media)): a spoiler "reveals significant plot
elements".

**Step one: copy T.** Write *deliberate* at the top. Copy your review. Replace paragraph three with more
description: what happened next, what was there, what you did. Take out paragraph four. End with one
flat line: *It was good.* or *It was not good.* Keep it about the same length as your real review. Do
not add a spoiler; the only change is that the judgement is gone.

**Step two: the S and J test.** Give copy T to one reader and your real review, R, to another. Neither
reader should see both. Ask each to mark every sentence S or J, and then to answer:

> Should I try it, and why?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if R's reader cannot say whether they would try
it, or gives a reason that is not in your review, or marks fewer J than S. It is **GREEN** when R's
reader answers with one of your details and marks at least as many J as S, and T's reader struggles.

**Step three: the repair.** If R went red, look at the sentences they marked S. Turn at least two of them
into judgement with a detail: not *The rotis came one at a time* but *The rotis were always hot, because
they came one at a time.* Make sure paragraph four says who it is for. Try a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a copy T with a spoiler in it**. Then the reader reacts
to the spoiler, and you cannot tell what the missing judgement did. Change one thing only.

The second is **a reader who marks every sentence J to be kind**. Show them one example of each first:
*The walls are blue* is S. *The walls are a cheerful blue that makes it feel bigger* is J.

The third, in real life, is **a real review that turns into story halfway through**. Paragraph three
starts with a judgement and then slides back into what happened. Check each sentence, not each
paragraph.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent reviewers do describe, but every description is chosen because it supports a judgement. A detail
that does not help the reader decide is cut.

Under pressure, for a short online review, the whole thing can be one judgement, one detail and one
recommendation. That is still a review. Twenty sentences of story are not.

A reader who finishes a review and asks *So, is it any good?* has told you the answer: there was no
judgement they could find.
