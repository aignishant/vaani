## One-line answer

When you hear yourself say something wrong that changes the meaning, such as a time, a place, a number,
a name or *yes* instead of *no*, fix it at once. Say a short signal, *sorry*, *I mean* or *no, wait*.
Then say **only the corrected piece**, with that word pushed, and carry on. If the slip doesn't change
the meaning, let it go and keep talking. You fix what the listener would act on, not everything you
notice.

## The story

Your cousin is telling the family the plan for a wedding, in a voice note to everyone:

> The bus leaves from the temple at seven in the morning. Bring your own water.

It leaves at **six**. She heard herself say *seven*, but she was in the middle of a sentence and didn't
want to stop. Half the family arrived at a quarter to seven, and the bus had already gone.

At the next wedding, she made the same slip, and this time she fixed it straight away:

> The bus leaves at seven, sorry, I mean SIX, six in the morning. Bring your own water.

Two extra words and one pushed number. Everybody was there by a quarter to six.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **stress for emphasis**: making the one word that carries the difference the loudest in the sentence,
  from [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/01-speaking/1.1-push-the-word-you-mean.md).
  On that day you used it to correct someone else. Today you use it on yourself.
- **chunk you own**: a group of English words you can say without thinking, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/01-speaking/1.1-start-from-english-you-own.md).
- **slip**: a small mistake you make while speaking, when you know the right word but a different one
  comes out.
- **self-correction**: fixing your own slip while you're still speaking.
- **repair signal**: the short word or words that tell the listener *the next bit replaces the last
  bit*, such as *sorry*, *I mean* or *no, wait*.
- **meaning slip**: a slip that would make the listener do or believe the wrong thing. **Small slip**:
  one the listener understands anyway.

| Slip | Kind | Fix it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *seven* for *six* (a time) | meaning slip | yes, at once |
| *Tuesday* for *Thursday* | meaning slip | yes, at once |
| *left* for *right* | meaning slip | yes, at once |
| *he go* for *he goes* | small slip | no, keep going |
| *a* for *an* | small slip | no, keep going |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate's unscripted talk and recorded conversation are one take each, with no editing. Slips
will happen. A slip fixed in two words sounds like fluent speech. A slip left standing puts a wrong fact
in the recording, and a slip fixed by starting the whole sentence again breaks the flow the gate listens
for.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[I mean](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/I_mean) gives, as its first sense: "Used to correct an error or
slip of the tongue."
[Conversation analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation_analysis) says that repair "describes
how parties in conversation deal with problems in speaking, hearing, or understanding". It says speakers
"seek to correct the trouble source by initiating and preferring self repair", meaning the speaker fixes
it rather than the listener.

**Step one: catch it.** You'll often hear the slip a word or two after you've said it. That's normal.
Fix it as soon as you hear it, not at the end of the sentence.

**Step two: signal.** One short signal, said quickly and a little quieter:

> sorry, I mean …
>
> no, wait …
>
> sorry …

**Step three: say only the corrected piece, pushed.** Don't go back to the start of the sentence. Say
the right word, louder and slightly longer than the words around it, as on
[day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/01-speaking/1.1-push-the-word-you-mean.md). If it's a
number or a time, say it twice: *SIX, six in the morning*. The repeat is for anyone who missed the first
one.

**Step four: carry on.** Go straight into the rest of what you were saying. No apology speech, no
laugh, no *oh, I'm so bad at this*.

**Let small slips go.** If you say *he go* and the listener understands, fixing it stops the flow for no
gain. Notice it, keep talking, and practise it later.

**Name the trap.** Many learners correct the wrong slips. They stop to fix small grammar in front of
everyone, because they were taught that grammar matters, and they let the wrong time go, because fixing
it means interrupting themselves. For a listener, it's the other way round.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the slip left standing**, as in the story. The speaker heard it, but
didn't want to stop.

The second is **the full restart**: *The bus leaves at seven, sorry, the bus, the bus leaves from the
temple, the bus leaves at six in the morning.* The listener has to find the difference themselves.

The third is **the correction with no signal**: *The bus leaves at seven six in the morning.* The
listener hears two numbers and doesn't know which one to believe.

The fourth is **fixing every small slip**: stopping on *a* and *an*, *go* and *goes*, until the listener
forgets what you were telling them.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers correct themselves all the time, and most listeners barely notice, because it's quick:
*on Tuesday, I mean Thursday*. It's a normal part of speaking, not a sign of weak English.

Under pressure, on a phone call or in a voice note someone will act on, fix meaning slips every time.
If you only notice after you've finished, add one line at the end: *Sorry, one correction: it's six,
not seven.*

A native listener forgives any number of small slips. What they act on is the wrong fact you didn't
fix.
