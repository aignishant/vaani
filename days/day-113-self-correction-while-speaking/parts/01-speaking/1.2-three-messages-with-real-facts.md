## One-line answer

Record three real voice messages, each carrying real facts someone will act on: times, places, days,
numbers, names. Don't plan any slips. Speak at your normal pace, and fix any meaning slip the moment you
hear it. Then write each message out, mark every slip, and sort them: meaning slips fixed, meaning slips
missed, small slips let go, small slips stopped for. The goal is every meaning slip fixed and no small
slip stopped for.

## The story

Your cousin, after the wedding bus, started listening to her own voice notes before she sent them. In
one about a family lunch she found *Sunday* where she meant *Saturday*, and she hadn't noticed saying
it. In another she'd stopped three times to fix *a* and *an*, and the message was twice as long as it
needed to be.

She didn't try to stop making slips. She changed what she fixed.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **slip**, **self-correction**, **repair signal**, **meaning slip**, **small slip**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-fix-it-the-moment-you-hear-it.md).
- **transcript**: your recording written out word for word, including the slips and the corrections.
- **slip sort**: putting every slip in a transcript into one of four boxes: meaning slip fixed, meaning
  slip missed, small slip let go, small slip stopped for.

## Why Vaani needs it

Part 1.1 showed a slip you made on purpose. Real slips are different: you often don't hear them at
all. This part shows you which slips you actually make, and whether your ear catches them while you're
talking.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[I mean](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/I_mean): "Used to correct an error or slip of the tongue."
[Conversation analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation_analysis): speakers "seek to correct
the trouble source by initiating and preferring self repair".

**Step one: three real messages.** Choose three things you really need to tell someone, each with at
least three facts in it. For example, arranging to meet someone, passing on a message from work, or
telling family about a change of plan. Record each as one voice message, at your normal pace, with no
script and no re-recording.

**Step two: the transcripts.** Write each message out word for word. Include every slip and every
correction exactly as you said them.

**Step four: check each fix.** For every meaning slip you fixed, check three things. Was there a signal?
Did you say only the corrected piece? Was the correction pushed?

**Step five: redo.** If any meaning slip was missed, record that message again. If you stopped for
small slips, record it again and let them go. Sort the new transcript.

It's fine if a message has no slips at all. Write *none* in the row. Don't invent slips for the table.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **reading the message from notes**. Slips happen when
you're making the sentence as you speak. Say it without a script.

The second is **speaking very slowly to avoid slips**. You'll make fewer, but that isn't how you'll
speak on the day. Use your normal pace.

The third is **leaving the corrections out of the transcript**. Write *seven, sorry, I mean six* exactly
as you said it, so you can check the fix.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers mostly fix the same few kinds of slips: numbers, days, names, *left* and *right*. Look
at your three transcripts for your own kinds. Those are what to listen for.

Under pressure, before you send a voice message someone will act on, play it back once. It costs less
than half the family missing the bus.

A native listener hearing *sorry, I mean Saturday* thinks nothing of it. A native listener who turns up
on Sunday thinks a lot of it.
