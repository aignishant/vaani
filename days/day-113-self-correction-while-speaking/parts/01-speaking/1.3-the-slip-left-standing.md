## One-line answer

Take one of your messages from part 1.2 and record it twice, with the same two meaning slips planted in
both. In take L, leave both slips standing, on purpose. In take C, fix each one with a signal and the
pushed corrected piece. Play each take to a different listener and ask them to write down the facts. L's
listener should write the wrong facts. C's listener should write the right ones, with no confusion about
which number or day was meant.

## The story

After the wedding bus, your cousin's uncle said something the family still laughs about: *I heard you
say seven. You never said anything else. So I came at seven.*

He was right. The only fact in the message was the wrong one. What she'd meant didn't reach him.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **slip**, **repair signal**, **meaning slip**: from

- **transcript**, **slip sort**: from [part 1.2](1.2-three-messages-with-real-facts.md).
- **planted slip**: a slip you put in on purpose, in a place you choose beforehand.
- **the facts test**: a listener hears a message and writes down every fact in it, as they would if they
  had to act on it.

## Why Vaani needs it

It's easy to believe a listener will *know what you meant*. They don't. They write down what you said.
The facts test shows you what reaches them, and whether your correction is clear enough to replace the
slip.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[I mean](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/I_mean): "Used to correct an error or slip of the tongue."
[Conversation analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation_analysis): repair "describes how
parties in conversation deal with problems in speaking, hearing, or understanding".

**Step one: plant the slips.** Take one message from part 1.2. Choose two facts in it and decide the
wrong version of each, such as a wrong day and a wrong number. Write them down, and write *deliberate*.

**Step two: take L.** Record the message with both wrong facts, and don't correct them. Keep your normal
pace.

**Step three: take C.** Record the message with the same two slips, and fix each at once, as in
[part 1.1](1.1-fix-it-the-moment-you-hear-it.md): a signal, only the corrected piece, pushed, and carry
on.

**Step four: the facts test.** Play L to one listener and C to another. Neither should hear both. Ask
each to write down every fact they'd need to act on the message.

as the slip? Fix the one that failed, record C again and try a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a planted slip so obvious that the listener corrects
it themselves**, such as *thirty-one o'clock*. Plant slips that sound like real facts, so the listener
has no way to know they're wrong.

The second is **a take C that apologises at length**: *Oh, sorry, sorry, I'm so bad at this, I meant
six.* The listener may still get the fact, but the message has stopped. Keep the signal short.

The third, in real life, is **a correction said as quietly as the slip**. The listener hears two
numbers, equally loud, and picks one. Push the right one.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers fix meaning slips almost without thinking, because they've learned what it costs not
to. This exercise gives you that lesson on purpose.

Under pressure, on a phone call with someone taking notes, such as a clinic, a bank or a delivery,
fix it at once and then check: *So that's six, not seven.* Their reply tells you whether the fix
reached them.

A native listener writes down what you said, not what you meant. The correction is the only thing that
can change what they wrote.
