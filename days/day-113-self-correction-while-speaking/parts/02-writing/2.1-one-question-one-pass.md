## One-line answer

A **proofreading checklist** is a short list of questions you ask of a finished page, one at a time, in
separate passes. Each question can be answered *yes* or *no* just by looking, such as *Does every
sentence end with a full stop?* Your own repeated mistakes go at the top. You read the whole page once
for each question, and look for nothing else on that pass. A page you have read once looking for
everything has been checked for nothing in particular.

## The story

Your friend applied for a place on a college course. She wrote the letter carefully, read it through
twice and sent it. The reply began *Thank you for your letter*, and then, in the same email, the office
asked her to confirm her date of birth. She had written it as *12/03/2001* in one place and *3 December*
in another. She had also written *there* for *their* twice. She had read those lines four times and seen
nothing wrong.

For the next application, she wrote six questions on a card: *Are all the dates written the same way?*
*Is every "there" really "there"?* She read the letter six times, once for each question. It took her
longer than reading it twice. She found both kinds of mistake, and one more she had not expected.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the second read**: reading your own page after a gap, out loud, as if a stranger wrote it, in three
  passes, from [day 78](../../../day-78-interrupting-politely/parts/02-writing/2.1-wait-then-read-as-a-stranger.md).
- **contraction**: a short form with an apostrophe in place of the missing letters, such as *don't* for
  *do not*, from [day 35](../../../day-35-contractions-aloud/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-mark-that-holds-the-missing-letters.md).
- **proofreading**: the last check of a finished page, for small mistakes you can see: spelling,
  punctuation, capitals, and things written two different ways.
- **pass**: reading the whole page once, from start to finish, looking for one thing.
- **proofreading checklist**: your list of questions for proofreading, one question per pass.
- **your own error**: a mistake that appears in your `docs/ERRORS.md` more than once.

The second read on day 78 fixes the whole page, from *does it make sense?* down to spelling. Proofreading
comes after that, when the words will not change any more. The checklist makes its last step narrower,
and it makes it yours.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Proofreading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofreading) says: "Checklists are common in proofreaders
where there is sufficient uniformity of product to condense some or all proofreading tasks into a
list." It says proofreading "generally focuses on correcting any final typos, spelling errors, stylistic
inconsistencies (e.g., whether words or numerals are used for numbers), and punctuation errors." It also
says it is "best not to proofread one's own text", and that it helps to "get some distance from the text"
first.
[Editing and Proofreading](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/editing-and-proofreading/) says:
"Keep an eye out for patterns of error; knowing what kinds of problems you tend to have will be helpful".
It says to proofread "for only one kind of error at a time". It suggests reading out loud, "which forces
you to say each word". For spelling, it says: "Start with the last word on the last page and work your
way back to the beginning, reading each word separately."

You usually have to proofread your own page. The checklist is how you make up for that: it tells your
eyes exactly what to look for, so they stop reading what you meant.

**Rule one: every item is a question you can answer by looking.** *Is the grammar good?* is not an item.
*Does every verb after he, she or it end in -s?* is.

**Rule two: your own errors first.** Look through `docs/ERRORS.md`. Any mistake that appears more than
once is an item. Write it as a question about the mistake itself.

**Rule three: a few items that every page needs.** These are the course's, not yours. They are the ones
the proofreading page names.

| Item | The question |
| --- | --- |
| Full stops and capitals | Does every sentence start with a capital and end with a full stop or question mark? |
| Written the same way | Are all dates, numbers and names written the same way every time? |
| Formal pieces | In an essay, report or formal letter, are there no contractions? |
| Spelling | Read backwards, word by word: is every word spelled right? |

**Rule four: short.** Six to eight items. A list of twenty will not be used.

**Rule five: one item, one pass.** Read the whole page for item one. Mark every problem. Then start again
from the top for item two. Put the spelling pass last, reading backwards. Read the grammar items aloud.

**Rule six: mark, then fix.** Put a small mark in the margin for every problem on every pass. Fix them all
at the end. Then count your marks by item. That count tells you which items are earning their place.

**Name the trap.** Your eyes read what you meant to write. That is why you can read *there* for *their*
four times and see nothing. A pass that looks for only one thing slows your eyes down enough to see what
is actually on the page.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the vague item**: *Check grammar.* You read, you feel it is fine, and
you tick it.

The second is **a checklist copied from somewhere else**, with none of your own errors on it. It catches
other people's mistakes.

The third is **one pass for everything**: reading the page once with the whole checklist in your head.
That is a read-through with a list beside it.

The fourth is **proofreading too early**, before the content is fixed. You tidy sentences that you then
delete.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers who write the same kind of document again and again, such as reports, letters or forms,
keep a checklist for that kind of document. They add to it when a mistake gets through.

Under pressure, with no time for eight passes, do your own top two items and the spelling pass. They
catch the most for the least.

A formal reader, such as an admissions office, an employer or an examiner, does not know which mistakes
are *just typos*. A date written two ways or *their* for *there* reads as careless, however good the
ideas are.
