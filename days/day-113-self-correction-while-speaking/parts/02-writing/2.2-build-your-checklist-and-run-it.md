## One-line answer

Write your own proofreading checklist of six to eight items: your own errors first, then the course's
items, with spelling last. Then run it on one real, finished page of at least two hundred words, one pass
per item. Mark every problem, fix them all at the end, and count the marks for each item. Keep the
checklist in `lab/writing/`. You will use it again at the phase 6 gate.

## The story

Your friend's card of six questions went into her purse. Before her next application, she ran it on a
letter she had already read twice. Her *dates written the same way* item found one problem. Her *there
and their* item found none this time. A new item she had added, *Is every "it's" really "it is"?*, found
three.

She crossed out the *there and their* item. It had done its job, because she was now checking it as she
wrote. She moved *it's* to the top.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **proofreading**, **pass**, **proofreading checklist**, **your own error**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-one-question-one-pass.md).
- **finished page**: a page whose content will not change any more. You have already done the second
  read and fixed the big things.
- **mark count**: how many problems each item found on one page.

## Why Vaani needs it

A checklist you have never run is a guess. Running it once shows which items find real mistakes on your
real pages, which items are too vague to use, and which of your mistakes are missing from it. The
version you keep is the one the phase 6 gate essay is proofread against.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Proofreading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofreading): "Checklists are common in proofreaders where
there is sufficient uniformity of product to condense some or all proofreading tasks into a list."
[Editing and Proofreading](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/editing-and-proofreading/):
"Proofread for only one kind of error at a time"; "Try reading out loud, which forces you to say each
word"; and, for spelling, "Start with the last word on the last page and work your way back".

**Step one: write the checklist.** Six to eight items. Your own errors first, from `docs/ERRORS.md` or
your last three pages. Then any of the course's four items from part 2.1 that fit. Spelling, read

the mistake has stopped appearing in `docs/ERRORS.md`.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **running it on a page that is not finished**. You start
rewriting sentences in the middle of a pass, and the passes stop being passes.

The second is **stopping to fix each mark as you find it**. Your eyes leave the pass and come back in a
different place. Mark first, fix at the end.

The third is **reading forwards on the spelling pass**. You start reading for meaning again, and the
spelling becomes invisible. Go backwards, one word at a time.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers' checklists change. The mistakes you make on day 113 are not the ones you will make a
year later. A checklist that never changes has stopped being yours.

Under pressure, keep the checklist where you write, on the first page of your notebook or at the top of
your document. A checklist you have to go and find does not get used.

A careful reader notices when a page has been proofread, even if they could not say how. There is
nothing to trip over.
