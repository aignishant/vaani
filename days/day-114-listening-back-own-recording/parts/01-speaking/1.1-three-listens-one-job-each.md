## One-line answer

Listen back to your own recording three times, and give each listen one job: first **as a stranger**
(what would they know or do afterwards?), then **for meaning** (wrong facts, lost threads, restarts),
then **for sound** (endings, stress, pace). Mark what you hear on a transcript, pick **the one problem
that costs the listener most**, and fix only that in your next take. How your voice sounds to you
isn't on the list.

## The story

Your neighbour runs a small tiffin service from her kitchen. Every Sunday evening she records a voice
message with the week's menu and the price, and sends it to her customers.

The first week she listened back once. All she heard was her own voice. *Do I really sound like that?*
She winced, decided it was fine, and sent it.

On Monday three customers called with the same question: *Is it eighty rupees a week, or eighteen?* She
had swallowed the end of *eighty*. It was right there in the message, but she'd missed it, because she'd
spent the whole listen on her voice instead of on the message.

The next Sunday she listened three times. Once as a customer, just to hear what she'd know. Once for
the facts: the days, the dishes, the price. Once for how the words came out. On the third listen she
caught *eighty* again, and it was the only thing she changed.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **take**: one recording attempt, from start to stop, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/01-speaking/1.2-record-the-introduction.md).
  A **retake** is a new take of the same piece.
- **transcript**: every word of a recording, written down exactly as it was said, from
  [day 19](../../../day-19-can-and-cant/parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-can-that-sounded-like-cant.md).
- **meaning slip**: a slip that would make the listener do or believe the wrong thing, from
  [day 113](../../../day-113-self-correction-while-speaking/parts/01-speaking/1.1-fix-it-the-moment-you-hear-it.md).
- **listen-back**: playing your own take to find out what a listener gets from it.
- **a listen**: one play of the whole take, start to finish, with one job.
- **the costliest problem**: of everything you marked, the one that does most harm to what the
  listener understands.

On day 20 you learned to listen once and retake only for a reason you could name. Today you learn
*how* to listen, so the reason you find is the right one.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate is judged by a listener who can't ask you what you meant. Your takes in days 115 to
119 are only as good as the listen-back after each one. If you listen for the wrong things, you'll
retake for the wrong reasons, and the take you keep won't be your best.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Voice confrontation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_confrontation) explains why a recording of
your voice sounds strange to you. When someone else speaks, you hear them through the air. When you
speak, "sound waves also travel through the person's body to their ears (an internal stimulus)". A
recording has only the air part. So everyone's recorded voice sounds odd to its owner, and it's normal.

**Before you start.** Write the transcript first, or at least the main lines, with room between them
for marks. Your marks go on the words, not on a counter.

**Listen one: the stranger's listen.** Play the whole take without stopping. Don't hold a pen. When it
ends, write one sentence: *After this, a stranger would know …* or *would do …*. Put it next to what you
meant to say. If they're different, that difference is already your costliest problem.

**Listen two: the meaning listen.** Pen in hand. Stop the recording as often as you need. Mark:

| Mark | What you're listening for |
| --- | --- |
| **F** | A fact that's wrong, missing or unclear, including any meaning slip you didn't fix |
| **T** | A place where the thread gets lost, and a stranger couldn't say what you're talking about |
| **R** | A restart: you went back to the start of a sentence instead of carrying on |

**Listen three: the sound listen.** Pen in hand again. Mark:

| Mark | What you're listening for | Taught on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **E** | A word ending you dropped | [day 6](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/01-speaking/1.1-finish-the-word.md) |
| **S** | Stress on the wrong syllable of a word | [day 7](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-loud-syllable.md) |
| **P** | Too fast, or a pause in the wrong place | [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/01-speaking/1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md) |

**Choose one.** Rank what you marked in this order, and take the highest:

1. Your stranger's sentence doesn't match what you meant.
2. An **F**, a wrong or unclear fact.
3. A **T**, a lost thread.
4. A sound mark (**E**, **S** or **P**) that changes which word a listener hears, like *eighty* heard as
   *eighteen*.
5. Everything else.

That one problem is what your next take fixes. The others wait for another retake.

**Name the trap.** Your voice sounding strange is not a problem on this list. It isn't something a
listener notices, because they've only ever heard you through the air. Many learners spend the whole
listen-back on their voice and hear nothing else, like the neighbour in the story.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **one listen for everything**. You try to hear facts, endings, stress and
pace at once, so you catch whatever is loudest and miss what matters, like the swallowed *eighty*.

The second is **listening only to your voice**: *I sound terrible.* That sentence gives you nothing to
fix.

The third is **skipping the stranger's listen**. You go straight to small sound marks and never notice
that the whole message didn't say what you meant.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers who record things, like voice messages, announcements or talks, do the same thing
without naming it. They listen once as the listener, then fix the one thing that would confuse
someone.

Under pressure, when you can only listen once before you send, make it the stranger's listen.
A wrong fact is the only thing that can't wait.

A native listener forgives an accent, a strange-sounding voice and a few dropped endings. What they
don't forgive is having to call you back to ask what you meant.
