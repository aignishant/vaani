## One-line answer

Take a real recording of yours from an earlier day, at least a hundred and fifty spoken words long. Do
the three listens and mark it. Choose the one costliest problem. Record the **whole piece again**,
changing only that. Then listen to the new take as a stranger and check two things: is the problem
gone, and did anything new break? Keep the better take, and file the retake in the portfolio ledger.

## The story

The neighbour's customers liked her new messages, so she went back to an old one: the long message she
had sent when she opened, explaining how the tiffin service worked, from ordering to paying.

She did the three listens. Her stranger's sentence came out as *they'd know the food is homemade, but not
how to order*. The ordering part was there, but she'd said it in the middle of a long bit about her
mother's recipes, and it got lost.

She didn't touch anything else. She recorded the whole message again with the ordering part moved to
the start, said once and clearly. Then she listened to it once as a customer. *They'd know how to
order.* Nothing else had got worse. That was the one she sent to new customers from then on.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **take**, **retake**, **transcript**, **listen-back**, **a listen**, **the costliest problem**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-three-listens-one-job-each.md).
- **take A**: the old recording you start from. **Take B**: your first retake. **Take C**: a second
  retake, if you need one.
- **the compare listen**: one stranger's listen to the new take, followed by a check of the one place
  you fixed.

**Step five: keep or go again.** If the problem is gone and nothing new broke, keep take B. If the

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a take B that changes several things**. Now you can't tell which change
helped and which one hurt.

The second is **a take B read from the transcript**. The fix is there, but the whole take sounds read:
flat, and with the pauses in the wrong places. Keep the transcript face down.

The third is **skipping the compare listen**. You feel that B is better because you tried harder, but
you never checked.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers who record things for other people rarely get it right first time. They get it right
by changing one thing and checking it.

Under pressure, before a message that matters, one retake with one fix is worth more than five retakes
with no plan.

A native listener hears the difference between your takes only in the one place that mattered. That's
the place you fixed.
