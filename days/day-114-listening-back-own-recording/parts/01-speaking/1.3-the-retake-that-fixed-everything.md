## One-line answer

Make one more retake of your take A from part 1.2, and this time try to fix **every** mark at once, on
purpose. Call it take X. Then a helper plays take A, your kept take and take X to a listener in a hidden
order. The listener says which one is easiest to follow and which one sounds read. The one-fix take
should win. If take X wins, or your kept take loses to take A, this part is RED.

## The story

One Sunday the neighbour had a long list of marks on her menu message: two dropped endings, a stress in
the wrong place on *vegetable*, an *umm* before the price, a pause in the wrong place and one wrong day.

She decided to fix all of it in one go. She wrote the message out word by word, with every fix marked,
and read it into the phone slowly and carefully.

Every mark was gone. But her oldest customer called on Monday: *Is everything all right? You sounded
like the railway station.* The message was correct and it sounded read. The warmth had gone, and so had
the easy pace that made people listen to the end.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **take A**, **take B**, **the compare listen**: from [part 1.2](1.2-one-fix-whole-retake.md).
- **the kept take**: the retake you kept at the end of part 1.2.
- **take X**: a retake that tries to fix every mark at once. You make it on purpose here.
- **blind listen**: a listener hears takes in an order they don't know, so they judge only what they
  hear.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | your old recording | harder to follow than the kept take |
| the kept take | one fix, from part 1.2 | easiest to follow, and not read |
| X (deliberate) | every mark fixed at once | correct, but slower or read, and not the easiest |

## Why Vaani needs it

When you listen back to a gate take, you'll hear many problems. Wanting to fix them all is natural. This
part shows you, with a real listener, what that does to a take, so you trust the one-fix method when it
matters.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Voice confrontation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_confrontation): a recording carries only the
sound that travels through the air, not the "internal stimulus" you hear when you speak. So the
listener judges the take the way the air carries it, and you can't. That's why this part uses a listener.

**Step one: make take X.** Go back to your marks on take A from part 1.2. Write *deliberate* at the top.
Try to fix every mark in one take: every ending, every stress, every pause, every fact, every restart.
Use the transcript if you want to. Most people do, and that's part of what this exercise shows.

**Step two: the blind listen.** Give your helper the three takes: A, the kept take and X. The helper
chooses an order and doesn't tell you or the listener. The listener hears all three, one after another,
and answers two questions:

Then the helper tells you which take was which.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if the listener chooses take X as easiest to
follow, or if the listener chooses take A over your kept take. It's **GREEN** when your kept take is
chosen as easiest to follow, and take A is not.

Take X winning isn't a disaster. It means your take A had so many problems that fixing everything
helped. Your one-fix method needs a better choice of problem, not more fixes.

**Step three: the repair.** If it went RED, listen to the three takes again yourself. Did your kept take
fix the costliest problem, or a smaller one? Was it read? Do part 1.2's step three again for the right
problem, and do the blind listen with a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of this exercise is **a helper who tells the listener which take is which**.
Then the listener judges your plan, not your takes. The order has to stay hidden.

The second is **a take X that isn't really trying**. If you make X badly on purpose, the test means
nothing. Try your honest best to fix everything.

The third, in real life, is **the take made perfect and dead**: every mark gone, and nobody listens to
the end.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers know that a recording is heard as a whole. One clear fix, in a take that still sounds
like a person talking, usually beats a take with no mistakes that sounds like a machine.

Under pressure, for a take that matters, fix the fact first, always. Leave the endings and the stress
for the next piece you record. You'll get them there.

A native listener stops listening to a voice that sounds read long before they'd stop for a dropped
ending.
