## One-line answer

To rewrite an old page, don't fix its sentences. Take the **facts** out of it as short notes, put the
old page away, and write a new page from the notes with everything you know now: paragraphs, a first
line that says what each paragraph is about, joining words, and a detail a stranger couldn't guess. The
facts stay; the sentences are new. The old page is never changed.

## The story

Two years ago your friend filled in the *Tell us about yourself* box on a job application. She was just
starting to write in English, and it was ten short sentences:

> I am Kavya. I live in Nagpur. I work in a pharmacy. I like my work. I have a brother.

This year she applied for a better job and took the old box out to use again. She went through it with a
pen. She fixed a spelling, added a comma, and changed *I like my work* to *I really like my work*. It was
tidier. It was still ten short sentences, and nine of them started with *I*.

Then she tried something else. She wrote the facts as a list of notes, *Nagpur*, *pharmacy, four years*,
*brother*, and turned the old page over. She wrote a new page from the list, the way she writes now. It
had two paragraphs, and it said *why* she liked the work. It sounded like the person she was this year.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **sentence**: a group of words that starts with a capital letter and ends with a full stop, from
  [day 3](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md).
- **paragraph**: a block of sentences about one thing, from
  [day 13](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-paragraph-is.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, which says what the paragraph is about, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **joining words**: *and*, *but*, *because*, *so*, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-four-joining-words.md).
- **edit**: change a page so it does its job better, from
  [day 78](../../../day-78-interrupting-politely/parts/02-writing/2.1-wait-then-read-as-a-stranger.md).
- **rewrite**: write a page again from its facts, in new sentences, as a new page.
- **fact list**: the facts of a page written as short notes, not sentences.

An edit changes the page you have. A rewrite makes a new page from the same facts. Today is a rewrite.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your day 20 page was written with the English you had on day 20. The phase 6 gate asks that both
portfolios are indexed in full, and a portfolio that shows only where you started hides how far you've
come. A rewrite, filed beside the original, shows it. The same method works for any old page you need
again, like a cover letter or a profile.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Revising Drafts](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/revising-drafts/) says:
"Revision literally means to "see again," to look at something from a fresh, critical perspective." It
also says "sometimes revision does mean trashing your first draft and starting from scratch", and
quotes the advice "THINK BIG, don't tinker".

**Step one: the fact list.** Read the old page once. For each sentence, write the fact as a short note.
No full sentences.

> Nagpur, with parents
>
> pharmacy, four years
>
> likes: helping old customers

Then change the list, not the page. Cross out a note that isn't true any more. Add a note for anything
true now that wasn't then.

**Step two: put the old page away.** Turn it over, or close the file. If you can see its sentences, you'll
copy them.

**Step three: group the notes.** Put notes that belong together in a group: who you are and where; your
work or studies; the people around you; what you like, and why. Each group becomes one paragraph.

**Step four: write from the list.** For each group:

- Start with a topic sentence that says what the paragraph is about.
- Join facts that belong together with *and*, *but*, *because* or *so*.
- Add one detail a stranger couldn't guess, like a reason, an example or a number.

**Step five: count, old against new.** These are things you can see on the page and count with a
pencil:

| Count | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Sentences | How many |
| Paragraphs | How many blocks |
| Sentences starting with *I* | How many |
| Joining words | *and*, *but*, *because*, *so* inside a sentence |
| Details a stranger couldn't guess | A reason, an example or a number |

A good rewrite has fewer sentences starting with *I*, more joining words, more than one paragraph and at
least one detail per paragraph. Every fact still on your list is still on the page.

**Name the trap.** Your old page was right when you wrote it. The rewrite doesn't prove it was bad. And
the page is committed to the repository, so the day 20 rule still holds: no phone number, no full street
address, no document number.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the rewrite done with a pen on the old page**, as in the story. It's
tidier, but it's still a list of short sentences.

The second is **the rewrite that loses a fact**. You write something that sounds better and quietly drop
a fact that didn't fit. Check every note on your list against the new page.

The third is **the rewrite that isn't you**: long words you don't use, copied from somewhere else. A
stranger who meets you afterwards won't recognise the person on the page.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers keep old pages, like a profile, a cover letter or a bio, and rewrite them from the facts
when they need them again. They don't patch the old sentences, because those were written for a
different time.

Under pressure, when you need an old page tomorrow, the fact list is the fast part. Even a short
rewrite from the list beats a long edit of the old page.

A careful reader can't see your old page. They see only whether this one reads like one person talking
about themselves, or like a form.

## Check yourself

- **Write:** `TODO(me)` Take any short old page of yours, even five sentences. Write its fact list, cross
  out and add notes, turn the page over, and write one new paragraph from the list with a topic sentence,
  at least one joining word and one detail.
- **Say out loud:** Say one fact from your list, first as it was in the old sentence and then as it is in
  the new one.
