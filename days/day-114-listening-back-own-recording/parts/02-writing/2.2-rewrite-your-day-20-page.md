## One-line answer

Take your day 20 gate page, the ten sentences about you. Make its fact list, bring the list up to date,
put the page away, and write a new page from the list in paragraphs, with topic sentences, joining
words and details. Count both pages the same way. Save the new page as `lab/writing/day-114.md`, leave
`day-20.md` exactly as it is, and file the rewrite in the portfolio ledger.

## The story

Your friend's new *Tell us about yourself* page went with her application. A week later she had an
interview, and the first thing the interviewer said was: *You wrote that you help the older customers
with their prescriptions. Tell me about that.*

Her old page had said *I like my work.* Nobody could ask a question about that. The new page gave the
interviewer something to talk to her about, because it had a detail in it.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Revising Drafts](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/revising-drafts/): "Revision literally
means to "see again," to look at something from a fresh, critical perspective"; "THINK BIG, don't
tinker".

**Step one: find the old page.** Open `day-20.md` or the photo `day-20.jpg` in
`days/day-20-gate-self-introduction/lab/writing/`. **Don't change it.** Copy nothing out of it except
facts.

**Step two: the fact list.** One note per fact. Then bring it up to date.

| Fact from the old page, as a note | Still true? | New note, if it changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

Add as many rows as your page has facts. Then add at least two notes that are true now and weren't on
the old page.

**Step three: turn the old page over and group the notes.** Three or four groups. Each group becomes one
paragraph.

**Step four: write the new page.** From the list only. Each paragraph opens with a topic sentence, joins
facts that belong together with *and*, *but*, *because* or *so*, and has one detail a stranger couldn't
guess.

**Step five: count both pages.** With a pencil, on both.

| Count | Old page | New page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sentences | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| Paragraphs | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| Sentences starting with *I* | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| Joining words inside a sentence | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| Details a stranger couldn't guess | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| Facts from the list that are on the page | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**Step six: check it.** The new page should have more paragraphs, fewer sentences starting with *I*, more
joining words and at least one detail in every paragraph. Every fact on your up-to-date list should be
on it. If a count went the wrong way, rewrite that paragraph from its notes, not from its sentences.

**Step seven: save and file.** Save the new page as `lab/writing/day-114.md` in today's folder, or a
photo named `day-114.jpg`. Its row goes in `docs/WRITINGS.md` as a rewrite of day 20, and says what
changed. The day 20 page and its row stay exactly as they are.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **copying the old sentences back**, because the old page was open while
you wrote. Turn it over.

The second is **the page that grows but doesn't change**: the same ten sentences, with five more added
at the end. The counts show it: the same number of sentences starting with *I*, and one paragraph.

The third is **the old page edited in place**. Now the portfolio has no day 20 page, and nobody can see
what changed. The old file is never touched.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers keep old versions. When a page is rewritten for a new job or a new reader, the old one
stays in a folder, because one day it's useful to see how you used to say it.

Under pressure, when an interviewer or a new neighbour asks about you, the details on your new page are
the things you'll talk about. Choose details you're happy to be asked about.

A careful reader of a page about you looks for one thing to ask you. A page with no details gives them
nothing.
