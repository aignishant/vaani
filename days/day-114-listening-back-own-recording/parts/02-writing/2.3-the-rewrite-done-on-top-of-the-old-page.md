## One-line answer

Make a copy of your day 20 page and improve it the tempting way, with a pen, one sentence at a time,
keeping its sentences. Call it copy E, and label it deliberate. Put it next to your rewrite from part 2.2
and give both to a reader who doesn't know which is which. They say which one sounds like a person and
which one sounds like a form, and they write one question they'd ask the writer of each. Your rewrite
should win, and should give them a better question.

## The story

Your friend showed both versions of her *Tell us about yourself* page to her cousin, who hires people at
a bank. She didn't say which was which.

Her cousin read them both and put a finger on the edited one. *This one's fine. There's nothing wrong
with it. But I'd skip it. It reads like a form someone filled in.* Then she tapped the rewrite. *This one,
I'd call. I want to ask her about the older customers.*

Both pages had the same facts. Only one of them made a stranger want to ask a question.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **rewrite**, **fact list**: from [part 2.1](2.1-from-the-facts-not-the-sentences.md).
- **the old page**, **the new page**: from [part 2.2](2.2-rewrite-your-day-20-page.md).
- **line-by-line edit**: improving a page one sentence at a time, keeping its sentences and their order.
- **copy E**: a line-by-line edit of your day 20 page, made on purpose here.
- **blind read**: a reader reads two pages without knowing which is which.

| Copy | How it's made | What a reader should say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E, line-by-line edit (deliberate) | the old sentences, each one fixed | correct, but reads like a form |
| R, the rewrite from part 2.2 | new sentences from the fact list | reads like a person, and gives a question to ask |

## Why Vaani needs it

When you go back to an old page, the line-by-line edit is what your hand wants to do. It feels like work
and it looks tidier. This part lets a real reader show you what it doesn't fix, so you choose the rewrite
when a page has to do a new job.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Revising Drafts](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/revising-drafts/) says that when the ideas
and the organisation are weak, "proofreading will just be putting a band-aid on a bullet wound". It also
quotes "THINK BIG, don't tinker".

**Step one: make copy E.** Copy your day 20 page onto a new sheet, not over the original. Write
*deliberate* at the top. Improve it one sentence at a time: fix spellings, add a word, add a comma. Keep
every sentence and keep the order. Don't join sentences and don't add paragraphs.

**Step two: make them look alike.** Write or type copy E and your rewrite R the same way, in the same
hand or the same font, with no titles. Take *deliberate* off the reader's copy, but keep it on yours.

**Step three: the blind read.** Give both to a reader who doesn't know which is which. Ask:

| Question | Reader's answer |
| --- | --- |
| Which one sounds like a person talking about themselves, and which like a form? | `TODO(me)` |
| One question you'd ask the writer of the first page | `TODO(me)` |
| One question you'd ask the writer of the second page | `TODO(me)` |

Then tell them which was which.

**Step four: count.** Do the counts from [part 2.2](2.2-rewrite-your-day-20-page.md) on copy E as well,
beside R's.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if the reader says copy E sounds more like a
person, or if the reader can't write a question for R, or if R has as many sentences starting with *I* as
copy E. It's **GREEN** when the reader chooses R as the person, writes a question about a detail that's
only on R, and R's counts beat copy E's.

**Step five: the repair.** If it went RED, look at R again. Did you write it with the old page open? Does
every paragraph have a detail? Rewrite the weakest paragraph from its notes, and do the blind read again
with a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of this exercise is **a copy E that isn't honest**. If you make it badly on
purpose, the test proves nothing. Make it as good as a line-by-line edit can be.

The second is **a reader who can see which is which**: one page is typed and one is handwritten, or one
still says *deliberate*. Make them look alike.

The third, in real life, is **the old page sent again with small fixes**, because it felt like enough.
Every fact is correct, and it still gets skipped.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers edit line by line when the page is already right and only needs polish. They rewrite
when the page has a new job or a new reader. The skill is telling which one you need.

Under pressure, ask one question: *Does this page still do the job I need now?* If the answer is no,
don't reach for the pen. Reach for the fact list.

A careful reader never sees how much work went into a page. They only see whether it gives them a reason
to reply.
