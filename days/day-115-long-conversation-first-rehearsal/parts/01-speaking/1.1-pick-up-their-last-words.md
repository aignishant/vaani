## One-line answer

A long conversation keeps going when every turn does two jobs: **say something of your own**, then
**hand the turn back**. And when you ask, build the question from **something they just said**, not
from a list in your head. A question made from their last words keeps the thread. A new-topic question
starts the conversation again from nothing, so save it for when a thread has really run out, and bring
it in with *by the way* or *anyway*.

## The story

You're on a long train journey, and the woman beside you smiles, so you start talking.

> Where are you going? — Pune.
>
> What do you do? — I'm a teacher.
>
> Do you have children? — Two.

Three questions, three short answers. You can't think of a fourth, and the rest of the journey is
silence and the window.

On the way back, a man sits beside you and says he's going to see his daughter's exam results. This time
you pick up his words: *Her exam? What was it for?* Nursing, he says, and she almost gave up in the
first year. *Almost gave up? What happened?* He tells you. You tell him about your cousin, who nearly
did the same. Then you ask him something about the first year.

You got off at your station in the middle of a sentence.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **turn**: one person's part of a talk, and then it's the other person's turn, from
  [day 8](../../../day-08-greetings-and-goodbyes/parts/01-speaking/1.1-hello-and-how-are-you.md).
- **agree and add one thing**: answering with a little extra, so the other person has something to talk
  to, from [day 29](../../../day-29-weather-and-small-talk/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-line-about-nothing.md).
- **ask back**: returning the question, as in *And you?*, from
  [day 38](../../../day-38-hobbies-and-free-time/parts/01-speaking/1.2-say-how-often-and-ask-back.md).
- **follow-up question**: a question about something somebody just said, from
  [day 33](../../../day-33-your-job-or-studies/parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-answer-nobody-could-follow.md).
- **thread**: one topic that runs through several turns, like the daughter's exam in the story.
- **new-topic question**: a question that isn't about anything just said, and starts a new thread.
- **hand back**: ending your turn so it's clearly the other person's turn, usually with a question.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Turn-taking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn-taking) says there are "two types of techniques: those
where the current speaker selects the next speaker, and those where the next speaker selects themself."
A question is the plainest way to select the next speaker.
[Keeping a conversation going](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b1/keeping-conversation-going)
uses phrases including "How about you, Noelia?", "Really? I didn't know that!", "Anyway …" and "By the
way, I wanted to ask you ...".

**Every turn, two jobs.**

| Job | What it sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| Say something of your own | Your answer, and one thing added, as on day 29 |
| Hand the turn back | A question, or *How about you?*, with your voice going up |

A turn with only the first job is a small speech: the other person has to find their own way in. A turn
with only the second job is an interview: you're asking, and giving nothing.

**Build the question from their last words.** Listen for one word or idea in what they just said, and
ask about that.

> Her exam? What was it for?
>
> You said you moved last year. Why?
>
> Really? What was that like?

The first two repeat a word before the question. That tells them *I was listening*, and it gives you a
moment to think.

**When a thread runs out.** You'll know, because the answers get short. Only then ask a new-topic
question, and mark it so it doesn't sound like a form:

> Anyway, how do you know the people here?
>
> By the way, I wanted to ask you about the new market.

**Name the trap.** Many learners prepare a list of questions before a conversation, because it feels
safe. A list is useful for the first question and for when a thread runs out. In between, it stops you
listening, because you're waiting for your next question instead of hearing their answer.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the form**: new-topic questions one after another, as in the story.
*Where are you from? What do you do? Do you like cricket?* Each answer is short, and the silence arrives
quickly.

The second is **the speech with no hand-back**. You say a lot, well, and then stop with your voice going
down. The other person doesn't know whether it's their turn or whether you'll carry on.

The third is **the follow-up about something they didn't say**: *So you like your job?* after they said
nothing about liking it. It sounds as though you weren't listening.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers do this without thinking. Most of their questions in a long conversation are about the
last thing the other person said, and they change topic with a small word like *anyway*.

Under pressure, when you can't think of anything to say, don't look for a new topic. Repeat their last
few words as a question: *Almost gave up?* It almost always gets a longer answer.

A native listener feels a follow-up question as interest. They feel a string of new-topic questions as
being checked, like at an office counter.
