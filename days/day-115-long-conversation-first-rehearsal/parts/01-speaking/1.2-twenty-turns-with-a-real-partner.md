## One-line answer

Record a real conversation with a partner, on a topic drawn from slips so that neither of you prepared
it, and go on for at least twenty turns between you. No list of questions. Then mark every question you
asked as a follow-up or a new topic, count roughly how many words your partner said after each one, and
count your share of the turns. This is the first rehearsal. It's allowed to be rough, and it's the one
you'll measure the next one against.

## The story

On a later journey, you and the man from the exam story ended up on the same train again. He waved you
over.

This time neither of you had a first line ready. He started with the weather, and you picked up *rain*
and asked about his village. You talked about the river there, then about flooding, then about his
brother, who fixes boats. When a thread got short, one of you said *anyway* and started another.

Afterwards you realised you'd asked him maybe ten questions, and he'd asked you about as many. Neither of
you had made a single one up in advance.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Turn-taking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn-taking): "The end of a TCU is a point where the turn may
end and a new speaker may begin, known as a transition relevance place or TRP." In plain words, there
are natural places where a turn can pass to the other person. A question is one of the clearest.
[Keeping a conversation going](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b1/keeping-conversation-going)
uses "Anyway …" and "By the way, I wanted to ask you ..." to move to a new thread.

**Step one: the partner.** Someone who can hold a conversation in English: a friend, a colleague, a
neighbour, a family member. Ask them to speak normally, and to ask you things too. Ask them if they're
happy to be recorded.

**Step two: the topic.** Each of you writes three everyday topics on slips of paper, such as food,
travel, work, a festival or a place you both know. Fold them, mix them, and draw one. Nobody prepares.

**Step three: record.** Start the recorder and talk. Keep going for at least twenty turns between you.
Count them afterwards, not during. Don't write any questions down first. Pick up their last words, hand
every turn back, and change thread with *anyway* or *by the way* only when a thread runs out.

This is this day's rehearsal size. The gate's own wording is in the
[plan's §7](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), and this part doesn't restate it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a conversation you secretly prepared**: you drew the topic, then wrote
five questions in your head before pressing record. The counts will show mostly N.

The second is **the partner who does all the asking**. Your share of the turns is fine, but every one of
them is an answer. Hand some back.

The third is **stopping at twenty turns exactly**, in the middle of a thread. Twenty is the least, not the
end. Finish the thread.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers can't count their turns, but they feel when a conversation is one-sided, and they fix it
by asking more or by saying more.

Under pressure, with a partner who answers in one word, don't ask more new-topic questions. Say
something of your own about their one word, and then ask: *Two? I've got one, and that's enough work.
Are they close in age?*

A native listener to your recording hears the balance straight away. Two people asking each other things
sounds like a conversation. One asking and one answering sounds like an interview.
