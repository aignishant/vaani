## One-line answer

Record two short conversations with a partner, on two different drawn topics. In take Q, on purpose, ask
only questions from a list you wrote beforehand, with no follow-ups. In take F, ask one question from
the list and then only follow-ups. Count how many words your partner says after each question, and ask
them which one felt like a conversation. Take F should get longer answers and feel like talk. If it
doesn't, this part is RED.

## The story

On one journey you watched an older man question the young student beside him. *Name? Which village?
What are you studying? What will you earn? Married?* He had a list, and he went down it.

The student answered each one in a word or two. After the fifth question he took out his earphones and
put them in.

The older man wasn't rude. He was interested. But he never asked a second question about anything the
student said, so it felt like filling in a form at an office counter.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **follow-up question**, **new-topic question**, **thread**, **hand back**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-pick-up-their-last-words.md).
- **turn share**: from [part 1.2](1.2-twenty-turns-with-a-real-partner.md).
- **take Q**: a take where you ask only prepared new-topic questions. You make it on purpose here.
- **take F**: a take where, after one opening question, you ask only follow-ups.
- **answer length**: roughly how many words your partner says after one question.

| Take | How it's made | What should happen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q, the form (deliberate) | six questions from a list, no follow-ups | short answers; feels like a form |
| F, the thread | one opening question, then five follow-ups | longer answers; feels like talk |

## Why Vaani needs it

When you're nervous about a recorded conversation, a prepared list is the first thing you'll reach for.
This part makes you hear, with numbers from a real partner, what the list does to their answers, so you
trust the follow-up in the conversation that counts.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Turn-taking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn-taking): there are techniques "where the current speaker
selects the next speaker, and those where the next speaker selects themself." Both takes select the next
speaker with a question. Only one of them gives the partner something to say.
[Keeping a conversation going](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b1/keeping-conversation-going)
uses "Really? I didn't know that!" as a reply that shows interest before the next question.

**Step one: the list.** Write six new-topic questions about a drawn topic. Write *deliberate* at the top.

**Step two: take Q.** Record a short conversation with your partner. Ask the six questions in order.
After each answer, say something short like *okay* and go to the next question. No follow-ups.

**Step three: take F.** Draw a different topic. Record a short conversation with the same partner. Ask
one opening question. After that, ask five questions, each built from their last words.

**Step four: count and ask.** For each take, write roughly how many words your partner said after each
question. Then ask your partner the question in the table.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a partner who knows what you're testing**, and so gives
long answers in take F to be kind. Tell them only that you're practising conversations. Explain after.

The second is **a take F where every follow-up is *why?***. It gets long answers at first, and then it
starts to feel like being questioned by a teacher. Mix them: *What was that like?*, *Almost gave up?*,
*And then?*

The third, in real life, is **the form with a smile**: friendly questions, one after another, and a
partner who gets quieter and quieter.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers do keep a question or two ready for the start and for when a thread runs out. That's
fine. What they don't do is go down the list while the other person is still in the middle of a story.

Under pressure, when your partner goes quiet, it's usually because your last question had nothing to do
with their last answer. Go back to what they said last and ask about that.

A native listener to a recorded conversation can tell within a few turns whether the two people are
listening to each other. The follow-up question is how they tell.
