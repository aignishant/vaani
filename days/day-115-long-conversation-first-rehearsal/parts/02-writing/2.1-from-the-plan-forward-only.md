## One-line answer

A first draft turns your essay plan into sentences, **from the first paragraph to the last, without going
back**. Keep the plan beside you, and start each body paragraph with the topic sentence you already wrote
in it. When a word or a fact is missing, write a **bracket** like *[word?]* or *[number]* and keep going.
Don't fix anything until the conclusion is written. A whole rough draft can be improved. A perfect first
paragraph with nothing after it can't.

## The story

Your friend was asked to give a short speech at her brother's wedding. She had a plan: three stories
about him, and a line to finish.

She started writing the opening, and it wasn't quite right, so she wrote it again. And again. Every
evening she read the opening, changed a few words and felt good about it. On the wedding day she had one
beautiful opening paragraph, and she said the rest from memory, with shaking hands.

For her cousin's wedding she did it differently. She wrote the whole speech, top to bottom, in one
sitting. Where she couldn't remember a detail she wrote *[the name of that bike?]* and carried on. The
next day she had a whole speech, full of rough sentences and brackets, and fixing it was easy.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **draft**: a first version of a piece of writing, written to be changed, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-the-page.md).
- **essay**, **thesis**, **essay plan**: a piece of several paragraphs that answers one question; your
  answer to it in one sentence; and the notes made before writing, from
  [day 99](../../../day-99-full-talk-structure/parts/02-writing/2.1-question-answer-three-points.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, which says what the paragraph is about, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
  Your plan already has one for each body paragraph.
- **introduction**: the first paragraph, which goes from general to your answer, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/02-writing/2.1-general-to-your-answer.md).
- **body paragraph**: a middle paragraph making one point, built as point, explain, example, link, from
  [day 102](../../../day-102-idioms-you-hear/parts/02-writing/2.1-point-explain-example-link.md).
- **conclusion**: the last paragraph, which answers the question again and says why it matters, from
  [day 103](../../../day-103-joke-or-anecdote/parts/02-writing/2.1-answer-again-say-why-it-matters.md).
- **first draft**: the first time the whole essay exists in sentences.
- **forward only**: writing from the start to the end without going back to change what's written.
- **gap bracket**: a bracket in a draft that marks something missing, such as *[word?]* or *[example]*.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate asks for a five-paragraph essay, and day 116 is its second draft. A second draft needs a
whole first draft to work on. Most essays that never get finished die in the first paragraph, while the
writer is still polishing it.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Freewriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freewriting) describes a way of writing in which "The writer
writes with no regards to spelling or grammar, and makes no corrections."
[Brainstorming](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/brainstorming/) says of the same method:
"Word must follow word, no matter the relevance", and "The crucial point is that you keep on writing even
if you believe you are saying nothing." A first draft borrows that habit of never stopping, but it
follows your plan, so it doesn't wander.

**Before you start.** Put the plan where you can see it without turning a page. Write the question at
the top of the draft.

**Write in this order, forward only.**

| Paragraph | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- |
| 1, the introduction | Start general, and end with your thesis, copied from the plan |
| 2, 3, 4, the body | Start each one with its topic sentence, copied from the plan; then explain, example, link |
| 5, the conclusion | The plan's conclusion line, then why it matters |

**Gap brackets.** When something is missing, don't stop. Mark it and keep writing.

| What's missing | What you write |
| --- | --- |
| A word you don't know in English | *[the word in your language]* or *[word?]* |
| A fact or a number you'd have to look up | *[number]*, *[check]* |
| An example you can't think of yet | *[example]* |

**Don't go back.** No rereading the paragraph before, no crossing out more than a word you've just
written, no fixing spelling. If you see a mistake, leave it. The second draft is for that.

**Name the trap.** When a sentence comes to you in your first language, don't translate it. Write the
key word in a bracket and say the idea in the plainest English you have. And this course writes essays
without contractions. If one slips in, leave it for the second draft.

**When the conclusion is written, count.** Paragraphs, words and brackets. The phase 6 gate's length is
in the [plan's §7](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases). A first draft that is short is
still a first draft. The count tells you where to add.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the polished first paragraph**, as in the story, with nothing after it.

The second is **the stop to look it up**. You leave the draft to find one word, come back and can't
remember where the sentence was going. A bracket costs nothing.

The third is **the draft that forgets the plan**. It's written forward, but the plan is in a drawer, so
the three body paragraphs make points the plan never had.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers' first drafts are rougher than most learners imagine. They're full of brackets, repeated
words and sentences that will be cut. What they have is the whole shape, early.

Under pressure, with a report or an application due, write the whole rough version first, even very
rough. A finished rough draft can be made good. A good half can't be made finished in the same way.

A careful reader never sees your first draft. They see the one after it, and it's only as good as the
first draft was whole.
