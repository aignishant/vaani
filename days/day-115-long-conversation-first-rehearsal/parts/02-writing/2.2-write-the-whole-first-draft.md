## One-line answer

Choose a real essay question and its plan, and write the whole five-paragraph essay forward only, with
gap brackets wherever something is missing. Then, and only then, count each paragraph: words, brackets,
and whether it opens with the sentence from the plan. Label it *Draft 1* and save it as it is. Don't fix
it today. The brackets and the rough sentences are tomorrow's work.

## The story

Your friend's whole wedding speech, written in one go, had eleven brackets in it. Some said *[name?]*.
One said *[the funny part about the scooter, make it shorter]*. One sentence was so long she couldn't
read it aloud.

She didn't touch any of it that night. She counted the stories, and there were three, as in her plan.
She counted the brackets, and there were eleven, so there were eleven things to do the next day. For the
first time, the speech was a thing that existed, and not a thing she was going to write.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **fixing while you count**. You see a mistake while counting words, fix it,
and then fix the next, and the draft becomes a half-edited mix. Counting is only counting.

The second is **the draft with no brackets at all**, because you stopped to look everything up. Either it
took you away from the page many times, or you wrote around what you didn't know with vague words like
*things* and *good*. Vague words are gaps that nobody marked.

The third is **a question with no real answer**: you chose a question you don't have an opinion on, so the
body paragraphs have nothing to say. Choose a question you'd argue about with a friend.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers keep their first drafts, labelled, and never send them. A draft that says *Draft 1* at the
top can be as rough as it needs to be, because nobody will mistake it for the real thing.

Under pressure, with a piece that has to be good, a finished rough draft is the moment the pressure
drops. Everything after it is improving, not starting.

A careful reader of the final essay will never know how many brackets Draft 1 had. They'll only notice
whether the essay feels whole.
