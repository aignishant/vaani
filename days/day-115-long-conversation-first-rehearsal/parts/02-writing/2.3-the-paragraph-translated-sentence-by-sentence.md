## One-line answer

Take one body paragraph from your plan and write it a second way, on purpose: first in your own
language, then translated into English one sentence at a time. Call it copy T, and label it
deliberate. Put it beside the same paragraph from your Draft 1, copy D, written forward in English. A
reader who doesn't know which is which marks every sentence they had to read twice, and says each
paragraph's point in one sentence. Copy D should get fewer marks and a clearer point. If it doesn't, this
part is RED.

## The story

Your friend wrote one part of her wedding speech in her own language first, because the feelings came
more easily that way. Then she translated it, sentence by sentence. One sentence came out as:

> My brother is having a very big heart which is always opening for everyone.

Every word was a real English word, and every word was there in the sentence she'd written in her own
language. The guests smiled politely and looked at each other. Her other stories, written straight in
simple English, got the laughs.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **first draft**, **forward only**, **gap bracket**: from [part 2.1](2.1-from-the-plan-forward-only.md).
- **Draft 1**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-the-whole-first-draft.md).
- **first language**: the language you grew up speaking.
- **translated sentence by sentence**: written first in your first language, then turned into English one
  sentence at a time, keeping its words and its order.
- **copy T**: one body paragraph translated sentence by sentence. You make it on purpose here.
- **copy D**: the same body paragraph from your Draft 1.
- **read-twice mark**: a wavy line a reader puts under any sentence they had to read twice.

| Copy | How it's made | What a reader should find |
| --- | --- | --- |
| T, translated (deliberate) | first language first, then English sentence by sentence | more read-twice marks; the point harder to say |
| D, from Draft 1 | forward only, in English, from the plan | fewer marks; the point easy to say |

## Why Vaani needs it

When the ideas are hard, your first language feels like the safe place to think. This course allows
that for thinking. This part shows you, with a real reader, what happens when your first language writes
the sentences too, so you use the gap bracket instead.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Freewriting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freewriting): "The writer writes with no regards to spelling
or grammar, and makes no corrections."
[Brainstorming](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/brainstorming/): "Word must follow word, no
matter the relevance." Both are about writing forward. Neither page says anything about translating, and
this part doesn't claim they do. What it tests is what happens to *your* sentences, with a real reader.
[Stative verbs](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/stative-verbs) confirms the
grammar in the story: stative verbs "aren't usually used in the present continuous form", with the
examples "I have an old car. (state – possession)" and "I'm having a quick break. (action – having a
break is an activity)".

**Step one: choose the paragraph.** Take one body paragraph from your plan, the one with the most ideas
in it.

**Step two: make copy T.** Write *deliberate* at the top. Write the paragraph in your first language,
from the plan's notes. Then translate it into English one sentence at a time, keeping each sentence's
words and order as close as you can. Don't use any translating tool. This is your own translation.

**Step three: make them look alike.** Copy T and copy D in the same hand or the same font, with no
labels on the reader's copy.

**Step four: the blind read.** Give both to someone who reads English well and doesn't know which is
which. Ask them to put a wavy line under every sentence they had to read twice. Then ask them to say each
paragraph's point in one sentence.

**Step five: the repair.** If it went RED, look at the sentences in copy D with a wavy line. Were they
translated in your head as you wrote? Rewrite each one from the plan's note in the plainest English you
have, with a gap bracket for the word you were missing. Try the blind read again with a new reader.

Copy T stays in your lab, labelled deliberate. It isn't part of your essay.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version, in real life, is **the sentence that follows the first language's order**:
*My brother is having a very big heart.* Every word is English, and the sentence isn't. *Have* for
something you own or are is a state, so English says *My brother has a big heart.*

The second is **the long chain**: one sentence in the first language, which carries four ideas joined by
*which* and *and*, becomes one long English sentence nobody can read once.

The third is **the word that's right in the dictionary and wrong in the sentence**, chosen because it
was the first English word listed for the word in your language.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers who speak two languages still think in either one. What they don't do is write sentences
in one language and then carry them across. They take the idea and say it again, from scratch, in the
language of the page.

Under pressure, when an idea only comes to you in your first language, write it in a bracket in that
language, and then write the simplest English sentence you can that says the same thing. Leave the
bracket for the second draft.

A careful reader can't tell which language you thought in. They can only tell which sentences they had
to read twice.
