## §1 Where we are

On your aunt's veranda the talk ran out, and you both looked at your tea. Then you remembered something
she'd said in passing, about the mango tree, and asked about it. She laughed and told you the whole
story. The speaking folder is about the stall and the loose end: noticing what the other person mentions
and moves past, and going back to it when a thread runs out. You record a second rehearsal of at least
thirty turns and measure it against the first, then let a listener compare a take where every stall sits
with one where every stall is restarted.

Your friend's wedding speech had three stories. When she wrote each one's point in the margin, two of
them were doing the same job, and the point she'd planned was missing. She fixed that before she touched
a sentence. Then she filled her brackets with real names and real things that happened. The writing
folder is about the second draft: margin notes against the plan, brackets filled with something specific,
thin paragraphs made whole, and sentences last. A reader then compares your specific paragraph with one
filled with vague words.

Choose either folder first. Each stands on its own. Follow the parts in that folder's order.

## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** In [part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-stall-with-no-way-back.md), a listener hears take S
and take L in a hidden order. RED is when they choose take S as the one where two people have been
listening to each other, or can't name anything the speaker remembered in take L. GREEN is when they
choose take L and name a back-link from it.

**Writing:** In [part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-the-bracket-filled-with-a-vague-word.md), a reader marks
every place they'd ask *like what?* in copy V and copy S. RED is when copy S gets as many marks or more,
or the reader believes copy V more. GREEN is when copy S has fewer marks and is believed more.

Only record what you actually heard or saw. A deliberate exercise is labelled deliberate.

## §7 Traps

- Letting a stall sit and waiting for the partner to restart it.
- A cold new-topic question at every stall.
- A back-link with no *you said earlier*, so it sounds like a quiz.
- A back-link to something the partner didn't say.
- The same easy partner as day 115.
- Starting a second draft with the sentences.
- Margin notes copied from the plan instead of read from the paragraph.
- Brackets filled by guessing, or with vague words.
- Words added to the introduction to reach the length.
- Draft 1 written over.

## §9 Say it out loud

After your practice, tell a friend what you did. Use this only when it is true: "I rehearsed a long
conversation for the second time, longer than the first, with someone I don't know so well. The new
thing was the stalls. When a topic ran out, instead of asking something new, I went back to something
they'd mentioned earlier and said *you said earlier …*. I compared my counts with the first rehearsal.
Then I did one conversation where I just let every stall sit, on purpose, and a listener could hear
which one sounded interested. In writing, I made the second draft of my essay. I wrote each paragraph's
point in the margin first and fixed the ones that didn't match my plan. Then I filled every bracket with
something real, a name or a number, and only then fixed sentences. A reader compared a paragraph full of
vague words with my real one, and kept asking *like what?* about the vague one." If you did only one
folder, say only that part.
