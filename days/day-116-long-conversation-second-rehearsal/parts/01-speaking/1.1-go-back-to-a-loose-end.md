## One-line answer

In a long conversation, the other person mentions things that nobody follows up. Those are **loose
ends**. Keep them in your head. When a thread runs out, don't reach for a new topic first. Go back to a
loose end: *You said earlier that …* or *Going back to …*. Use *speaking of …* for something related
to what was just said. A partner who hears their own words come back knows you've been listening all
along, and they already have something to say about it.

## The story

You go to see your old aunt, whom you visit once a year. You sit with her on the veranda.

Early on, while talking about the weather, she says in passing: *The mango tree gave nothing this year,
not after what your uncle did to it.* You didn't ask. The conversation moved on to your cousins, then to
her knees, then to the price of vegetables. Then it stopped. She looked at the street. You looked at your
tea.

Last year you would have asked *So, how is the neighbourhood?*, and got *Same as always.* This year you
remembered the tree. *You said earlier, about the mango tree. What did Uncle do to it?*

She laughed, put her cup down, and told you the whole story. Then she asked about your job.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **turn**, **follow-up question**, **thread**, **new-topic question**, **hand back**: from
  [day 115](../../../day-115-long-conversation-first-rehearsal/parts/01-speaking/1.1-pick-up-their-last-words.md).
  A **thread** is one topic that runs through several turns. A **new-topic question** isn't about
  anything just said.
- **loose end**: something the other person mentioned that nobody asked about, like the mango tree.
- **back-link**: a question or comment that goes back to a loose end from earlier in the conversation.
- **stall**: the moment a thread runs out and nobody knows what to say next.

## Why Vaani needs it

A recorded conversation that runs long enough for the phase 6 gate will stall. Every long conversation
does. Day 115 taught you to keep a thread going. This part is what you do when one ends: a back-link
restarts the talk without sounding like a form, and a listener to the recording hears two people who
remember what the other said.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[speaking of](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/speaking_of) gives: "Speaking of which; relatedly, used to
introduce a related topic."
[Turn-taking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn-taking) says there are "two types of techniques: those
where the current speaker selects the next speaker, and those where the next speaker selects themself."
At a stall, nobody has been selected. Whoever speaks first chooses the next thread.

**Step one: hear the loose ends.** While your partner talks, notice anything they mention and then move
past: a person, a place, a problem, an *after what happened*. You don't need to write it down. Just
notice it.

**Step two: let the thread finish.** Don't jump to a loose end in the middle of a thread that's still
going. That's a new topic, however you say it.

**Step three: at the stall, go back.** Say where you're going back to, then ask:

> You said earlier that the tree gave nothing. What happened?
>
> Going back to your knees, did the doctor say anything?
>
> Speaking of prices, is the market still on Thursdays?

*You said earlier* and *going back to* point to something from before. *Speaking of* points to something
just said, and turns it towards a related topic.

**Step four: a new topic only if there's no loose end.** Then *anyway* or *by the way*, from day 115.

| At a stall, you have … | You say |
| --- | --- |
| a loose end from earlier | *You said earlier …* or *Going back to …* |
| something related to what was just said | *Speaking of …* |
| nothing at all | *Anyway …* or *By the way …* and a new-topic question |

**Name the trap.** Many learners go back to a loose end with a question that sounds like a quiz: *What
about the tree?* Without *you said earlier*, the partner doesn't know why you're asking, and it can
sound like you're changing the subject. Say where it came from.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the stall left to sit**: *Okay. … Yes.* Nobody chooses the next thread,
and the partner fills the silence with something polite, or ends the conversation.

The second is **the cold new topic at every stall**: *So, what do you think about the new road?* It
works once. Three times, and the conversation is a form again.

The third is **the back-link to something they didn't say**: *You said you were worried about money.*
They didn't. Go back only to their own words.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers do this all the time without noticing: *oh, you mentioned your brother earlier …* It's
one of the ways people show they've been listening, and it's warmer than any new question.

Under pressure, when you're nervous and can't remember any loose ends, go back to the first thing they
said in the conversation. People almost always say something about themselves at the start.

A native listener feels a back-link as attention. It's often the moment a polite conversation turns
into a real one.
