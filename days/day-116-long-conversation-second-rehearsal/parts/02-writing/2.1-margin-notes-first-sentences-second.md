## One-line answer

A second draft works from big to small. First, write each paragraph's main point **in the margin**, in
fewer than ten words, and put the notes beside your plan. Fix any paragraph whose note is missing,
doubled or off the plan. Second, fill every gap bracket with something **specific**. Third, make thin
paragraphs whole. Only then touch the sentences. Spelling and commas wait for your proofreading
checklist. Keep Draft 1 and save the new one as Draft 2.

## The story

Your friend's wedding speech, Draft 1, had three stories and eleven brackets. The next day she started
fixing the first sentence, stopped herself, and did something else instead.

Next to each story she wrote, in the margin, what it was *for*. Story one: *he's generous*. Story two:
*he's funny*. Story three: *he's funny*. Two stories were doing the same job, and nothing showed that he
was brave, which was the whole point of her third planned story. She'd drifted while writing forward.

She swapped story three for the one about the night of the flood. Then she filled the brackets. Only
after that did she fix a single sentence.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **first draft**, **gap bracket**, **Draft 1**: the first time the whole essay exists in sentences; a
  bracket marking something missing; and its label, from
  [day 115](../../../day-115-long-conversation-first-rehearsal/parts/02-writing/2.1-from-the-plan-forward-only.md).
- **essay plan**: notes made before writing, showing the answer and what each paragraph will say, from
  [day 99](../../../day-99-full-talk-structure/parts/02-writing/2.1-question-answer-three-points.md).
- **body paragraph**: a middle paragraph making one point, built as point, explain, example, link, from
  [day 102](../../../day-102-idioms-you-hear/parts/02-writing/2.1-point-explain-example-link.md).
- **edit** and **second read**: changing a page so it does its job better, and reading it after a gap as
  a stranger, from
  [day 78](../../../day-78-interrupting-politely/parts/02-writing/2.1-wait-then-read-as-a-stranger.md).
- **proofreading checklist**: your own list of questions for the last check, one per pass, from
  [day 113](../../../day-113-self-correction-while-speaking/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-question-one-pass.md).
- **second draft**: the version made by changing Draft 1 from big to small. Its label is **Draft 2**.
- **margin note**: a paragraph's main point, in fewer than ten words, written beside it.
- **reverse outline**: all the margin notes of a draft, read in order, as if they were a plan.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate essay is proofread against your day 113 checklist, but proofreading can't fix a
paragraph that makes the wrong point. The second draft is where the essay becomes the one you planned.
Day 119 writes the final essay, and it's only as good as the drafts behind it.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Reverse Outlining](https://writingcenter.gmu.edu/writing-resources/writing-as-process/reverse-outlining)
says: "Reverse outlining is simply making an outline AFTER an essay has been written." It says: "For
every paragraph, write the main idea or main argument in a small number of words in the margin next to
that paragraph." And: "If you are finding more than one main idea, you will need to break the paragraph
up into two or more paragraphs."

**Pass one: the margin notes.** Read Draft 1 one paragraph at a time. Beside each, write its main point
in fewer than ten words. Write what the paragraph *actually* says, not what you planned it to say.

**Then compare them with the plan.**

| What the margin shows | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| The note matches the plan | Nothing yet |
| No note: you can't say the point | Rewrite the paragraph from the plan's topic sentence |
| Two notes: two points in one paragraph | Split it, or cut the point that isn't in the plan |
| The same note on two paragraphs | Cut one, and write the missing planned point |
| A note that isn't in the plan | Cut it, or change the plan if it's better, and then the thesis too |

**Pass two: the brackets.** Fill every gap bracket with something specific: the real word, the real
number, a real example that happened. If you can't fill one, cut the sentence it's in.

**Pass three: the thin paragraphs.** A body paragraph without an explanation or an example is thin. Add
what's missing, from the plan's notes.

**Pass four: the sentences.** Now read each sentence once. Rewrite any sentence you had to read twice.
Don't proofread yet. That's your checklist's job, later.

**Name the trap.** A second draft that starts with the sentences never reaches the structure, because the
sentences always need something. Start in the margin.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the second draft that's a proofread**: spellings fixed, commas added,
brackets filled, and a paragraph that makes the wrong point still standing.

The second is **the margin note that repeats the plan** instead of reading the paragraph. The note says
what you meant, so nothing ever looks wrong.

The third is **Draft 1 written over**. Now you can't see what changed, or go back if the new version is
worse. Keep both.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers spend most of a second draft on structure: moving, cutting and adding whole paragraphs.
They leave the small things for last, because a sentence polished today may be cut tomorrow.

Under pressure, with only one round of changes possible, do the margin notes. A paragraph in the wrong
place costs a reader more than any spelling mistake.

A careful reader can't see your margin notes. They feel them as an essay where every paragraph knows
what it's for.
