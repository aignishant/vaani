## One-line answer

Take your Draft 1 from day 115. Write its margin notes and put them beside the plan. Fix the structure
first, then fill every bracket with something specific, then make thin paragraphs whole, then fix the
sentences you had to read twice. Count before and after. Label the result *Draft 2* and save it next to
Draft 1, which stays exactly as it was.

## The story

Your friend's Draft 2 of the speech was shorter than Draft 1 in one place and longer in another. The
second funny story had gone. The flood story was new. Every bracket was gone. Where Draft 1 said *[name
of that bike?]*, Draft 2 said *the old blue scooter he called Rani*.

She read the margin notes of Draft 2 aloud to her mother: *generous, funny, brave, and we love him*. Her
mother said, *Yes, that's him.*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **filling the table from memory** instead of reading each paragraph. The
notes all match, because they're the plan.

The second is **words added to the introduction** to reach the length. The introduction gets long, and
the body paragraphs are still thin.

The third is **a bracket filled by guessing** a number or a name you should have checked. A specific
detail that's wrong is worse than a vague one. Check it, or cut the sentence.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers keep every draft. When a reader asks *why did you change this?*, the drafts are the
answer.

Under pressure, if you can do only one step today, do the margin notes and fix the one paragraph that's
off the plan.

A careful reader of your final essay will notice specific details more than anything else. A name, a
number or a real example is what makes them believe you.
