## One-line answer

Take one body paragraph from Draft 2 and make a second copy of it, on purpose, with every place you
filled a bracket filled with a vague word instead: *many things*, *a lot of people*, *it was very good*.
Call it copy V, and label it deliberate. Give copy V and your real paragraph, copy S, to a reader who
doesn't know which is which. They mark every place they want to ask *like what?* or *who?* Copy S should
get fewer marks. If it doesn't, this part is RED.

## The story

The first time your friend's cousin gave a wedding speech, he'd filled his brackets in a hurry:

> He was always a good friend, and many funny things happened. He helped a lot of people.

The guests nodded and waited. Nobody laughed, because there was nothing to laugh at.

Your friend's speech said: *On his first day with the scooter, he rode it straight into the vegetable
cart outside our gate, and then paid for every tomato.* The room laughed, and then it clapped.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **gap bracket**, **Draft 2**, **specific**: from [part 2.2](2.2-make-draft-2.md).
- **vague word**: a word that could fit almost any essay, like *things*, *many*, *good*, *a lot*,
  *stuff*.
- **copy V**: a body paragraph with its brackets filled by vague words. You make it on purpose here.
- **copy S**: the same paragraph from Draft 2, with specific fills.
- **like-what mark**: a mark a reader puts where they'd want to ask *like what?* or *who?*
- **blind read**: a reader reads two pages without knowing which is which, from
  [day 114](../../../day-114-listening-back-own-recording/parts/02-writing/2.3-the-rewrite-done-on-top-of-the-old-page.md).

| Copy | How it's made | What a reader should find |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V, vague (deliberate) | every filled bracket filled with a vague word | many like-what marks |
| S, specific | the same paragraph from Draft 2 | few like-what marks |

## Why Vaani needs it

A vague word is a gap bracket that nobody marked. It hides the gap so well that your second draft can't
find it. This part lets a reader show you where vague words sit, so the final essay has none.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Reverse Outlining](https://writingcenter.gmu.edu/writing-resources/writing-as-process/reverse-outlining):
"If you find that there is no main idea, then your paragraph is probably just "fluff" or unnecessary—or
might need some connections or clarity." A paragraph of vague words is where that happens.

**Step one: choose the paragraph.** The body paragraph in Draft 2 that had the most brackets in Draft 1.

**Step two: make copy V.** Write *deliberate* at the top. Copy the paragraph, but wherever you filled a
bracket, put a vague word instead of the specific one. Change nothing else.

**Step three: make them look alike.** Same hand or same font, no labels on the reader's copy.

**Step four: the blind read.** Give both to a reader. Ask them to put a mark wherever they'd want to ask
*like what?* or *who?*, and then to say which paragraph they believe more.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if copy S gets as many like-what marks as copy V,

**Step five: the repair.** If it went RED, look at every mark on copy S. Each one is a vague word you
didn't know was there. Replace it with a name, a number or a thing that happened, and try a new reader.
Then search the rest of Draft 2 for the same vague words.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a copy V that changes more than the brackets**, so the
reader is comparing two different paragraphs. Change only the filled places.

The second is **a copy S that still has vague words** you never bracketed in Draft 1. That's the most
useful result of all. Those words were hiding.

The third, in real life, is **the essay of *many* and *very***. Every sentence is correct, and the reader
believes none of it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers use vague words on purpose sometimes, when the detail doesn't matter. What they don't do
is use one where the reader needs to see something.

Under pressure, when you can't find the specific word, a short concrete example works better than a
general sentence: not *he helped many people*, but *he drove our neighbour to the hospital twice*.

A careful reader trusts details. One real name or number in a paragraph does more than three adjectives.
