## One-line answer

Record one more take of the same gate piece, and this time change the task on purpose: make it shorter,
put it on a topic you find easier, or drop the hardest requirement. Call it take M, and label it
deliberate. Give a helper the gate's wording from the plan and nothing else. They listen to take M and
your kept retake in a hidden order and check each against the requirements. Take M should fail and your
retake should pass. If your retake misses any requirement, or the helper can't tell that take M fails,
this part is RED.

## The story

The neighbour's daughter had a friend applying to the same college. Her friend redrew her *drawing from
memory* too, but she found drawing from memory frightening. So she drew her own street from a photo on
her phone, very carefully. It was her best drawing by far.

The college sent it back with one line: *This is not a drawing from memory.* It was a better drawing,
and it didn't count, because it had answered a different question.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **requirement**, **its own rules**: from [part 1.1](1.1-weakest-by-its-own-gate.md).
- **portfolio retake**: from [part 1.2](1.2-retake-it-under-its-own-rules.md).
- **the kept retake**: the retake you kept in part 1.2.
- **take M**: a take of the same piece with the task changed. You make it on purpose here.
- **blind listen**: a listener hears takes in an order they don't know, from
  [day 114](../../../day-114-listening-back-own-recording/parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-retake-that-fixed-everything.md).

| Take | How it's made | What a helper with the gate wording should find |
| --- | --- | --- |
| M, the changed task (deliberate) | shorter, easier, or a requirement dropped | at least one requirement missed |
| the kept retake | same task, same rules | every requirement met |

## Why Vaani needs it

A retake that changes the task often sounds better than the original. That's exactly why it's
dangerous. This part shows you, with a helper who has only the gate's wording, that *sounding better*
and *meeting the gate* are different tests.

## The rule

**The reference for this part is the plan itself**:
[§7, the phases](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases). The helper reads the gate's wording
there, not your requirement list, so that your list gets checked too. No outside page is needed for this
rule, and none is cited.

**Step one: make take M.** Write *deliberate* on a slip of paper and keep it by you. Record the same gate
piece with one change to the task. Choose one:

| Change | Example of what it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Shorter | fewer sentences, turns or words than the gate asks for |
| Easier topic | a topic you like, where the gate asked for a kind of topic |
| A requirement dropped | the counter-argument left out, or only one voice in a role-play |

Make it as good as you can in every other way.

**Step two: the helper's check.** Give the helper the gate's wording from §7 and nothing else. They
listen to both takes in an order they choose and don't tell you. For each take, they write every
requirement they think is missed.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if the helper finds any requirement missed in
your kept retake, or finds nothing missed in take M. It's **GREEN** when the kept retake misses nothing
and take M misses at least one requirement.

If the helper found nothing missed in take M, your change was too small to hear, or your reading of the
gate was different from theirs. Compare their reading with your requirement list from part 1.2.

**Step three: the repair.** If your kept retake went RED, find the requirement the helper named. Record
the piece again under its own rules, and check it with a new helper. Take M is never filed as a
portfolio piece.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a helper who's given your requirement list** instead of
the gate's wording. Then they check your reading of the gate, not the gate.

The second is **a take M that's bad on purpose**. If take M is also worse in every other way, the helper
learns nothing about the task. Make it your best, with only the task changed.

The third, in real life, is **the better piece that answers a different question**, like the drawing from
a photo. Nobody marks it wrong for being good. They mark it missing.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers in interviews and tests learn this the hard way. A brilliant answer to a question you
weren't asked scores less than a plain answer to the one you were.

Under pressure, when a requirement feels too hard, meet it plainly rather than replacing it with
something you do well. A plain answer to the right question passes.

A native listener who knows what was asked hears a changed task at once, however good it sounds.
