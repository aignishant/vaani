## One-line answer

An unscripted talk has **no notes and no script**. Its plan lives in your head. After you draw the
topic, give yourself two things before you speak: **one answer sentence**, and **three single words**,
one for each point. Then talk through the shape you already own: the answer, three points each with an
example and a turn, and the answer again. Three words are easy to hold. A page of sentences isn't.

## The story

At your cousin's wedding reception, the host walked over with the microphone. *Say a few words about the
couple.* Nobody had warned you. Three other guests had been asked before you.

The first guest took out his phone and read a speech he'd typed the night before. He never looked up.
Halfway through, people went back to their food.

The second guest had nothing prepared. She started with the bride as a child, then the groom's job, then
the food, then the child again. She stopped when she ran out, with *so … yeah*.

The third guest was your aunt. She stood still for a breath, then said: *I've known Meena all her life,
and she's the most stubborn person I know, in the best way.* She told three short stories: stubborn at
school, stubborn at work, stubborn about marrying the right man. Then she said the first line again, and
raised her glass. People clapped before she'd finished.

Later you asked her how long she'd prepared. *I didn't*, she said. *I thought of three words while the
host was walking over. School, work, Rahul.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **talk**: one person speaking to listeners for a long turn, with an opening, three points and a close,
  from [day 99](../../../day-99-full-talk-structure/parts/01-speaking/1.1-point-example-turn.md).
- **point**, **example**, **turn**: a point is one sentence saying what this part of the talk is about;
  an example is one real case of it; a turn is the sentence that closes one point and opens the next,
  from the same part of day 99.
- **chunk**: a group of words you already say as one piece, like *The thing is*, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/01-speaking/1.1-start-from-english-you-own.md).
- **topic slip**: a slip of paper with one everyday topic on it, drawn without looking, from
  [day 101](../../../day-101-speaking-without-translating/parts/01-speaking/1.2-the-no-stop-hundred-words.md).
- **unscripted talk**: a talk given with no script and no notes, on a topic you didn't choose.
- **answer sentence**: one sentence that says what you think about the topic. It opens the talk and
  closes it.
- **anchor word**: one single word that stands for one point. You hold three of them in your head.

An unscripted talk isn't the same as talking with no plan. The plan is still there. It's just small
enough to carry without paper: one sentence and three words.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 6 gate on day 120 asks for an unscripted talk of at least four hundred spoken words on a topic
drawn at random. A hundred words, as on day 101, can be carried on one idea. Four hundred can't. Without
a plan, the talk runs dry around the first idea, or wanders like the second guest. With a script, it
isn't unscripted.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-25.**
[Impromptu speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impromptu_speaking) says that impromptu speaking "is a
speech that a person delivers without predetermination or preparation". It also describes the outline
speakers commonly use: an introduction with a roadmap, then three sections, then a conclusion. This part
uses that shape and asks you to hold it in your head.

**Step one: draw, and say the topic out loud.** Saying it out loud starts your voice. It also stops you
from reading the slip again and again.

**Step two: find the answer sentence.** Ask yourself one question: *What do I actually think about
this?* Answer it in one short sentence you could say to a friend.

> I think the bus is the best part of my day, not the worst.

**Step three: find three anchor words.** One word per point. A place, a person, a thing or a time works
best, because each one brings an example with it.

> Morning. Window. Home.

**Step four: speak the shape.** You already own it from day 99.

| Piece | What you say | Held as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | the answer sentence, and *I'll say three things* | the sentence |
| Point 1 | the point, one example from your life, a turn | anchor word 1 |
| Point 2 | the point, one example, a turn | anchor word 2 |
| Point 3 | the point, one example, a turn | anchor word 3 |
| Close | the answer sentence again, in new words | the sentence |

**Step five: when a point runs out, go to the next word.** Don't fill the silence with *and also*. Say
the turn, and reach for the next anchor word. That's all the next point needs to start.

**Name the trap.** Most learners spend the moment after the draw choosing good sentences. Sentences are
too heavy to hold, and one forgotten sentence stops the whole talk. Choose words, not sentences. The
sentences come when you speak, from the chunks you own.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the silent script**. The speaker writes the talk in their head, sentence
by sentence, then tries to say it back. It sounds like this:

> I think the bus is … the bus is the best part … of the day. Because … because in the morning … no,
> first … sorry. First, the morning.

The listener hears someone searching for a lost sentence, not someone talking to them.

The second is **no plan at all**, like the second guest. Every point arrives by accident, and the talk
stops when the first idea runs out, often well short of four hundred words.

The third is **anchor sentences instead of anchor words**. Three long sentences are three things to
forget.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers who are asked to say a few words at a wedding, a farewell or a meeting do what the aunt
did. They pick one thing they believe and two or three stories that show it. They almost never write
the sentences in their head first.

Under pressure, when your mind goes blank in the middle, your anchor words are still there. Say the
answer sentence again. It buys a breath and puts you back on the path.

A native listener doesn't notice a small grammar slip in a talk like this. They notice whether you knew
where you were going. A talk with a clear answer and three stories is remembered. A perfect sentence in
a wandering talk isn't.
