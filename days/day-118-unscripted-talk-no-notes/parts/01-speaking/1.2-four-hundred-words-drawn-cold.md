## One-line answer

Draw a topic you haven't seen. Find your answer sentence and three anchor words in your head, with no
pen in your hand. Then give the whole talk to one listener, recorded in one take, and don't stop until
the close. Afterwards, write the transcript and count: the words, the long silences, and whether all
three points arrived. Then ask the listener to name your answer and your three points.

## The story

At the reception, after the aunt sat down, the host came back. *Your turn.* You said the first thing that
came, and it was fine for a while. Then you ran out, somewhere in the middle, and looked at the cake.

On the way home, you tried it again in the car, out loud, with your sister listening. This time you held
three words. You didn't get every sentence right. But you got to the end, and she could tell you what
your three points were.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **unscripted talk**, **answer sentence**, **anchor word**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-three-words-in-your-head.md).
- **take**: one recording attempt, from start to stop, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/01-speaking/1.2-record-the-introduction.md).
- **transcript**: every word of a recording, written down as it was said, from
  [day 114](../../../day-114-listening-back-own-recording/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-listens-one-job-each.md).
- **drawn cold**: drawn and spoken about straight away, with no time spent preparing on paper.
- **long silence**: a pause in the middle of a sentence, long enough that a listener wonders whether
  you've finished. It's the day 101 word for the same thing.

**Step two: draw, then think without a pen.** Put the pen away. Draw one slip. Say the topic out loud.
Find your answer sentence and three anchor words in your head. Don't write them down.

**Step three: record in one take.** Face your listener, not the phone. Start with the answer sentence.
Speak all three points, each with an example and a turn. Close with the answer again. If you lose a
sentence, say the answer again and go on. Don't stop and restart.

**Step four: the listener's test.** Straight after, before they hear the recording, ask your listener
two things, and write their words down:

- *What was I saying about the topic?*
- *What were my three points?*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a pen in the hand**. The learner writes the three words, then a line
under each, then sentences. Now it's a script, and the talk sounds read.

The second is **stopping to restart** after a bad sentence. On a phone, a restart is easy. At the gate,
and in real life, there's no restart. One take.

The third is **counting from memory**. *It felt like four hundred.* It's often much less. Count the
transcript.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

When a fluent speaker is asked to say a few words, nobody counts their words. But a listener feels the
difference between a talk that ends because it's finished and one that ends because the speaker ran
out.

Under pressure, a real listener's face helps you. Talk to them, not to the phone. If they look lost, say
the point again more simply, and give another example.

A native listener forgives *um* and a wrong word. They notice when the answer at the end isn't the answer
from the start.
