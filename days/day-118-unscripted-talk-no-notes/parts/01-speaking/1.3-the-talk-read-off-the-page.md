## One-line answer

Give a short talk twice, on one topic. In take R, on purpose, write the whole talk out first and read it
aloud from the page. Label it deliberate. In take U, hold only an answer sentence and three anchor words,
and talk. Play both to a listener who doesn't know which is which. Ask: *Which one was talking to you?*
and *What were the three points?* Take U should win on both. If it doesn't, this part is RED.

## The story

The first guest at the reception, the one who read from his phone, was a kind man, and his speech was
well written. His sister said later that it was lovely when she read it.

But in the hall, his voice went flat and fast. He paused in the wrong places, at the end of each line on
his screen. He said *the couple's shared commitment to one another*, words nobody says out loud. And
because his eyes were on the phone, he didn't see that people had stopped listening.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one
we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **unscripted talk**, **answer sentence**, **anchor word**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-three-words-in-your-head.md).
- **take R**: a talk written out in full and read aloud. R is for *read*. It's labelled deliberate and
  never filed as a portfolio piece.
- **take U**: the same talk, unscripted, held as one sentence and three words. U is for *unscripted*.
- **blind listen**: a listener hears two takes without being told which is which.

| Take | How it's made | What a listener should say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R, read (deliberate) | written in full, then read aloud | sounds read; points harder to name |
| U, unscripted | an answer sentence and three anchor words | sounds like talking; names three points |

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate says *unscripted*. A read talk can reach four hundred words and still fail the gate. This part
lets you hear why the gate asks for it: a listener can tell.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-25.**
[Impromptu speaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impromptu_speaking): a speech delivered "without
predetermination or preparation". Take R is the opposite of that on purpose.

**Step one: choose one topic.** Draw a slip. Use the same topic for both takes, so the only difference is
the page.

**Step two: take R, deliberate.** Write the whole talk, about two hundred words, in full sentences. Then
record it, reading from the page. Say *deliberate, take R* at the start of the recording, so it can
never be mistaken for a real take.

**Step three: take U.** Put the page away, face down, in another room if you can. Find your answer
sentence and three anchor words. Record the talk, about the same length, in one take.

**Step four: the blind listen.** Play both takes to someone who didn't hear you make them. Cut or skip
the words *deliberate, take R* so they don't hear the label. Don't say which is which. Then ask:

| Which one sounded like someone talking to you? | `TODO(me)` |
| What were the three points in the first one you heard? | `TODO(me)` |
| What were the three points in the second one? | `TODO(me)` |
| Where did either one sound strange? | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if the listener picks take R as the one talking
to them, or names more of take R's points than take U's. It's **GREEN** when they pick take U and name at
least as many of its points.

**Step five: the repair.** If it went RED, listen to take U yourself for the moment you started reciting.
Often, a sentence from the written page came back word for word. Choose three new anchor words that
aren't in the written version, and record take U again. Then play it to a new listener.

## When it breaks

In take R, the listener usually hears one or more of these:

- **pauses at the end of lines**, not at the end of ideas;
- **the same speed everywhere**, with no slowing down for the important word;
- **written words**, like *furthermore* or *one another*, that nobody says to a friend.

The commonest wrong version in real life is **the half-script**: notes that start as three words and grow
into sentences. The speaker glances down, reads a line, looks up, and loses their place.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Some talks are read on purpose: a news report, or a statement where every word must be exact. A fluent
speaker reading from a page practises it until it doesn't sound read. That's a different skill, and it's
not the one the gate asks for.

Under pressure, many learners reach for a script because it feels safe. It fails in the worst way: one
lost line and there's nothing left. Three anchor words can't be lost like that.

A native listener hears a read talk within a few sentences, and starts listening less. They don't hear
your grammar mistakes in an unscripted talk nearly as much as you fear.
