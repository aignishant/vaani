## One-line answer

A **one-sitting page** is written from start to finish in one sitting, and it's the only version there
is. It has four steps, in order. **Plan** in the margin: the answer in one sentence and three words.
**Write** forward, from the first line to the last, without going back. **One pass** at the end, for
the top items on your proofreading checklist, and fill any gap. Then **hand it in**. There's no second
draft, so the plan has to be made before the first sentence, not during it.

## The story

Your friend Arjun applied for a job at the district office. On the test day, after the forms, the clerk
gave everyone a sheet with one question on it: *Describe a problem in your area and how you would solve
it.* One page. Hand it in before you leave.

Arjun started writing at once. He wrote well about the broken streetlights on his road. Halfway down,
he remembered the water problem, which was worse, and started on that. At the end he noticed a missing
word in the first line and crossed out half the sentence to fix it.

The man next to him sat still for a moment, then wrote a few words in the margin. Then he wrote without
stopping. At the end he read his page once, fixed two things, and handed it in.

Arjun's page had more good ideas. The other man's page answered the question.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **essay plan**, **thesis**: a half-page plan with the question, your answer in one sentence, three
  topic sentences and a conclusion line; the thesis is that answer sentence, from
  [day 99](../../../day-99-full-talk-structure/parts/02-writing/2.1-question-answer-three-points.md).
- **forward only**, **gap bracket**: writing from the first paragraph to the last without going back,
  and a bracket like *[word?]* where something is missing, from
  [day 115](../../../day-115-long-conversation-first-rehearsal/parts/02-writing/2.1-from-the-plan-forward-only.md).
- **proofreading checklist**, **pass**: your own list of yes-or-no questions for a finished page, and
  one read of the page looking for one thing only, from
  [day 113](../../../day-113-self-correction-while-speaking/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-question-one-pass.md).
- **one-sitting page**: a page planned, written and checked in one sitting, with no later draft.
- **margin plan**: the plan for a one-sitting page, small enough to fit in the margin: one answer
  sentence and three words.

## Why Vaani needs it

A written test, a form with a *describe* box, a statement at a police station, an answer in an exam: many
real pages have no second draft. The drafting days taught you to improve a page over several sittings.
This part teaches the page you only get to write once.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-25.**
[Free writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_writing) describes a different thing: writing
continuously "with no regards to spelling or grammar", and it says free writing "often produces raw, or
even unusable material". That's why a one-sitting page isn't free writing. It's written forward like free
writing, but it has a plan before it and a pass after it, because it's the final page.

**The four steps.**

| Step | What you do | What you never do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Plan | Underline the question's key words. In the margin, write your answer sentence and three single words, one per paragraph. | start writing before the answer is in the margin |
| 2. Write | Write forward: the answer first, three paragraphs, one per margin word, and a close that answers again. Leave a gap bracket if a word won't come. | go back to fix a sentence |
| 3. One pass | Fill every gap bracket. Then read the whole page once for each of your top two or three checklist items. | add a new idea |
| 4. Hand in | Stop. The page is finished. | start a clean copy |

**What the margin plan looks like.**

> *Answer:* The worst problem on my road is the dark, and it's the cheapest to fix.
> *Words:* lights · danger · letter

**Why so small?** You don't have the sitting for a half-page plan and a page. Three words are enough to
stop the page changing direction in the middle. The sentences come while you write.

**Crossing out.** Since there's no fair copy, a mistake found in the pass is fixed with one neat line
through it and the right word above. Never scribble.

**Name the trap.** A learner who has just learned to redraft will want to go back while writing. On a
one-sitting page, going back eats the ending. A page with a weak second sentence and a real ending beats
a perfect first paragraph with no ending.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **no plan**, like Arjun's page. The writer starts on one answer and ends on
another. The reader can't tell which one they meant.

The second is **the half-finished page**. The writer went back to polish the start and never wrote the
close.

The third is **an unfilled bracket handed in**: *The office should [word?] the lights.* On a draft, a
bracket is a note to yourself. On a finished page, it's a hole.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers facing a written test do what the man next to Arjun did: a short plan, a steady page, one
careful read. They don't try to write their best sentences. They try to finish, on one answer.

Under pressure, when you have a better idea halfway down, don't change the page's answer. If it fits
one of your three words, use it there. If not, leave it out.

A formal reader, an examiner or a clerk, marks what the page says about the question. They forgive a
neat crossing-out. They don't forgive a page that answers two different questions.
