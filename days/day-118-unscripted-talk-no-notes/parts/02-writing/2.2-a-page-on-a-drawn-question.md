## One-line answer

Write five real questions on slips and draw one without looking. Then write a one-sitting page of at
least two hundred and fifty words: margin plan, forward writing, one pass, hand in. Don't get up in the
middle. When it's done, count the words and the paragraphs, and give it to a reader. Ask them to write
your answer in one sentence and your three points. If they can, the page did its job.

## The story

A week after the test, Arjun tried again at home. His sister wrote five questions on scraps of paper
and folded them. He drew one: *What would you change about your school?*

This time he wrote his margin plan first: an answer and three words. He wrote to the end without going
back. He read it once for his three worst mistakes and filled one bracket.

His sister read it and said what he thought, in one sentence. It was the sentence in his margin.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a longer plan**. The learner writes a whole essay plan with topic
sentences. Now the plan takes most of the effort, and the page is rushed.

The second is **a redraft after the reader's test**. That turns the one-sitting page into a draft, and it
teaches you nothing about the page you only get to write once.

The third is **a question the learner chose because it was easy**. Draw it. The page is practice for a
question you didn't choose.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers in a written test are not faster at sentences than you are. They lose less of the sitting
to changing their minds.

Under pressure, a page that runs short is better finished than long. A close of two sentences that
answers again is enough.

A formal reader reads the first and last paragraphs first. If they say the same thing, the reader trusts
the middle.
