## One-line answer

Write two short one-sitting pages on two drawn questions. For page N, on purpose, skip the margin plan
and start writing at once. Label it deliberate. For page M, make the margin plan first. Give both to a
reader, who writes the answer from the **first** paragraph and the answer from the **last** paragraph of
each. On page M, the two should match. If they don't, this part is RED.

## The story

The clerk at the district office marked the test pages that evening. On Arjun's page, she underlined
the first line: *The biggest problem in my area is the streetlights.* Then she underlined the last line:
*So the water supply must be fixed first.*

She wrote one word in the margin: *Which?*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one
we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **one-sitting page**, **margin plan**: from [part 2.1](2.1-plan-write-one-pass-hand-in.md).
- **page N**: a one-sitting page written with no plan. N is for *no plan*. It's labelled deliberate and
  never filed as a portfolio piece.
- **page M**: a one-sitting page written from a margin plan. M is for *margin*.
- **first-and-last test**: a reader writes the answer that the first paragraph gives and the answer that
  the last paragraph gives, and says whether they match.

| Page | How it's made | What the first-and-last test should show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| N, no plan (deliberate) | start writing at once | the first and last answers may not match |
| M, margin plan | answer and three words first | the first and last answers match |

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't see your own page drift, because you remember what you meant. A reader who only has the page
can. This part shows you what the margin plan is for, by showing the page without one.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-25.**
[Free writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_writing): writing continuously, which "often produces
raw, or even unusable material". Page N is close to that on purpose. Page M isn't.

**Step one: draw two questions.** Use two of your slips from part 2.2, or write two new ones. A shorter
page is enough here: about one hundred and fifty words each, in at least three paragraphs.

**Step two: page N, deliberate.** Write *deliberate, page N* at the top. Read the question and start
writing straight away. No margin. Write forward, one pass, stop.

**Step three: page M.** Read the second question. Write the margin plan first, then the page, then one
pass. Stop.

**Step four: the first-and-last test.** Cover the label and the margin. Give both pages to a reader who
didn't watch you write. For each page, they fill one row.

| Page | The answer in the first paragraph | The answer in the last paragraph | Do they match? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| first page they read | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| second page they read | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if page M's first and last answers don't match.
It's **GREEN** when page M's match. Page N may match or not. If page N matched too, that's fine: you
held a plan in your head without writing it. Look at how long its first paragraph took you to start.

**Step five: the repair.** If page M went RED, compare its margin with its last paragraph. Usually the
margin answer was too wide, like *There are many problems*. Make a new margin answer that picks one
side. Draw a new question, and write a new page M. Give it to a new reader.

## When it breaks

In page N, the reader usually finds one of these:

- **the new answer in the last paragraph**: the page starts on one thing and ends on another, like
  Arjun's lights and water;
- **the list with no answer**: *There are many problems, like lights, water and roads*, and the page
  never says which one matters;
- **the stop without a close**: the page ends on an example because the writer ran out.

The commonest wrong version in real life is **a margin answer with no side**: *Schools can be good and
bad.* It's a plan on paper, but it plans nothing.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A fluent writer can sometimes write without a plan and still land, because they've written many pages of
that kind. Until you have, the margin plan is how you get the same result.

Under pressure, when the page is drifting, go back to the margin, not to the page. Read your answer
sentence and make the next paragraph serve it.

An examiner reading a page that changes its mind doesn't know which answer to mark. A reader on a
complaint form doesn't know which problem to fix. Both usually choose the one you didn't mean.
