## One-line answer

Record two short conversations with a partner, each on a new drawn topic, each with at least ten turns.
End take X, on purpose, with *Okay, bye*, in the middle of a thread. Label it deliberate. End take C with
the four closing moves. Play only the last few turns of each to a listener who doesn't know which is
which. Ask: *Which one ended well? Did anything go wrong in the other one?* Take C should win. If it
doesn't, this part is RED.

## The story

After your neighbour's first visit, the one that ended with *Okay, bye*, she told her sister about it.
Her sister asked, *Did you say something to upset her?*

Nothing had gone wrong. You'd only answered the phone. But from the outside, a conversation that stops
like that sounds like a problem.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one
we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **closing**, **pre-closing**, **abrupt ending**: from [part 1.1](1.1-signal-look-back-thank-go.md).
- **take X**: a short conversation ended abruptly on purpose. X is for *cut*. It's labelled deliberate
  and never filed as a portfolio piece.
- **take C**: a short conversation ended with the four closing moves. C is for *closed*.
- **ending listen**: a listener hears only the last few turns of each take and judges the ending.

| Take | How it ends | What a listener should say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X, cut (deliberate) | *Okay, bye*, mid-thread | the ending was sudden; something may have gone wrong |
| C, closed | signal, look back, thank, go | the ending was fine |

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't judge your own ending, because you know why you stopped. A listener only has what they hear.
This part lets you hear the gap between the reason in your head and the ending on the recording.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-25.**
[9.4: Closing Conversation](https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Communication/Introduction_to_Communication/Communicating_to_Connect_-_Interpersonal_Communication_for_Today_(Usera)/09:_Conversation_Skills/9.04:_Closing_Conversation)
describes a listener's reaction to a sudden *bye*: "you would think that B perhaps made a mistake in the
answer or that A was rude in some way." Take X tests that on your own recording.

**Step one: tell your partner the plan.** They should know that one take will end suddenly on purpose,
so they aren't hurt by it.

**Step two: take X, deliberate.** Draw a topic. Talk for at least ten turns. In the middle of a thread,
while your partner is still interested, say *Okay, bye* and stop. Say *deliberate, take X* before you
start, and cut that part before the listener hears it.

**Step three: take C.** Draw a new topic. Talk for at least ten turns. When a thread runs out, close with
the four moves.

**Step four: the ending listen.** Play the last four or five turns of each take to a listener who
didn't hear them made. Don't say which is which.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if the listener picks take X as the better
ending, or can't hear a difference. It's **GREEN** when they pick take C and can name the look back.

**Step five: the repair.** If it went RED, listen to take C. Usually the signal had no reason, or the look
back was missing. Record a new take C, with a reason in the signal and a look back to a loose end. Play
it to a new listener.

## When it breaks

In take X, the listener usually says one of these:

- *Were they angry?*
- *Did the call drop?*
- *He just left.*

The commonest wrong version in real life isn't *Okay, bye*. It's **the silent fade**: the talk slows, both
people say *yeah … yeah*, and nobody closes. It ends when someone walks away. That's an abrupt ending in
slow motion.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers rarely end a conversation without a signal, even with close family. When they do, the
other person usually asks if everything's all right.

Under pressure, like a phone about to die, a meeting running over or a bus arriving, one line of reason
turns an abrupt ending into a closing: *Sorry, my battery's going. Talk tomorrow?*

A native listener doesn't judge your English by the ending. They judge whether you liked talking to them.
