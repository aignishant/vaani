## One-line answer

Take your Draft 2 from day 116. Freeze it. Run your whole proofreading checklist on it, one pass per
item, and count the marks. Write the fair copy and title it *Final essay*. Compare it with the marked
draft, line by line. Then count its words and paragraphs, and give it a row in `docs/WRITINGS.md`. Keep
the marked draft and the checklist sheet beside it: they're the proof that it was proofread.

## The story

After the lunch line, your aunt made a rule for the thank-you cards. Once the card was checked, it went
in an envelope with the checked proof, and nobody opened it until the printer's copy came back. Then two
people read the printer's copy against the proof, one reading aloud and one following.

The thank-you cards came back with no mistakes. Your uncle wanted to add a line. She said no.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **starting from Draft 1**, or from a new essay, because Draft 2 has
problems. The final essay is Draft 2, proofread. Its problems are what the checklist is for.

The second is **the checklist sheet filled from memory**. Count the marks on the draft.

The third is **the old drafts overwritten**. Draft 1 and Draft 2 stay where they were. The final essay
is a new file.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A fluent writer handing in a final version to a college, a manager or a magazine keeps the draft that
was checked. When a reader asks *Was this proofread?*, the marked draft answers.

Under pressure, a proofread essay with one weak paragraph is better than an unproofread essay with a
brilliant new one.

A formal reader doesn't count your drafts. They count your mistakes.
