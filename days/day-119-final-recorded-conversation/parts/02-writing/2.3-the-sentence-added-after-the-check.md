## One-line answer

Make a second copy of one body paragraph of your final essay, on purpose, with two new sentences added
after the check and never checked. Call it copy L, for *late*, and label it deliberate. Give copy L and
the same paragraph from your final essay to a reader, with your checklist. They run the checklist on
both and mark every mistake. Your final essay's paragraph should get no marks. If it gets any, this part
is RED.

## The story

When the three hundred invitations came back, your aunt laid one on the table beside the checked proof.
She ran her finger down both, line by line. Every line matched until the last one, which wasn't on the
proof at all.

*That's the line*, she said. *It was never checked, so it was never right.*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one
we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **freeze**, **marked draft**, **final essay**: from [part 2.1](2.1-freeze-check-copy-compare.md).
- **checklist sheet**: from [part 2.2](2.2-make-the-final-essay.md).
- **copy L**: a copy of one final-essay paragraph with two sentences added after the check, labelled
  deliberate. It's never filed as a portfolio piece.
- **copy F**: the same paragraph, exactly as it is in your final essay.
- **checklist reader**: a reader who runs your checklist on a page and marks every mistake they find.

| Copy | How it's made | What a checklist reader should find |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L, late (deliberate) | two sentences added after the check, not proofread | marks, most of them in the new sentences |
| F, final | the paragraph from your final essay | no marks |

## Why Vaani needs it

You trust your final essay because you ran the checklist. This part asks someone else to run it. If
they find nothing in copy F, your proofreading held. If they find the new sentences in copy L full of
marks, you've seen why the freeze matters.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-25.**
[Proofreading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofreading): proofs are compared "against the original
manuscripts" to find errors made in copying. Copy L has sentences with no original to compare against.

**Step one: choose one body paragraph** of your final essay.

**Step two: make copy L, deliberate.** Write *deliberate, copy L* at the top. Copy the paragraph, and add
two new sentences, quickly, as if you'd just thought of them. Write them the way you'd write a quick
message. Don't read them over.

**Step three: copy F.** Copy the same paragraph exactly as it is in your final essay.

**Step four: the checklist reader.** Cover the labels. Give both copies and your checklist to a reader
who didn't see you write them. They run each checklist item on both copies and mark every mistake.

| Copy | Marks in the old sentences | Marks in the new sentences | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| first copy they read | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| second copy they read | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if the reader finds any mark in copy F. It's
**GREEN** when copy F has no marks. Copy L may have marks or not. If it has none, your quick sentences
were clean this time. Look at whether the reader could tell they were added.

**Step five: the repair.** If copy F went RED, the mark shows a checklist item that didn't do its job, or
a copying slip the compare step missed. Fix it on the final essay with one neat line, add a correction
row to `docs/WRITINGS.md` if the word count changed, and add the missed mistake to your checklist. Then
run that item on the whole final essay again.

## When it breaks

In copy L, the reader usually marks the kind of mistake your checklist was made to catch, now sitting in
the two new sentences:

> Also this is important for the family because they are spending less time together and there not
> talking.

A run-on sentence, a missing comma after *Also*, and *there* for *they're*. The old sentences around it
are clean.

The commonest wrong version in real life is **the late fix that adds a new mistake**: changing one word
in the fair copy, like *good* to *well*, without running the checklist again, and getting the grammar
wrong.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Anyone who prints things for other people, like the printer in the story, learns to check a change made
after the check on its own before it goes in. Your version of that is simple: no change after the freeze
without the checklist.

Under pressure, a reader may ask you to add something. Add it, then freeze again and check again, only
the new sentences if that's all that changed.

A formal reader marks a mistake in the last sentence exactly as they mark one in the first. The reader
doesn't know which sentence you checked.
