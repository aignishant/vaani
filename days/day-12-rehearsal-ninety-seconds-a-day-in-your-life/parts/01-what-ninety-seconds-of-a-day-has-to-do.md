# What ninety seconds of a day has to do

`CF-04` · Day 12

## The one-line answer

Ninety seconds of your day is not your routine read out at greater length — it is a walk you take
someone on through your day, and a good walk stops at three doors and opens them properly rather
than pointing at every door in the corridor.

## The model

You built the material on Day 10. Sequence markers, frequency words, one thing going wrong. All of
that stands. What you do **not** do today is take that and stretch it.

A routine list is the corridor. It is every door, named in order, at the same speed, none of them
opened. Stretched to ninety seconds it becomes genuinely difficult to listen to, and the reason is
not that your day is boring. It is that a corridor with forty doors gives the listener nothing to
stop at.

Three jobs, in order.

**Don't start at the alarm.** Almost everybody opens with waking up, which spends the first
stretch of a ninety-second piece on the least interesting part of the day and on the part every
other person's day also has. Start where your day actually starts — the first moment that is
yours rather than everybody's.

> Most days look pretty much the same.
> The day really starts when …

**Give moments, not a timetable.** No clock readings. Nobody can picture a number, and everyone
can picture *before anyone else is up*, or *the dead stretch after lunch*, or *once the last
message has gone out*. A moment is a door you can open. A number is a door with no handle.

> Before anyone else is up, I …
> There's a dead stretch where …
> Then it all happens at once.

**Stop at three doors, and only three.** Pick them before you start: the part you like, the part
you dread, and the part that surprises people. Everything else is corridor — you walk past it in
a few words and you do not apologise for walking past it. When you stop, you stop properly: one
specific detail, said slowly enough that it lands.

> That's the part I dread.
> That's when the day's properly over.

Notice the shape this produces. Fast, fast, **stop**, fast, **stop**, fast, **stop**, end. That
change of speed is the whole difference between a walk and a corridor, and it costs no vocabulary
at all.

One more thing you already have: the hook. Day 6 taught you to leave something open rather than
finish cleanly. It still applies, and today it points forwards instead of backwards — which is
what part 02 is for.

**Today's phrases** — all of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Most days look pretty much the same. | Opening honestly, without apologising for it |
| The day really starts when … | Skipping the alarm and landing where it matters |
| Before anyone else is up, I … | Fixing a moment without giving a time |
| There's a dead stretch where … | Naming the flat part instead of pretending it isn't there |
| That's the part I dread. | The friction door — one per piece is plenty |
| Then it all happens at once. | The pile-up, and a gear change in the telling |
| That's when the day's properly over. | Ending on a moment rather than on going to bed |

## Say it now

Say each line three times, with your own content. Nothing written down.

1. *Most days look pretty much the same. The day really starts when {{…}}.*
2. *Before anyone else is up, I {{…}}.*
3. *There's a dead stretch where {{…}} — and then it all happens at once.*
4. *That's the part I dread: {{…}}. And that's when the day's properly over.*

Then run all four as one continuous stretch, twice, without stopping. On the second run, walk
faster between them and slow right down inside each one. You are rehearsing the gear change, not
the words.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d12-free.m4a`.**

This is not the take. This is the walk-through before the walk.

> **One unbroken run at your day**, starting somewhere that is not waking up, with no clock
> readings anywhere in it, stopping properly at exactly three doors.

Pick your three doors out loud before you start the recorder. Say them to the empty room: *the
part I like, the part I dread, the part nobody expects.* Then record. If a fourth door tempts you
while you are talking, walk past it.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** afterwards they name the three places you stopped. If they name four, or
  two, or name three you did not intend, that is the finding — the doors you *think* you opened
  and the doors that actually opened are not always the same set.

## The deliberate failure

Record a short run at your day as a pure timetable. Every step, in order, nothing skipped, nothing
opened, no stopping anywhere. Wake up, then this, then this, then this.

**What breaking sounds like:** every door gets the same handful of words and the same falling
tone, so nothing is louder than anything else. Play it back and try to answer this: which part of
that day mattered to the person telling it? You won't be able to, and neither could a listener —
which is why a complete, accurate description of your day can leave someone knowing nothing about
you at all.

Then say your three doors again, on their own, out loud. Listen for what comes back: the pauses.
A corridor has no pauses in it. A walk is mostly the stopping.

## When it breaks in the wild

Someone at a table asks what your days are like. You give the corridor — accurate, complete, in
order — and you watch them wait politely for the end of it. Then they say "right, yeah", and
somebody starts a different conversation.

**The repair:**

> …it's all a bit samey, honestly. The part that actually matters is {{…}}.

You can do this halfway through the corridor. You are not correcting yourself and nobody hears a
correction — you are just stopping at a door, late.
