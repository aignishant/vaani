## One-line answer

A question that wants yes or no starts with are, is, do or does and goes up at the end, and the rise is what tells the listener it is their turn to say yes or no.

## The story

You are at the tea stall near your work. The man behind the counter says "Sugar?" with his
voice going up, and you say "Yes." He says "Milk?" going up, and you say "Yes." Then a new
customer beside you says "You are new here." flat, like a fact, and you do not know if he is
asking. He was. You did not answer, and he thought you were rude. The tea man's voice went up.
The customer's did not.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **yes/no question** — a question whose answer is yes or no.
- **rise** — go up.
- **stall** — a small open shop on the street.
- **counter** — the flat top of a stall or shop where things are handed over.
- **flat** — with no rise or fall.

A **yes/no question** wants one of two answers. In English it does two things. It starts with
a small verb, **are**, **is**, **do** or **does**, and it **rises** at the end. The voice goes
up on the last word, like a hand lifting, and stays up. That lift is the listener's signal:
your turn, yes or no.

This is the opposite of the what, where and who questions from
[day 11](../../../day-11-asking-what-where-who/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-where-who.md), which
fall. Both are questions. The tune tells the listener which kind of answer you want: a fall
wants a name, a place or a person; a rise wants yes or no.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) and in every real
conversation, the other person will ask you yes/no questions: Are you new here? Do you live
nearby? If you cannot hear the rise, you do not know they asked. If you cannot make the rise,
they do not know you asked. On [day 19](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed)
"Can you…?" is the same rising shape with a new first word.

## The rule

The four first words and what follows each:

| First word | Then | Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Are | you / they / we + the rest | Are you new here? |
| Is | he / she / it / one name + the rest | Is this your cup? |
| Do | you / they / we + a plain verb | Do you take sugar? |
| Does | he / she / it / one name + a plain verb | Does he work here? |

> Are you new here? ↗
> Do you take sugar? ↗
> Is this your cup? ↗

Say each at the mirror with a hand lifting on the last word and staying up. The last word
should sound as if it is waiting.

The first-word rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Questions and negatives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/questions-negatives):
"We make Yes/No questions by putting the first part of the verb in front of the subject", and
"For all verbs except be and have, we use do/does", with "Do they work hard?" and "Does he work
hard?" The forms "Am I? Are you? Is he/she/it?" were seen at
[The verb 'be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/verb-be),
opened the same day.

The tea man's one-word questions, "Sugar?" "Milk?", are the shortest yes/no questions there
are, and they work because the rise does all the work. With people you know, at a counter, they
are fine. With a stranger, put the first word in: "Do you want sugar?"

The trap: a rise that goes up and then comes down again on the last word. That sounds like
surprise, not a question. Up, and stay up.

## When it breaks

You ask with a flat voice:

> You are new here.

The listener hears a fact about themselves and waits to see why you said it. The fix: "Are you
new here?" with the rise. First word moved, voice up.

You use the first word but let the voice fall:

> Are you new here? ↘

The listener hears a question, but a tired or unfriendly one, as if you already know. The fix:
lift on here.

You put the question word from day 11 on a yes/no question:

> What you are new here?

The listener is lost. Yes/no questions have no question word. The fix: "Are you new here?"

If, on playback, your yes/no questions do not rise on the last word, the check is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers rise on yes/no questions without knowing they do it, and they hear a flat one
as a statement every time. In fast speech "Do you" becomes "D'you" and "Are you" almost
becomes "You", so the rise carries more and more of the meaning. That is why the tea man can
ask with one word.

Under pressure, in noise, on the phone, the rise is what survives. A native listener will
answer "Yes" or "No" to a rising anything. They will not answer a flat sentence, however
correct, because to them nobody asked.
