## One-line answer

A short answer is yes or no, then the person, then the same small verb the question started with, so "Do you take sugar?" gets "Yes, I do" and "Are you new here?" gets "No, I'm not".

## The story

The next morning the same customer is at the tea stall. He tries again, with the rise this
time: "Are you new here?" You say "Yes." He waits. You say nothing more. He turns to his tea.
Later the tea man asks "Do you take sugar?" and you say "Yes, I do. Two." He laughs and puts
two in. Same word, yes, but the second time it had a tail, and the tail made it a reply, not a
grunt.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **short answer** — yes or no plus two or three words that echo the question.
- **echo** — say back the same small word.
- **tail** — the small words after yes or no.
- **grunt** — a sound that is barely a word.
- **reply** — an answer that keeps the talk going.

In [part 1.1](1.1-the-rising-question.md) a yes/no question started with are, is, do or does.
A **short answer** gives yes or no and then **echoes** that first word back, with the right
person in front of it. Do you take sugar? Yes, I do. Are you new here? No, I'm not. Does he
work here? Yes, he does.

The echo is the tail. Without it, "Yes" alone can sound like you want the talk to end. With
it, the listener hears that you heard the question and answered it properly. It also hands the
turn back to them in a friendly way.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), after your

## The rule

The echo follows the first word of the question. The verb in the answer is the same small
verb; the person changes from you to I when the question was about you.

| Question starts | Yes | No |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Are you …? | Yes, I am. | No, I'm not. |
| Is he / she / it …? | Yes, he is. / Yes, she is. / Yes, it is. | No, he isn't. / No, she isn't. / No, it isn't. |
| Do you …? | Yes, I do. | No, I don't. |
| Does he / she …? | Yes, he does. / Yes, she does. | No, he doesn't. / No, she doesn't. |

> Are you new here? — Yes, I am.
> Do you take sugar? — No, I don't.
> Does he work here? — Yes, he does.

Say each pair at the mirror, both voices. Yes and no fall; the tail falls too, gently. Then add
one small true thing after the tail, if you like: "Yes, I do. Two, please."

The short-answer shapes were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got),
which gives short answers as yes or no plus the person plus the small verb: "Yes, I/you/we/they
have. No, I/you/we/they haven't." and "Yes, he/she/it has. No, he/she/it hasn't." The same
pattern with be and do follows the forms at
[The verb 'be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/verb-be)
and [Questions and negatives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/questions-negatives),
opened the same day. The short forms I'm not, isn't, don't and doesn't are the ones a fluent
speaker says.

The trap: echoing the main verb instead of the small verb. Do you take sugar? "Yes, I take."
The listener waits for what you take. The tail echoes do, not take: "Yes, I do."

One more: the answer to "Are you…?" is "I am", not "I are". The person changed, so the verb
changes with it.

## When it breaks

You answer with the bare word:

> Are you new here? — Yes.

Correct, and cold. The listener does not know if you want to talk. The fix: "Yes, I am." Two
more words.

You echo the wrong verb:

> Do you take sugar? — Yes, I take.

The listener waits for the rest of the sentence. The fix: "Yes, I do."

You keep you in the answer:

> Are you new here? — Yes, you are.

You have just told the other person that they are new. The fix: the question was about you, so
the answer starts with I. "Yes, I am."

If, on playback, any of your answers is yes or no with no tail, or echoes the main verb, the
check is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers say short answers constantly, and then add the real content: "Yes, I do. Two,
please." "No, I'm not. I've been here years." The tail is a habit; the content is the reply.
Under pressure, a fast speaker will drop the yes and keep the tail: "I do." "I'm not." That is
fine too. What they do not do is leave the bare "Yes" hanging with a stranger.

A native listener hears a bare "Yes" as an end. They hear "Yes, I do" as a door held open. It
is the same difference as the greeting that comes back, and it is two words.
