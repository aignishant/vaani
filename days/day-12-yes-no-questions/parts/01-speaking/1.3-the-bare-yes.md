## One-line answer

Record a flat yes/no question and a bare one-word answer on purpose, play them back as the other person at the stall, feel the talk stop, then record the rising question and the tailed answer beside them.

## The story

At the tea stall you try the experiment on yourself. You ask the tea man, flat, "You have
change." He looks at you, then goes back to the kettle. You ask again, rising, "Do you have
change?" and he says "Yes, I do," and holds out his hand for your note. Then a stranger asks
"Are you waiting for the bus?" and you say "Yes." and nothing else, on purpose, and watch him
look away. You have now been on both sides of the flat question and the bare yes.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong versions are deliberate. They rest on what beginners
commonly do, not on a mistake found in your own recordings.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **bare** — with nothing added; the word alone.
- **die** — here, stop; the talk ends.
- **repair** — a small correction that helps someone understand, from
  [day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.3-change-one-sound.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the rising question from [part 1.1](1.1-the-rising-question.md) and the short answer
from [part 1.2](1.2-short-answers.md). Now you break both on purpose. The question goes flat.
The answer loses its tail. You listen as the other person and notice the exact moment the talk
dies. Then you record the repair beside it, so your ear has both.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), the stranger will
ask you something after your introduction. A bare yes ends the recording early, and a flat
question of yours gets no answer. A learner who has heard their own talk die on a recording
adds the tail without thinking on the day.

## The rule

Two rows, two voices. Recorder on. Wrong first, then right, with a gap.

**Row 1 — the flat question.**

> You have change. → (silence)
> Do you have change? ↗ — Yes, I do.

**Row 2 — the bare answer.**

> Are you waiting for the bus? ↗ — Yes.
> Are you waiting for the bus? ↗ — Yes, I am. Are you?

Play each row. Be the other person. After the wrong version, say out loud what you would do
next. For row 1, most listeners do nothing, because nothing was asked. For row 2, most
listeners look away, because the answer closed. After the right version, say what you would do
next. It should be different.

Then the repair, in one breath, as it happens when you hear your own bare yes:

> Yes. — Yes, I am. Sorry. Are you?

The forms were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Questions and negatives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/questions-negatives)
and [The verb 'be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/verb-be).

## When it breaks

The break this part is about: as the listener, you answer the flat question anyway.

> You have change. → "Yes, I do."

You knew it was a question because you wrote it. The tea man did not. Play it again and hear
only the tune. If it falls, nobody asked. If you still hear a question, your flat version was
not flat enough; say it as a plain fact about the weather and record again.

A second break: the repair adds a tail that echoes the wrong verb.

> Yes. — Yes, I wait.

The tail must echo the small verb the question started with. Are you waiting → Yes, I am. The
fix is not more words; it is the right two.

If you cannot tell the wrong version from the right one by what you would do next, the check
is RED.

## In real life

Nobody asks flat on purpose, and nobody answers bare on purpose. But beginners whose first
language asks with a particle or a word order do both, all day, and the people around them
are too polite to say. The only signal is that conversations keep ending early. You have now
heard why.

A native listener forgives a bare yes from someone who is clearly busy. From someone at a
stall, on a bus, in a queue, it reads as "leave me alone", even from a person who wanted to
talk. The repair, "Yes, I am. Sorry. Are you?", takes one breath and turns it round.
