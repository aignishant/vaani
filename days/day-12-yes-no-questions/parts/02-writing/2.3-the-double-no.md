## One-line answer

Copy your fridge notes with three breaks on purpose, not with no helper, an -s left after doesn't, and two negatives in one sentence, read them as a flatmate deciding what to do, mark all three, then fix only those.

## The story

You leave three notes on the fridge before a weekend away. When you come back, the cat has
been fed fish, the window has been left open, and the bin is full. Your flatmate shows you the
notes. "The cat not eat fish." She fed it fish, to be safe. "He doesn't likes the window open."
She read likes and thought you meant he does. "I don't have no bags." She thought you had
some. Three notes, three opposite results.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong version is deliberate. It rests on what beginners
commonly write, not on a mistake found in your own page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **double no** — two negative words in one sentence, such as don't … no.
- **cancel** — one thing undoes another.
- **read back** — read your own page again, slowly, as if it were someone else's, from
  [day 2](../../../day-02-long-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.3-a-form-nobody-can-use.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have not with be from [part 2.1](2.1-not-with-be.md) and don't and doesn't from
[part 2.2](2.2-dont-and-doesnt.md). Now you plant the three breaks beginners really make, and
you read as the flatmate who has to decide what to do from the note alone. One of the three,
the **double no**, is new: in English, two negative words in one sentence do not make it more
negative. They confuse the reader, and to many readers they **cancel** each other. "I don't
have no bags" reads as "I have bags."

## Why Vaani needs it

Every note, message and form you write from here has a no in it somewhere, and the reader acts
on the note. If your read-back cannot catch three planted broken negatives today, it will not
catch the real one that feeds the cat fish.

## The rule

Under your correct notes from parts 2.1 and 2.2:

1. Copy them with three deliberate breaks: write one not with no helper; leave the -s on the
   main verb after one doesn't; put a second negative word, no or nothing, into one sentence
   that already has don't or isn't.
2. Cover the correct notes.
3. Read the broken notes out loud as the flatmate, and after each say what you would do. If
   what you would do is the opposite of what the writer meant, or you cannot decide, that is a
   break. Then count the negative words in each sentence. More than one is a break.
4. Mark each RED. There should be three.
5. Uncover. Fix only the three, on a new line, and mark it GREEN.

The model, broken on purpose:

> The cat not eat fish. He doesn't likes the window open. I don't have no bags.

Three RED marks: "not eat" has no helper; "doesn't likes" has the -s twice; "don't … no" is a
double no and reads as "I have bags."

The rules were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple)
and [Questions and negatives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/questions-negatives):
"We make negatives by putting not after the first part of the verb"; "They don't work hard."

## When it breaks

You read the broken notes back and mark them GREEN:

> The cat not eat fish. He doesn't likes the window open. I don't have no bags. — GREEN

In many first languages, two negatives make a stronger no, and "not eat" is a full sentence.
Your eye read the meaning you meant. The check itself failed. Read again as the flatmate and
say out loud what you would do after each note. If you would feed the cat fish, it is RED.

Another break: you fix the double no by removing the wrong word.

> I have no bags.

Correct English, but it was not the marked break. The marked break was the extra no beside
don't. Either "I don't have any bags" or "I have no bags" is right; the repair keeps the
sentence closest to what you wrote and takes out one negative, not the helper.

If any planted break was marked GREEN, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody writes a double no on purpose. But it is one of the most common shapes in beginners'
English, because in many languages it is correct and strong. In English it is heard in some
speech, and on paper it is read as a mistake or as the opposite. Fluent writers use one
negative word per idea: don't … any; isn't … anything; never on its own.

A flatmate who fed the cat fish will laugh about it. A note to a carer about what a child does
not eat, a message to a doctor about a medicine you do not take, a form about what you do not
own: these are read once and acted on. Keep the broken notes on your practice page as proof
that your read-back can go RED.
