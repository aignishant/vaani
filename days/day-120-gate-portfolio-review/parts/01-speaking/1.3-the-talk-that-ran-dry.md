## One-line answer

Record a second talk on a new slip, on purpose with only **one** anchor word, and stop when that point
runs out. Call it take D, for *dry*, and label it deliberate. Then give a gate-check listener the gate
wording from §7, the transcripts of take D and your gate take, and both recordings, in a hidden order.
They mark every requirement each take misses. Your gate take should get no marks. If it gets any, this
part is RED, and that's the gate working.

## The story

Your helper told you about her brother's speaking exam. He drew *public transport*, talked about the bus
to his office, and was fluent and clear for a while. Then he said, *So … yes. That's the bus.* He had
nothing else to say. He'd had one idea, and he'd used it.

The examiner wrote down one thing: *Too short.*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one
we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **requirement**, **gate take**: from [part 1.1](1.1-what-the-gate-asks-for.md) and
  [part 1.2](1.2-record-the-talk-and-review-the-portfolio.md).
- **take D**: a talk with one anchor word only, stopped when that point runs out. It's labelled
  deliberate and never filed as a portfolio piece.
- **gate-check listener**: a listener who has only the gate wording, the transcripts and the recordings,
  and marks every requirement a take misses.

| Take | How it's made | What a gate-check listener should mark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D, dry (deliberate) | one anchor word, stopped when it runs out | at least four hundred words: missed |
| the gate take | an answer sentence and three anchor words | nothing |

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't check your own gate take fairly, because you know how hard you tried. A listener with only the
gate wording can't be kind to you by accident. This part lets them check the real take, beside one built
to fail.

## The rule

**The reference for this part is the plan itself**:
[§7, the phases](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases). The listener gets the speaking wording
from there, copied by hand, and nothing else from you.

**Step one: take D, deliberate.** Draw a new slip from your helper's bowl. Say *deliberate, take D* before
you start. Choose one anchor word only. Talk until that point runs out, and stop.

**Step two: the transcripts.** Write take D's transcript and count its words. Put it beside your gate
take's transcript. Cover the counts and the labels, and mark them only *first* and *second*.

**Step three: the gate-check listener.** Give them the gate wording, both transcripts and both
recordings, with the label cut from take D. They count the words themselves, and mark each requirement
yes or no for each take.

**Step four: when it goes RED.** That's the gate working, not a disaster. Look at what the listener
marked. If it's the count, your talk ran dry on one point: record a new gate take on a new slip, making
sure each anchor word has an example. Then pass part 1.2 again and try a new listener. If the gate stays
red, name two or three days from days 101 to 119 to re-run, give each re-run its own `PROGRESS.md` row,
and come back to the gate after them.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version at a gate is **take D without knowing it**: a talk that feels long because
you were nervous, and counts two hundred and ninety.

The second is **the listener given your own count**. Then they check your arithmetic, not the talk.

The third, in real life, is **filling to four hundred**: saying the same point three times in new words to
reach the number. A gate-check listener will mark *unscripted* yes and the count yes, but a stranger will
hear one point. Three anchor words are how you reach four hundred with three things worth hearing.

These are errors beginners commonly make at a final gate. This day was written before your own error
ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Speaking exams, interviews and meetings all have their version of *too short*. It's rarely said out
loud. The listener just decides you had less to say than you did.

Under pressure, when one point runs out early, don't stop. Say the turn, and reach for the next word. If
the whole talk runs out, go back to your answer sentence and give one more example from your own life.

A native listener remembers a short talk as a weak one, even when every sentence was right.
