## One-line answer

The phase 6 writing gate asks for a five-paragraph essay of at least three hundred words, proofread
against your day 113 checklist, and a Writing Portfolio indexed in full. This part teaches nothing new,
because a gate that teaches isn't a gate. It splits the gate into requirements you can check yes or no,
and names the evidence for each. The essay is the one you made on day 119. Today it's reviewed, not
written.

## The story

On the first day, you wrote your name with a capital letter. Since then you've written a note for the
fridge, a message, a memory, an opinion, a complaint, a cover letter, an essay in three drafts. They're
in six folders now, with a list on top.

Today a stranger could open that list and read your writing from the first page to the last, in order.
The last thing they'd read is your final essay, with the marked draft and the checklist sheet behind it
to show it was checked.

This folder is about making sure that stranger finds every page, and that the last one does what the
gate says.

**This is a rehearsal, and it says so.** This day was written before your error ledger had rows in it.
Until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked, the checks below are the general ones
rather than your own recurring mistakes, and the gate is not yet a gate.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate**: a check at the end of a phase that the work has to pass before the phase is finished, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-the-page-must-carry.md).
- **five-paragraph essay**: an introduction, three body paragraphs and a conclusion, from
  [day 99](../../../day-99-full-talk-structure/parts/02-writing/2.1-question-answer-three-points.md).
- **proofreading checklist**: your own list of yes-or-no questions for a finished page, from
  [day 113](../../../day-113-self-correction-while-speaking/parts/02-writing/2.2-build-your-checklist-and-run-it.md).
- **final essay**, **marked draft**, **checklist sheet**: the fair copy for your portfolio, the frozen
  draft with every checklist fix marked, and the page of marks per item, from
  [day 119](../../../day-119-final-recorded-conversation/parts/02-writing/2.2-make-the-final-essay.md).
- **Writing Portfolio**, **index**, **reader's note**: your gate pages and their rewrites, the list in
  `docs/WRITINGS.md` that says where each is, and the short page on top written to a stranger, from
  [day 117](../../../day-117-retake-weakest-piece/parts/02-writing/2.1-findable-by-a-stranger.md).
- **requirement**: one thing a gate asks for that can be checked yes or no.
- **indexed in full**: every piece has a row in the index, and every row leads to a page that opens.

The gate wording is in the plan's §7, and it's copied here without a word changed:

> Writing: a five-paragraph essay of at least three hundred words, proofread against the day 113
> checklist. Both portfolios indexed in full.

In this folder, *both portfolios* means your Writing Portfolio.

| Requirement | What it means | The evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a five-paragraph essay | introduction, three body paragraphs, conclusion | the final essay, paragraphs counted |
| at least three hundred words | counted, not guessed | the word count, from the page |
| proofread against the day 113 checklist | every checklist item run as its own pass, before the fair copy | the marked draft and the checklist sheet from day 119 |
| indexed in full | a row for every gate page and rewrite, each leading to a page that opens | the index audit in part 2.2 |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the end of the course. Everything in the writing folder, from the first capital letter on day 1,
exists so that a stranger can read a whole essay of yours without asking what you meant, and find every
page you've written for them. Until that's checked, nobody knows whether they can.

## The rule

There's no new rule today. What follows is the setup.

**The essay comes from day 119.** This day reviews it and never makes it. If there's no final essay with
its marked draft, checklist sheet and `WRITINGS.md` row when you reach this part, go back to day 119. An
essay written today and filed as day 119's isn't honest, and it isn't the gate.

**Nothing is added to the essay today.** Day 119 froze it. If the review finds a real mistake, it's fixed
with one neat line and noted in the ledger. A new sentence is not a fix.

**Old pages stay as they are.** The index is fixed by adding rows, never by changing old ones. A missing
page is written as missing, never made today and filed as old.

**Name the trap.** A gate written early is easy to make kinder: *about* three hundred words, a checklist
run once on the fair copy, an index that's *nearly* complete. None of those is in §7. If you notice
you're making the gate easier, stop and read §7 again.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the essay proofread in one read**. The writer read it through once,
looking for everything. §7 says *against the day 113 checklist*, one pass per item, and the checklist
sheet is the proof.

The second is **the missing essay**. The learner reaches day 120, finds no final essay, and writes one
today. It's an essay, but it hasn't been drafted, frozen or proofread, and the index now lies about its
day.

The third is **the index checked from memory**. Open each page from its row.

These are errors beginners commonly make at a final gate. This day was written before your own error
ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Any real piece of writing that matters, like an application, a report or an essay for a course, is
judged by a reader who has a list of what it must do. That reader doesn't know how hard you worked. They
count the paragraphs and read the words.

Under pressure, a portfolio with one page honestly marked *missing* is stronger than one where a page was
rushed in to fill the gap.

A stranger reading your portfolio won't compare your handwriting on day 1 and day 119. They'll notice
that the writer of the last page could explain something the writer of the first page couldn't.
