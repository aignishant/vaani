## One-line answer

Make a copy of your final essay, on paper, broken on purpose: two body paragraphs run together into one,
so it has four paragraphs, and the checklist sheet left out. Call it copy B and label it deliberate. Give
copy B and your real final essay, with its marked draft and checklist sheet, to a gate-check reader who
has only the gate wording from §7. They mark every requirement each one misses. Your final essay should
get no marks. If it gets any, this part is RED, and that's the gate working.

## The story

At the college office, a clerk was checking application essays against a printed list: *five paragraphs,
three hundred words, proofread.* One essay was very good. It was long, it was clear, and it had four
paragraphs, because the writer had run two points together without a break.

The clerk didn't read it twice. She ticked *three hundred words*, left *five paragraphs* empty, and
moved on.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one
we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

You've read your final essay many times, so you see five paragraphs even where there might be four. A
reader with only the gate wording sees what's on the page. This part checks the real essay beside one
built to fail.

## The rule

**The reference for this part is the plan itself**:
[§7, the phases](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases). The reader gets the writing wording
from there, copied by hand, and nothing else from you.

**Step one: copy B, deliberate.** On paper, write *deliberate* at the top and copy the final essay by
hand. Where your second body paragraph starts, don't start a new line: run it straight on from the first.
Don't include a checklist sheet. Never file copy B.

**Step two: hide the labels.** Cover the word *deliberate*. Put the two in a hidden order, marked only
*first* and *second*. The final essay goes with its marked draft and checklist sheet; copy B goes alone.

**Step three: the gate-check reader.** Give them the gate wording and both piles. They count the
paragraphs and words themselves, look for the proofreading evidence, and mark each requirement yes or no.

**Step four: when it goes RED.** That's the gate working, not a disaster. If the reader counted four
paragraphs in the real essay, look at where a new paragraph should start, and whether the line break is
clear. If they found no proof of the checklist, the sheet or the marked draft is missing or unclear. Go
back to day 119 to fix it there, then review again with a new reader. If the gate stays red, name two
or three days from days 101 to 119 to re-run, give each re-run its own `PROGRESS.md` row, and come back
to the gate after them.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version at a gate is **the paragraph break a reader can't see**: a new paragraph
that starts on the same line, or with no indent or gap, so the reader counts four.

The second is **the reader told the answer**: *There are five, look.* Then the reader is checking you,
not the page.

The third, in real life, is **the checklist run and thrown away**. The essay was proofread, but nothing
shows it. For a gate that says *proofread against the checklist*, the sheet is the evidence.

These are errors beginners commonly make at a final gate. This day was written before your own error
ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Readers who check writing against a list, like a clerk, an examiner or an editor, look for the things
they can count first. A missing paragraph break costs you before they've read a sentence.

Under pressure, before you hand in any piece of writing that has a list of requirements, give it to one
person with only the list. What they mark is what the real reader will mark.

A formal reader never asks you what you meant to do. They read what you did.
