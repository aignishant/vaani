## One-line answer

Record a sentence with every word the same weight and another with the small words made loud, on purpose, play them back as the person across the kitchen, notice which words reach you, then record the beat beside them.

## The story

You run the experiment on your brother. With the fan on, you call, flat, every word the same:
"Can you give me the big spoon from the drawer." He says "What?" You call again with "me" and
"from" loud and "spoon" quiet, and he brings you a fork. Then you call it with the beat, and he
brings the spoon. You did the whole test in one kitchen. Now you will do it with the recorder
as your brother.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong versions are deliberate. They rest on what beginners
commonly do, not on a mistake found in your own recordings.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **robot** — a machine that speaks with every word equal.
- **drown** — be lost under noise.
- **reach** — arrive at the listener.
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the beat from [part 1.1](1.1-loud-words-and-quiet-words.md) and your own sentences
from [part 1.2](1.2-tap-the-beat-in-your-sentence.md). Now you break the beat the two ways
beginners really do. First, every word equal, the robot. Second, the small words loud and the
meaning words quiet, the beat upside down. You listen as the person across the room and write
down which words reached you. Then you record the beat beside them.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), nerves flatten
speech. A learner who has heard their own robot voice on a recording knows what flat sounds
like from outside, and puts the beat back on the day. A learner who has not, cannot hear that
it has gone.

## The rule

Two rows. Recorder on. Wrong first, then right, with a gap. Use one of your own five sentences
from part 1.2; the model uses the kitchen one.

**Row 1 — the robot.** Every word the same length and loudness:

> Can. You. Give. Me. The. Big. Spoon. From. The. Drawer.
> Can you GIVE me the BIG SPOON from the DRAWER?

**Row 2 — upside down.** The small words loud, the meaning words quiet:

> Can YOU give ME THE big spoon FROM THE drawer?
> Can you GIVE me the BIG SPOON from the DRAWER?

Play each row. Be the brother at the far end with the fan on. After the wrong version, write
down the three or four words that reached you most clearly. After the right version, do the
same. In row 1 you should have written words at random, or all of them equally faintly. In
row 2 you should have written you, me, the, from. In the right version, give, big, spoon,
drawer.

Then the repair in one breath, as it happens when you hear "What?":

> Can you give me the big spoon from the drawer. — The BIG SPOON. From the DRAWER.

The rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Sentence Stress](https://www.englishclub.com/pronunciation/sentence-stress.php): the content
words are stressed and "we maintain a constant beat on the stressed words".

## When it breaks

The break this part is about: as the listener, you write down the meaning words from the
robot version too.

> Can. You. Give. Me. The. Big. Spoon. → spoon, drawer

You knew which words mattered because you wrote the sentence. The brother did not. Play it
again with your eyes closed and write the words in the order they hit you hardest. If the
meaning words still come first, your robot was not flat enough; make every word exactly the
same length, like a clock ticking.

A second break: the repair is the whole sentence again, louder.

> CAN YOU GIVE ME THE BIG SPOON FROM THE DRAWER?

Louder and equal is still equal. The fix: repeat only the meaning words, with the beat. "The
BIG SPOON. From the DRAWER."

If, as the listener, the same words reached you from the wrong and the right versions, the
check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody speaks like a robot on purpose. Everybody does it when reading aloud, when nervous, or
when their first language weights every word the same. Fluent speakers do it too when they
read a list of names. The moment they stop reading and start talking, the beat comes back.
That is the difference you are learning to hear.

A native listener across a room cannot tell you why the robot version was hard. They will say
"I couldn't hear you" even though every word was clear. What they mean is that the meaning
words did not stand up. The repair, saying only the meaning words with the beat, is what
fluent speakers do when asked "What?", and it works from any distance.
