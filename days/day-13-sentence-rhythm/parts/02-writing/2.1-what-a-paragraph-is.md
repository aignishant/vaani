## One-line answer

A paragraph is a group of sentences that are all about one thing, written one after another as a block, with the first sentence saying what the thing is.

## The story

Your aunt is arriving by train and your friend has offered to collect her. He has never met
her. You send him a message: "Short. Grey hair. Red bag. Glasses. Sixty." He stands at the
station gate reading five words and watching two hundred people come out. Half the women are
short and grey. He goes home without her. You gave him facts. You did not give him a picture.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph** — a group of sentences about one thing, written together.
- **collect** — meet someone and bring them home.
- **picture** — what the reader sees in their mind.
- **topic** — the one thing a paragraph is about.
- **gate** — the way out of a station.

A [sentence](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md) is one
complete thought with a capital letter and a full stop. A **paragraph** is several sentences
about **one topic**, written one after another in a
[block](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/02-writing/2.2-five-sentences-on-paper.md). The
first sentence says what the topic is. The rest add to it. When the topic changes, the
paragraph ends and a new one starts on a new line.

Five words in a list give the reader five facts. A paragraph gives the reader one picture,
because the sentences are joined by the topic and the reader holds them together.

## The rule

A paragraph on the page, as a picture:

```text
  First sentence: what the topic is.  Second sentence adds to
it.  Third sentence adds to it.  Fourth sentence adds to it.
Last sentence closes it.
                                          ← new line = new topic
```

Four rules:

1. **One topic.** Every sentence is about the same thing. If a sentence is about something
   else, it belongs in another paragraph.
2. **First sentence names the topic.** The reader knows what they are holding before they
   pick up the rest.
3. **Sentences run on from each other**, one space after each full stop, no new line between
   them. A new line means a new topic.
4. **Four to eight sentences.** Fewer and it is a note; more and the reader loses the thread.

The model, about a person who is not your aunt:

> My aunt Sunita is arriving on the ten o'clock train. She is short, with grey hair tied back.
> She wears glasses with thin gold frames. She will have a red cloth bag on her shoulder. She
> walks slowly, so she will be near the end of the crowd.

Five sentences, one topic, first sentence names her, last sentence helps most. Your friend
would find her.

The word [paragraph](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/paragraph)
was opened on 2026-09-22; its definition reads "a section of a piece of writing, usually
consisting of several sentences dealing with a single subject". The habit of giving an email
"three main paragraphs", each with its own job, was seen the same day in the tips at
[A thank-you email](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/thank-you-email).

The trap: a list of words with full stops between them looks like a paragraph from a distance.
"Short. Grey hair. Red bag." Three full stops, no verbs, no sentences. A paragraph is made of
sentences, and a sentence has a verb.

## When it breaks

You write the facts as a list:

> Short. Grey hair. Red bag. Glasses. Sixty.

The reader gets five facts and no picture. The fix: make each a sentence, and put the topic in
the first one. "My aunt is short. She has grey hair…"

You change topic in the middle:

> My aunt is short, with grey hair. The train is often late. She has a red bag.

The second sentence is about the train, not the aunt. The reader loses the picture. The fix:
move the train sentence to its own paragraph, or leave it out.

You start each sentence on a new line:

> My aunt is short.
> She has grey hair.
> She has a red bag.

Each new line says "new topic", so the reader gets three topics. The fix: run them on, one
space after each full stop.

If your paragraph has a sentence about a different topic, or sentences on separate lines, or a
line with no verb, it is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers break a page into paragraphs without thinking, one per topic, and readers use
the breaks to breathe and to know where one thing ends. A message with no paragraphs is a
wall; a message where every sentence is its own line is a list. Neither is read carefully.

A friend at the station gate will do their best with five words. A form, an office or a
stranger will not fill in the picture for you. Give them the paragraph and they see your aunt.
