## One-line answer

A paragraph about a person goes who they are, what they look like, what they are like, and one thing they do, using is and has for the facts, and the reader can pick them out of a crowd.

## The story

Your friend goes back to the station the next week, for your aunt's second visit. This time
your message is a paragraph: who she is, what she looks like, how she moves, what she will be
carrying. He reads it twice on the platform, looks up, and walks straight to her. She is
surprised; she has never met him. "Your nephew said you would be at the back, with the red
bag," he tells her. She laughs. You were not there, and you found her for him.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **describe** — say what someone or something is like, in words.
- **look like** — how a person appears: height, hair, face, clothes.
- **be like** — how a person is: kind, quiet, funny, slow.
- **describing word** — a word that says what something is like: short, grey, kind.
- **platform** — the place at a station where you wait for the train.

In [part 2.1](2.1-what-a-paragraph-is.md) a paragraph was several sentences about one topic.
Today the topic is a person, and the sentences go in an order the reader expects, from
outside to inside: **who** they are, **what they look like**, **what they are like**, and **one
thing they do**. The reader builds the picture in that order, the way you would notice a
stranger.

The verbs that do most of the work are ones you know.
[Is](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md) joins the person to a
**describing word**: she is short. [Has](../../../day-09-introducing-yourself/parts/02-writing/2.2-have-for-what-you-are-like.md)
joins the person to a feature: she has grey hair. A describing word says what something is
like, and it goes after is, or before the thing it describes: a red bag.

## The rule

The order, and the sentence shapes for each step:

| Step | What it tells | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | who | Name + is + my + family word. / Name + is + a + job. |
| 2 | look like | She is + describing word. She has + feature. She wears + clothes. |
| 3 | be like | She is + describing word for character. |
| 4 | one thing they do | She + verb with -s + the rest. |

> My aunt Sunita is my mother's older sister. She is short and thin, with grey hair tied back.
> She wears glasses with thin gold frames. She is quiet and very kind. She walks slowly and
> always carries a red cloth bag.

Six sentences, one person, one block. Step 4 is the one that finds her on a platform.

The shape "She's very good at her job" and "She's lovely" for describing a person with is plus
a describing word was seen on 2026-09-22 at
[Talking about other people](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/talking-about-other-people).
The forms of be were checked at
[Present simple: 'to be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-be)
and has for one person at
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got),
opened the same day.

Describing words you may need, in pairs: tall and short; thin and heavy; young and old; quiet
and loud; kind and strict; slow and quick. Pick the true one.

The trap: "and" holding three describing words in one sentence, then four, then five. "She is
short and thin and quiet and kind and slow." One sentence for how she looks; another for how
she is. Two describing words per sentence is plenty.

## When it breaks

You write how she is before who she is:

> She is quiet and kind. She has grey hair. My aunt Sunita is my mother's sister.

The reader holds two facts about a stranger before they learn she is your aunt. The fix: who
first.

You mix look-like and be-like in one sentence:

> She is short and kind.

Not wrong, but the reader's picture jumps from body to character mid-sentence. The fix: "She is
short. She is kind." Or keep like with like: short and thin; quiet and kind.

You leave out step 4:

> My aunt is short. She has grey hair. She wears glasses. She is kind.

All true and she is still invisible on a platform, because half the aunts in the world match.
The fix: one thing she does or carries that others do not. "She always carries a red cloth
bag."

If your paragraph is out of order, or has no step 4, it is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers describe people in exactly this order without knowing it is an order. Read any
message that says "look out for my colleague, he's tall with a beard and he'll be wearing a
blue jacket" and you will find who, look like, one thing. They skip be-like when the reader
only needs to find the person, and add it when the reader will have to get along with them.

A friend at a platform needs steps 1, 2 and 4. A landlord being told who will collect the keys
needs step 1 and 4. A teacher being told about your child needs step 3 most. The order stays;
which steps you keep depends on the reader.
