## One-line answer

Copy your paragraph as a list of words, as one run-on sentence, and with a sentence about something else inside it, on purpose, read each as the friend at the gate, mark all three, then rebuild the block.

## The story

Your friend keeps the three messages you sent him about your aunt over the weeks and shows
them to you. The first was five words with full stops; he went home alone. The second was one
long sentence with "and" nine times; he read it, forgot the beginning by the end, and asked a
woman with a blue bag. The third was a paragraph with a sentence about the train timetable in
the middle; he spent a minute checking the board instead of watching the gate. The fourth
found her. Three ways to break a paragraph, and he lived through all of them.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong versions are deliberate. They rest on what beginners
commonly write, not on a mistake found in your own page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **run-on** — sentences joined together with no full stop, from
  [day 3](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md).
- **rebuild** — put back together properly.
- **read back** — read your own page again, slowly, as if it were someone else's, from
  [day 2](../../../day-02-long-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.3-a-form-nobody-can-use.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have what a paragraph is from [part 2.1](2.1-what-a-paragraph-is.md) and a paragraph
about a person from [part 2.2](2.2-describe-a-person.md). Now you break it the three ways
beginners really do: as a list of words, as one run-on sentence, and with a stray sentence
about another topic inside. You read each as the friend at the gate with the crowd coming out.
Then you rebuild.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is ten sentences a
stranger can follow, and the three breaks here are the three ways ten sentences stop being
followable. If your read-back cannot catch them planted today, it will not catch them on a page
you wrote in a hurry.

## The rule

Under your correct paragraph from part 2.2:

1. Copy it three ways, each broken on purpose: as a list of words with full stops and no
   verbs; as one sentence with every full stop replaced by "and"; and as a paragraph with one
   sentence about a different topic dropped into the middle.
2. Cover the correct paragraph.
3. Read each broken version out loud as the friend at the gate. After each, ask: "Can I see
   her? Where did I lose her?" For the list, you never had a picture. For the run-on, you lost
   the beginning by the end. For the stray sentence, you looked away from the gate.
4. Mark the break in each RED. Three marks, one per version.
5. Uncover. Rebuild the paragraph from the pieces, on a new line, and mark it GREEN.

The model, broken three ways:

> Short. Grey hair. Glasses. Red bag. Slow.

> My aunt Sunita is my mother's sister and she is short and she has grey hair and she wears
> glasses and she is kind and she walks slowly and she carries a red bag.

> My aunt Sunita is my mother's sister. She is short, with grey hair. The train is usually ten
> minutes late. She wears glasses and carries a red bag.

Three RED marks: no verbs and no sentences; one sentence where six belong; a sentence about
the train inside a paragraph about the aunt.

The paragraph rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[paragraph](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/paragraph): "several
sentences dealing with a single subject".

## When it breaks

You read the broken versions back and mark them GREEN:

> Short. Grey hair. Glasses. Red bag. Slow. — GREEN

You saw your aunt because you know her. The friend does not. The check itself failed. Read
again and describe out loud, from the words alone, the person you would look for. If you
cannot say a full sentence about her, it is RED.

Another break: you rebuild and drop a sentence.

> My aunt Sunita is my mother's sister. She is short, with grey hair. She wears glasses and
> carries a red bag.

The stray train sentence is gone, and so is "she walks slowly", which was never marked. A
rebuild puts back every piece of the original and removes only the break.

If any planted break was marked GREEN, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody breaks a paragraph on purpose. Everybody writes the list when in a hurry, the run-on
when talking onto the page, and the stray sentence when a thought arrives mid-paragraph.
Fluent writers do all three in a first draft. The difference is that they read back as the
reader, and the reader at the station gate has no time and no picture but yours.

A friend forgives all three and goes back to the station next week. A form, an office, or a
stranger reads the page once. Keep the three broken versions on your practice page as proof
that your read-back can go RED.

- **Say out loud:** Which of the three breaks was hardest to see as a break, and what did the
