# Where the light lands

`PR-06` · Day 13

## The one-line answer

Every English sentence points a beam of light at one word, and moving that beam changes what the
sentence means without changing a single word in it.

## The model

Say this out loud four times, and each time point the beam at a different word — make that one
word louder, longer and a little higher, and let everything else go small.

> I never said that.
> I never said that.
> I never said that.
> I never said that.

Four words, four sentences. One of them means somebody else said it. One means you deny it
completely. One means you may have written it or thought it but you did not say it. One means you
said something, just not *that*.

Nothing changed except where the light landed. That is the whole day.

**Where it lands by default.** Left alone, the beam falls near the end of the sentence, on the
last word carrying real information. Real information means the words you would keep if you had
to shorten the sentence to almost nothing — the ones you'd type in a hurry. The small connecting
words are not lit; they sit in the dark and get short and quiet, and that is correct, not lazy.

**Where it lands when you're correcting.** This is the part that matters in conversation. The
moment you are fixing, denying or distinguishing something, the beam leaves the default position
and goes onto the one word that is different from what the other person said.

> It wasn't me who left it open.
> I did tell you.
> I meant the other one.
> No, the other way round.

Say each of those with the light on one word only — *me*, then *did*, then *other*, then *other*.
Then say the
same lines with the beam back at the end, where it would normally go. Both versions are correct
English. Only one of them corrects anybody.

**What learners do instead.** They spread the light evenly. Every word the same size, the same
length, the same weight. It sounds careful, and it costs you two things at once: the sentence
turns flat, and — much worse — the correction stops arriving. You said the right words, the other
person did not hear that you were disagreeing, and they carry on with the same wrong idea.

Lighting every word is the same as lighting none.

**Today's phrases** — every one of them lives or dies on where you put the beam. All of these go
into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I did tell you. | Insisting something happened, when it's being denied |
| It wasn't me who … | Putting an accusation somewhere else without a fight |
| You did, actually. | Contradicting someone politely, in three words |
| I never said that. | A flat denial that needs the light in exactly the right place |
| I meant the other one. | Fixing a mix-up without restarting the sentence |
| It's not about … , it's about … | Moving an argument off the wrong thing onto the right one |
| That's the whole point. | Landing your own argument, or agreeing hard |
| No, the other way round. | Correcting a reversal |

## Say it now

Say each line three times, with your own content. Each time, decide out loud which word the beam
is on **before** you say it.

1. *I did tell you about {{…}}.*
2. *It wasn't me who {{…}}.*
3. *I meant the other one, not {{…}}.*
4. *It's not about {{…}}, it's about {{…}}.*
5. *No, the other way round — {{…}}.*

Then take line 4 on its own and say it once with the light on both nouns and on nothing else.
Everything between them small, short and dark. That line has exactly two lit words in it, and if
a third one is lit the contrast disappears.

Finally, run all five as one continuous stretch — someone correcting a misunderstanding without
pausing between sentences. The joins are where the beam drifts back to the ends, and the joins are
the exercise.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d13-free.m4a`.**

> **Talk about something people regularly get wrong about you, your work, or where you're from.**
> Not the polite version. What they assume, what is actually true, and what the difference is.

The topic is chosen because it forces contrast into every other sentence. Do not plan which words
to light. Light them as you go, and find out afterwards where they landed.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** they must interrupt three times with a wrong summary — *"so you're saying
  …"* — getting it deliberately slightly wrong each time. You correct each one on the spot and
  keep going.

## The deliberate failure

Take three of today's phrases and record them twice.

First pass: **light every word.** Same volume, same length, same weight, none of them small.
*I. Did. Tell. You.*

Second pass: the same three, one lit word each, everything else allowed to go dark.

**What breaking sounds like:** the first pass sounds like a machine reading, which you will expect.
What you probably will not expect is the second thing — play it to yourself and try to work out
what was being corrected. You can't. With everything lit, *I did tell you* stops being an
insistence and becomes a sentence with no argument in it at all.

Then listen to what came back in the second pass: the line got shorter and quicker without you
speeding up, because the unlit words shrank on their own. That shrinking is not sloppiness. It is
what makes the lit word visible.

## When it breaks in the wild

Someone repeats back what you said and gets one detail wrong. You correct them — *"I said the
small one"* — with every word the same size. They nod, agree, and then carry on using the wrong
detail, because nothing in your sentence told them which part had changed. So you say it again,
louder. Louder is not brighter; it lights everything equally, and now you sound annoyed as well as
unclear.

**The repair:**

> Not the big one — the small one.

Say the wrong thing and the right thing next to each other, with the light on the two words that
differ and nothing else. Putting the contrast in the sentence itself means the beam has nowhere
else to go, and it lands correctly even when you are tired or rushed.
