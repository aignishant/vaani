## One-line answer

The people in your family each have one English word, and you say them with I have, a number, and the -s when there is more than one.

## The story

At lunch a colleague shows you a photo on her phone: "That's my mum, that's my brother, and
that's his little girl." You show yours. You point and say "Mother. This, father. This is…" and
stop, because you do not have the word for your father's brother. She waits. You say "uncle?"
and she says "Yes! Your uncle." You had the people. You were missing some of the words, and
the shape to put them in.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **family word** — a word for a person in your family: mother, uncle.
- **older** — born before you.
- **younger** — born after you.
- **point** — show with a finger.
- **colleague** — a person you work with.

Every person in a family has an English word, and the words come in pairs: mother and father,
sister and brother, daughter and son, wife and husband, aunt and uncle, grandmother and
grandfather. In speech, mother and father are often mum and dad, and grandmother and
grandfather are often grandma and grandpa.

To say how many you have, use **have**, from
[day 9](../../../day-09-introducing-yourself/parts/02-writing/2.1-have-and-has.md) in its
spoken shape, then a number, then the family word with its **-s** for more than one, from
[day 4](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md). I have two
brothers. I have one sister.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 self-introduction](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) has a
sentence about your people, and a stranger asks about family before anything else. Every
speaking day from here may reach for a family word. Have them ready, with their shapes, so the
photo on your phone is a conversation and not a list of pauses.

## The rule

The pairs, with the ones that are said differently marked:

| Woman | Man |
| --- | --- |
| mother (mum) | father (dad) |
| sister | brother |
| daughter | son |
| wife | husband |
| aunt | uncle |
| grandmother (grandma) | grandfather (grandpa) |

Add older or younger in front of brother and sister when it matters: my older sister, my
younger brother.

The shape for how many:

> I have two brothers and one sister.
> I have one daughter.
> I have no brothers or sisters.

Three sounds to watch. Mother, father and brother have the
[voiced th from day 3](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/01-speaking/1.2-the-same-place-with-voice.md)
in the middle: tongue between the teeth, voice on. Daughter has a silent gh; say "daw-ter".
Every two-syllable family word has its
[stress](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-loud-syllable.md) on the
first syllable: MOther, FAther, SISter.

The trap: English has one word, cousin, for the children of your aunts and uncles, and it does
not say boy or girl or older or younger. "Cousin brother" and "cousin sister" are used in some
places, and a listener from elsewhere will not know them. Say "my cousin", and add "he" or
"she" in the next sentence if it matters.

## When it breaks

You leave the -s off after a number:

> I have two brother.

The listener hears two and then one, and waits. The fix: two brothers.

You say the th in mother and brother as a d:

> I have one mudder and two brudders.

Understood, and marked. The fix: tongue between the teeth, voice on.

You say a word from your own language for a person because the English word is missing:

> This is my … chacha.

The listener does not know who that is. The fix: "my uncle, my father's brother." Two words
you have.

If, on playback, a family word after a number has no -s, or mother, father and brother have no
th, the check is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use the short warm words, mum, dad, grandma, with friends, and the full words,
mother, father, on forms and with strangers. They point at photos and say "That's my…" for
each, and they add one fact per person: "That's my brother. He lives in Dubai." One word, one
fact, next person.

A native listener follows a photo tour easily if each person gets a word and a fact. They lose
the thread if a person gets a pause and a word from another language. They will never mind
"my uncle, my father's brother"; that is exactly what they would say.
