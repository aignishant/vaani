## One-line answer

Record your photo tour with he and she swapped and the -s dropped on purpose, play it back as the colleague looking at the photo, notice who she is looking at, then record it right beside it.

## The story

You test it on your colleague with the photo. "This is my sister. He is a nurse." Her eyes go
to your brother. "This is my brother. She works in a bank." Her eyes go to your sister. She
is not confused; she is following the words exactly, and the words point at the wrong faces.
Then you do it right, and her eyes go where yours do. The photo did not change. The small
words did.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong versions are deliberate. They rest on what beginners
whose first language does not separate he and she commonly do, not on a mistake found in your
own recordings.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **swap** — put one where the other was.
- **look at** — turn the eyes to.
- **repair** — a small correction that helps someone understand, from
  [day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.3-change-one-sound.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the family words from [part 1.1](1.1-the-family-words.md) and the two-sentence tour
from [part 1.2](1.2-talk-about-your-own-family.md). Now you break the tour the two ways that
happen under pressure: he for she and she for he; and the -s dropped from the fact verb. You
listen as the colleague and ask, at each fact, "whose face am I looking at?" Then you record
the repair beside it.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), a stranger asks
about your family and you answer with he and she a dozen times in a minute. One swap and they
picture the wrong person for the rest of the recording. A learner who has heard their own swap
from the outside chooses the word before the fact, every time.

## The rule

Two rows, recorder on. Wrong first, then right, with a gap.

**Row 1 — swapped.**

> This is my sister. He is a nurse. This is my brother. She works in a bank.
> This is my sister. She is a nurse. This is my brother. He works in a bank.

**Row 2 — no -s.**

> This is my father. He drive a taxi.
> This is my father. He drives a taxi.

Play row 1. Be the colleague with the photo. At "He is a nurse", say out loud whose face you
look at. If you say "the sister", you are not yet listening as a stranger; the stranger looks
at the brother, because he means a man. Play it again and follow only the words.

Play row 2. Say what you heard at "drive". Most listeners hear it as a small slip, not a
different meaning, and that is the point: the swap changes the person; the missing -s only
marks you. Both are worth fixing; the swap is worth fixing first.

Then the repair, in one breath, as it happens when you hear yourself:

> This is my sister. He — she — is a nurse.

The he and she rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Personal pronouns](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/personal-pronouns):
"We use he/him to refer to men, and she/her to refer to women." The -s was checked at
[Present simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple).

## When it breaks

The break this part is about: as the colleague, you look at the right face anyway.

> This is my sister. He is a nurse. → "I look at the sister."

You knew who you meant. The colleague follows the word. If you cannot make yourself look at
the brother, play the wrong row with your eyes closed and picture only what the words say. A
man who is a nurse. That is who she saw.

A second break: the repair restarts the whole tour.

> This is my sister. He is a nurse. — Sorry. This is my mother. She lives with us. This is my
> sister…

You went back to the beginning and the listener now has the mother twice. The fix: repair one
word. "He — she — is a nurse." And on.

If you cannot tell, as the listener, which row was wrong by whose face you looked at, the
check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody swaps he and she on purpose. Everybody whose first language has one word for both, or
marks it in a different place, swaps them under pressure for years, and native listeners
quietly re-sort the faces and say nothing. It is one of the most common marks of a learner,
and one of the easiest to hear once you have heard it from the outside.

A native listener forgives a swap in a long story about many people. In a photo tour, where
each sentence points at one face, they follow the wrong word to the wrong face every time. The
one-word repair, "he — she —", costs nothing and fluent speakers do it too.
