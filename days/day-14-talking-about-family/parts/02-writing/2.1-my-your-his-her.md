## One-line answer

My, your, his, her, our and their go in front of a thing to say whose it is, and each one matches the person: my for I, his for he, her for she.

## The story

Four of you share a kitchen and things go missing. You put a sticker on your milk: "milk". It
goes anyway. Your flatmate puts a sticker on hers: "my milk — Priya". Nobody touches it. Then
someone leaves a note on the shelf: "Whose cup is this?" and you write "her cup" under it,
pointing at Priya's name. One small word in front of a thing, and everyone knows whose.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **whose** — belonging to which person.
- **belong to** — be owned by.
- **sticker** — a small label that sticks on.
- **match** — go with the right person.
- **shelf** — a flat board for putting things on.

There are six small words in English that go in front of a thing and say **whose** it is. They
are called **possessive words**, from the word possess, which means own. Each one belongs to a
person word you know from the verb
[be](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md):

- I → **my**
- you → **your**
- he → **his**
- she → **her**
- we → **our**
- they → **their**

The possessive word goes right in front of the
[noun](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md), the thing owned,
and nothing goes between them. My milk. Her cup. Their shelf. It does not change for one or
many: my cup, my cups.

## The rule

| Person | Whose word | Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | my | my milk |
| you | your | your cup |
| he | his | his shelf |
| she | her | her bag |
| we | our | our kitchen |
| they | their | their fridge |

> This is my milk.
> Is this your cup?
> Her bag is on his shelf.

Two checks for every possessive word you write. Finger on the word: who owns it? Does the word
match that person? Finger on the next word: is it the thing owned, with nothing in between?

The six words and what they do were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Possessives: adjectives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/possessives-adjectives),
which lists my, your, his, her, its, our, their and says they show "something belongs" to
somebody, and are used for family "relations and friends" and "parts of the body". The person
words they match were seen at
[Personal pronouns](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/personal-pronouns),
opened the same day.

Two traps. **His and her** follow the owner, not the thing. A man's bag is his bag, even though
bags have no man or woman in them. If your first language matches the word to the thing, this
feels backwards; follow the owner. **Their** is spelled t-h-e-i-r. There is another word,
there, that sounds the same and means a place. On a label, their milk means the milk belongs
to them.

## When it breaks

You match the word to the thing instead of the owner:

> Priya left her bag. His bag is red.

You meant the same bag. The reader now sees a man's bag. The fix: her bag, because Priya owns
it.

You put a word between the possessive and the thing:

> my the milk

The reader stops. Nothing goes between. The fix: my milk.

You write there for their:

> there milk

The reader sees a place word in front of milk and looks for a place. The fix: their.

If a possessive word on your page does not match its owner, or has a word between it and the
thing, that sentence is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers use these six words in almost every sentence about people and never notice.
What they do notice is a wrong one, because "his bag" and "her bag" send the eye to different
people. On a shared shelf, a form, a message to a school about your child, the possessive word
is what tells the reader whose life you are describing.

A friend reading "his bag" about Priya laughs. A form that says "his name" about a daughter
gets filed wrong. The word is small and it carries the whole person.
