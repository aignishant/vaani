## One-line answer

To say a named person owns a thing, write the name, an apostrophe, and s, Priya's cup, and the apostrophe is the only thing that tells a reader this is not the plural cups.

## The story

The kitchen has a new rule: label your things with your name. You write "Priyas cup" on a
sticker for your flatmate. The next morning there are two cups on the shelf and a note under
your sticker: "Which Priyas?" Someone read your label as many Priyas. You add one small mark,
Priya's, and the joke ends. The mark is the difference between one owner and many people.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **owner** — the person a thing belongs to.
- **label** — a small piece of paper with writing, stuck on a thing.
- **differ** — be different.
- **rule** — something everyone in the kitchen has agreed to do.

In [part 2.1](2.1-my-your-his-her.md) you said whose with a small word: her cup. When you want
the owner's **name** instead, English adds two marks to the name: an
[apostrophe](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.2-short-forms-and-not.md), the
small mark ', and then **s**. Priya's cup. Ravi's shelf. The apostrophe s means "belonging to".

This is the same apostrophe you met in short forms on day 6, doing a different job. In I'm it
shows a missing letter. In Priya's it shows owning. The mark is the same; the reader knows
which job it is doing from the word before it: a name or a person means owning.

The trap is one letter away. **Cups**, with s and no apostrophe, means more than one cup, the
[plural](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md) from day 4.
**Cup's** means belonging to the cup. On a label the apostrophe is the only thing that tells
the reader which you meant.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every page about your family from here uses names with 's: my mother's shop, my brother's
bike. The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) will have at least
one. Get the mark in the right place now, and get the plural -s to stay without it.

## The rule

| To say | Write | Not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the cup belonging to Priya | Priya's cup | Priyas cup |
| the shelf belonging to my brother | my brother's shelf | my brothers shelf |
| more than one cup | cups | cup's |
| the cups belonging to Priya | Priya's cups | Priyas' cups |

> Priya's cup is the blue one.
> My brother's shelf is the top one.
> These two cups are Ravi's.

The last model shows the name standing alone at the end: "Ravi's" with the thing left off,
because the reader already knows it is cups.

The rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Possessive 's](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/possessive-s):
"When it is one person who owns something, we usually use 's", with "Simon's phone is new";
and the warning that "s at the end of a word without an apostrophe (') can make it plural,
but this doesn't show possession". The same rule was seen at
[Possessives: nouns](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/possessives-nouns):
"We add 's to singular nouns to show possession", with "John's house", and the standing-alone
use, "No, it's Mary's."

Two fingers for every 's you write. Finger on the word before the apostrophe: is it a person
or a name? Finger on the word after: is it the thing owned? Both yes, and the mark is right.

For more than one owner, the mark moves after the plural s: my parents' room, the room of both
parents. You will not need that often today. Know it exists, so parents' does not look like a
mistake when you meet it.

## When it breaks

You leave the apostrophe out of a name:

> Priyas cup

The reader sees many Priyas. The fix: Priya's.

You put an apostrophe into a plain plural:

> Two cup's on the shelf.

The reader looks for what belongs to the cup. The fix: cups. A plural has no apostrophe.

You add the apostrophe s to the thing instead of the owner:

> Priya cup's

The reader sees a cup that owns something. The fix: the mark goes on the owner. Priya's cup.

If a name on your page owns something and has no apostrophe s, or a plural has an apostrophe,
that sentence is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers put 's on names without thinking, and the ones who slip put apostrophes into
plurals: "apple's" on a market sign is so common it has a nickname. Readers notice it and think
less of the sign. On a label, a form or a message about whose thing is whose, the mark is read
every time.

A flatmate reading "Priyas cup" makes a joke. A form asking for your father's name, a message
to a school about your daughter's teacher, a note about a neighbour's parcel: these are read
by people who act on them, and "neighbours parcel" is a parcel for several neighbours.
