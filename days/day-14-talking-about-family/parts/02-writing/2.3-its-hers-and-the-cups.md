## One-line answer

Copy your kitchen labels with three breaks on purpose, a his for a her, a name with no apostrophe, and a plural with one, read them as a flatmate who moved in yesterday, mark all three, then fix only those.

## The story

A new flatmate arrives and reads the shelf. "His cup" on a cup she has just seen Priya
drinking from. "Ravis shelf" and she asks how many Ravis live here. "Two spoon's" and she
looks for what the spoons own. She does not know anyone yet, so she cannot fix your labels in
her head. She reads exactly what is there, and what is there points at the wrong people and
the wrong number.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong version is deliberate. It rests on what beginners
commonly write, not on a mistake found in your own page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **new flatmate** — a reader who knows nobody, so cannot guess.
- **whose-word** — my, your, his, her, our, their, or a name with 's.
- **read back** — read your own page again, slowly, as if it were someone else's, from
  [day 2](../../../day-02-long-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.3-a-form-nobody-can-use.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the six small words from [part 2.1](2.1-my-your-his-her.md) and the apostrophe s
from [part 2.2](2.2-the-apostrophe-s.md). Now you plant the three breaks beginners really make,
and you read as the one reader who cannot fill in the gaps: someone who moved in yesterday.

One more thing to know before you break anything. The word **its**, with no apostrophe, is the
whose-word for it: the cat and its bowl. **It's**, with an apostrophe, is the short form of it
is. They look one mark apart and mean different things. You will meet it's on labels written
by others; on yours, its bowl has no apostrophe.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is read by a stranger who
knows none of your people. Every his, her and 's on it sends their eye to a person. If your
read-back cannot catch three planted wrong whose-words today, it will not catch the real one
that gives your sister a brother's bike.

## The rule

Under your correct labels from parts 2.1 and 2.2:

1. Copy them with three deliberate breaks: swap one his for her or her for his; drop the
   apostrophe from one name with 's; put an apostrophe into one plain plural.
2. Cover the correct labels.
3. Read the broken labels out loud as the new flatmate, who knows nobody. At each whose-word,
   ask: "Which person do I picture?" and "One or many?" If you picture the wrong person, or
   cannot tell one from many, that is a break.
4. Mark each RED. There should be three.
5. Uncover. Fix only the three, on a new line, and mark it GREEN.

The model, broken on purpose:

> his cup — Priya. Ravis shelf. Two spoon's.

Three RED marks: his should be her, because Priya; Ravis should be Ravi's; spoon's should be
spoons.

The rules were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Possessives: adjectives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/possessives-adjectives),
which also carries the its warning: "The possessive adjective its does not have an apostrophe
(')" and "it's always means it is or it has"; and at
[Possessive 's](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/possessive-s):
an s "without an apostrophe (') can make it plural, but this doesn't show possession".

## When it breaks

You read the broken labels back and mark them GREEN:

> his cup — Priya. Ravis shelf. Two spoon's. — GREEN

You know Priya, so "his" slid past; you know there is one Ravi, so "Ravis" slid past. The
check itself failed. Read again as someone who has met nobody, and say out loud who you
picture at each word. A man at "his". Several Ravis at "Ravis".

Another break: you fix the plural by removing the s as well.

> Two spoon.

The apostrophe was the break, not the s. Two spoons keeps its s and loses its mark. One fix
per mark, and the fix is the smallest one.

If any planted break was marked GREEN, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody plants a wrong his. But everyone whose first language matches the whose-word to the
thing, or has no apostrophe, makes these three breaks for months, and readers who know the
people fix them silently. Readers who do not, a new flatmate, a form, an office, follow the
word to the wrong person or the wrong number.

A flatmate asks how many Ravis. A form that says "his" about your daughter, or "the
neighbours parcel" about one neighbour, is acted on as written. Keep the broken labels on your
practice page as proof that your read-back can go RED.
