# What happened, and the beat you keep adding

`PR-07` · `FN-03` · Day 14

## The one-line answer

Telling what happened is one setup, one turn and one landing — and the past-tense ending that
rides on every verb in it is three different sounds, only one of which is allowed to be its own
beat.

## The model

Think of a spoken account as a short train. The setup is the engine, the turn is the point where
the track bends, and the landing is where it stops. Most learners couple up plenty of carriages
and never bend the track — so the listener hears a lot of movement and nothing happening.

**A small account has exactly one turn.** Not a plot. One place where it stopped being what you
expected:

> The other day I was trying to sort out my bank card, right, and the machine just ate it.
> That's when I realised the branch shuts early on a Wednesday.
> So anyway, I ended up doing the whole thing on the phone instead.

Setup, turn, landing. Three moves. Anything longer is the same three moves with more scenery.

**The markers do the coupling.** *The other day* opens without you having to date anything.
*That's when* announces the bend before it arrives, so the listener leans in. *So anyway* drags
you back when you have wandered — and you will wander, and that is fine, because *so anyway* is
free.

Now the ending, because it is riding on every verb you just said.

**Every regular past verb drags an `-ed`, and that `-ed` is three different sounds.** Which one
you get is decided entirely by the sound in front of it — not by the spelling, not by the verb.

Put two fingers on your throat and say *sss*, then *zzz*. The *zzz* buzzes. That buzz is the
whole test.

**No buzz in front of it → a plain `t`.** *Asked* is *askt*. *Walked* is *walkt*. *Finished* is
*finisht*. *Stopped* is *stopt*. *Watched* is *watcht*. The word does **not** get longer. You met
*asked* on Day 11 as a cluster to survive; today it comes back with a `t` welded on the end of
that cluster, which is why it is the single most reliable place to catch yourself.

**A buzz in front of it → a `d`.** *Called* is *calld*. *Played*, *moved*, *arrived*, *opened*,
*showed*. Again — no extra beat. The `d` is a tail, not a carriage.

**Only after a `t` or a `d` sound does it become its own beat.** *Wanted*, *needed*, *started*,
*decided*, *ended*. Say those and you will hear a genuine extra syllable, and it belongs there,
because *want-d* is unsayable.

That is the whole rule, and it is entirely checkable in your own mouth. The trap is not getting
it wrong occasionally. The trap is doing what the spelling tells you and giving every single one
of them a carriage — *walk-ed, ask-ed, finish-ed* — which is exactly when a past-tense story
starts sounding like a chant.

**Today's phrases** — check `docs/CHUNKS.md` first. Days 5 and 9 have story lines that pair
with these.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| The other day, … | Opening a past account without having to date it |
| That's when … | Announcing the turn just before you deliver it |
| It turned out … | The reveal — what you did not know at the time |
| I ended up …ing | Where you landed, skipping how you got there |
| Next thing I knew, … | Jumping over a dull middle without apologising |
| Which was fine, until … | Planting the complication before it lands |
| So anyway, … | Steering back after you have wandered off |
| …and that was the end of that. | Closing flat, when the outcome was a dead end |

## Say it now

Say each three times, filling in your own content. Nothing written down. On every line, catch the
`-ed` before you say the next word.

1. *The other day I asked {{someone}} about {{something}}, and it turned out {{…}}.*
2. *I'd planned to {{…}}, but I ended up {{…}}ing instead.*
3. *I waited for {{…}}, and that's when {{…}}.*
4. *Which was fine, until {{…}}. So anyway, {{…}}, and that was the end of that.*

Now join all four into one continuous account, twice, without stopping. On the second run,
deliberately let one wander — go off on something irrelevant, then bring it back with *so
anyway*. The recovery is the rep.

## The deliberate failure

Tell a short past account in which you give **every single `-ed` its own beat.** *I walk-ed to
the shop, I ask-ed the man, he check-ed the back, I finish-ed and I left.* Force it. Make every
one a carriage.

**What breaking sounds like:** the train grows even. Every verb the same weight, the same length,
a small drum landing at the end of each one. The sentence stops having a shape and starts having
a pulse — and once you hear that pulse you will never again mistake it for careful speech, which
is what it feels like from the inside.

Now say the same account with the endings welded on. Listen for what came back: the stressed
words stand up again, and the account gets shorter without losing a word. That shortening is the
whole of `PR-07`.

## When it breaks in the wild

Someone at work asks what happened with the thing you were handling. You start, you hit a regular
past verb, you feel the ending coming and you brake — you slow down to place it correctly. By the
time you have placed it you have lost the thread of the account, and you finish with something
vague because the story fell off the rails behind you.

The ending is never worth the thread. If you have to choose, say the wrong ending fast rather
than the right one late — nobody has ever lost a conversation over a `t` that should have been a
`d`, and everybody has lost one by stopping.

**The repair:**

> So anyway — it turned out fine, and that was the end of that.

Land the account, then go back and fix your mouth on the next one.
