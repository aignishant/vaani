## One-line answer

A day is told with a small set of verbs, get up, have breakfast, go to work, come home, go to bed, each said with I and a time, and the verb comes before the time.

## The story

The doctor asks, "Tell me about a normal day. When do you sleep, when do you eat?" You want
to help. You say, "Morning six. Office nine. Night late." She writes something and asks again,
slower. You gave her times. You did not give her what you do at them, so she cannot tell if
"night late" means you eat late or sleep late, and that was the whole question.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **routine** — the things you do every day, in the same order.
- **get up** — leave your bed.
- **have** — here, eat or drink: have breakfast, have tea.
- **come home** — return to your house.
- **normal** — usual; like most days.

A **routine** is what you do most days, in the order you do it. English tells a routine with a
small set of **verbs**, words that say what someone does, from
[day 6](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md). Many of them are
two words that act as one: get up, go to work, come home, go to bed. Learn each pair as one
piece, the way you learned chunks on
[day 9](../../../day-09-introducing-yourself/parts/01-speaking/1.2-say-it-with-your-eyes-off-the-page.md).

The sentence shape is I, then the verb, then the time. I get up at six. The verb comes first,
because it is the thing the listener wants; the time comes after, with the small word at in
front of it. The times are the ones you learned to say on
[day 10](../../../day-10-numbers-times-dates/parts/01-speaking/1.2-times-and-dates-out-loud.md).

## The rule

The verbs of a day, in the order a day happens:

| Part of the day | Verb |
| --- | --- |
| morning | wake up · get up · have a shower · get dressed · have breakfast · go to work / go to school |
| middle | have lunch · work / study |
| evening | come home · have dinner · watch TV |
| night | go to bed |

The shape:

> I get up at six.
> I have breakfast at half past seven.
> I go to bed at eleven.

Say each with the recorder on. The verb is a loud word, from
[day 13](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/01-speaking/1.1-loud-words-and-quiet-words.md);
at is quiet; the time is loud. I GET UP at SIX.

The list of routine verbs was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Daily Routine Vocabulary](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/vocabulary/a1-a2/daily-routine-vocabulary-a1-beginner-english-vocabulary-lesson),
which teaches "wake up", "get dressed", "have breakfast", "go to work", "have lunch", "go
home", "have dinner", "watch TV" and "go to bed", with model lines such as "I go to work at a
quarter past eight." The two-word verbs
[get up](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/get-up) and
[wake up](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/wake-up) each carry a
sound button, opened the same day, as does
[breakfast](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/breakfast)
/ˈbrekfəst/, with the stress on the first syllable.

Two traps. **Have** with meals means eat: I have breakfast, not I eat breakfast, though eat is
not wrong. And **go to bed** is what you do at night; **sleep** is what happens after. "I go
to bed at eleven" tells the doctor when your night begins.

## When it breaks

You give the time and not the verb:

> Six. Nine. Late.

The listener has a timetable with nothing on it. The fix: I get up at six. I go to work at
nine. I go to bed late.

You use the wrong small word before the time:

> I get up in six.

Understood, and marked. Times take at. The fix: at six.

You split the two-word verb:

> I up get at six.

The listener hears two words looking for each other. The fix: the pair stays together. Get up.

If, on playback, any routine sentence has no verb, or the verb comes after the time, the check
is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers tell a routine in exactly these verbs, and they add the small words that show
it repeats: "I usually get up at six." You will meet usually and always and never soon. For
now the bare verb and the time is a full answer, and it is what the doctor wanted.

A native listener asking about your day is listening for the verbs. Give them "get up",
"eat", "sleep" and they can help you. Give them times alone and they have to ask each one
again.
