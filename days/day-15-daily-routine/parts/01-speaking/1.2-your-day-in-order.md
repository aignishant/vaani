## One-line answer

Tell your day as eight sentences in the order it happens, joined by then and after that, and the listener walks through it with you.

## The story

The doctor asks again, and this time you are ready. "I get up at six. Then I have tea. After
that I go to work at nine. I have lunch at one. I come home at seven. Then I have dinner with
my family. After that I watch TV. I go to bed at eleven." She writes as you speak, nodding at
each line, and at the end she says, "So dinner is late, and bed is soon after. That may be the
problem." She could see your day because you walked her through it in order.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **in order** — first thing first, last thing last.
- **then** — the next thing after this one.
- **after that** — the same as then, a little slower.
- **walk through** — go through one step at a time.
- **problem** — the thing that is wrong.

You have the routine verbs from [part 1.1](1.1-the-routine-verbs.md). A routine told out of
order is a pile of true sentences. A routine told **in order**, morning to night, is a day the
listener can follow. Two small words join the sentences and tell the listener "next": **then**,
and **after that**. You do not need one on every sentence. One every two or three keeps the
listener moving.

Eight sentences is a good size: two for the morning, two for the middle, two for the evening,
two for the night. Each has I, a verb, and most have a time.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 self-introduction](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is at least
ten sentences a stranger can follow. Eight sentences in time order are the easiest ten
sentences you will ever say, because the order is given to you by the clock. Practise
following an order now, and the introduction on day 20 has a spine.

## The rule

1. Write your eight sentences, one for each step of your real day, in the order they happen.
2. Put then at the start of two of them and after that at the start of one or two. Never on
   two sentences in a row.
3. Say them at the mirror with a [pause](../../../day-09-introducing-yourself/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-five-spoken-sentences.md)
   after each. Then and after that are quiet words; the verb and the time are loud.
4. Record the eight. Then cover the page and record them again from memory. The order will
   hold you up; that is what it is for.

The model, for a day that is not yours:

> I get up at six. Then I have tea. After that I go to work at nine. I have lunch at one. I
> come home at seven. Then I have dinner with my family. After that I watch TV. I go to bed at
> eleven.

The routine shape and the joining word were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Daily Routine Vocabulary](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/vocabulary/a1-a2/daily-routine-vocabulary-a1-beginner-english-vocabulary-lesson),
whose model day runs in order from "I get up early in the morning" through "I go to work at a
quarter past eight" to "After that I usually watch TV" and "I go to bed at 10:00 p.m."

The trap: your real day may not be a neat line. You may work nights, or split shifts, or have
no fixed times. Tell it anyway in the order it happens for you, starting from when you get up,
whenever that is. The order is yours; it only has to be an order.

## When it breaks

You tell the day in the order things come to mind:

> I go to bed at eleven. I have lunch at one. I get up at six. I come home at seven.

Every sentence is true and the listener has to rebuild your day in their head. The fix: start
from getting up and go forward.

You put then on every sentence:

> Then I get up. Then I have tea. Then I go to work. Then I have lunch.

The joining word stops joining and starts drumming. The fix: one then every two or three
sentences.

You put after that at the end:

> I have tea after that.

The listener hears "after that" and waits for what "that" was. The fix: after that goes at the
front. "After that I have tea."

If, on playback, your eight sentences are not in time order, or then starts more than half of
them, the check is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers tell a day in order without thinking, and they skip the boring middle: "I get
up at six, work till seven, home, dinner, bed." When someone actually wants to know, a doctor,
a new flatmate, a boss planning a shift, they slow down and give the eight, with then and after
that, exactly as you practised.

A native listener follows an ordered day with no effort and remembers the odd thing in it: the
late dinner, the early start. An unordered day they cannot remember at all. Order is what makes
your day worth listening to.
