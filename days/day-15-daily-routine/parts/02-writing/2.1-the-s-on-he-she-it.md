## One-line answer

When you write what one other person or thing does every day, the verb takes an -s: I come at six, but he comes at six.

## The story

The milkman changed last month and the new one comes late. You write a note for the building
noticeboard so everyone knows: "The milkman come at eight now." The secretary reads it and
asks, "Come? Who is coming, you or him?" One letter was missing, and the sentence stopped
saying who did the coming. You write it again, "The milkman comes at eight now," and it goes on
the board.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **every day** — again and again, as a habit.
- **habit** — something you do again and again.
- **noticeboard** — a board on a wall where messages are pinned.
- **secretary** — the person who runs the office of a building.
- **letter** — a written sign, from
  [day 1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.1-capital-and-small-letters.md).

When you write what someone does **every day**, as a habit, English uses the plain
[verb](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md): I come, you come,
we come, they come. But for **one other person or thing**, he, she, it, or one name, the verb
takes an **-s**: he comes, she works, it opens.

That -s is the only difference between the two, and it carries the same job as has against
have on [day 9](../../../day-09-introducing-yourself/parts/02-writing/2.1-have-and-has.md).
It tells the reader, before the sentence ends, that this is about one other person, not about
you and not about several.

Some verbs add **-es** instead, when the plain verb ends in a hissing sound: goes, watches,
finishes. And a verb ending in a consonant plus y changes the y to i and adds -es: study
becomes studies.

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) about you will have a

## The rule

| Subject | Verb | Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I, you, we, they | plain | I come at six. They work nights. |
| he, she, it, one name | + s | He comes at six. She works nights. |

The spelling, for the ones that are not a plain -s:

| Plain verb | Ends in | He, she, it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| come, work, live | most sounds | comes, works, lives |
| go, watch, finish, wash | a hissing or o sound | goes, watches, finishes, washes |
| study, carry | consonant + y | studies, carries |

> The milkman comes at eight.
> My sister studies at college.
> The shop opens at nine and closes at eight.

The rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple):
"Remember that we add s or es for he, she and it. Sometimes we also change y to i", with the
models "My favourite TV show starts at 8 o'clock" and "She finishes work early on Fridays." The
same -s appears in the routine sentences at
[Daily Routine Vocabulary](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/vocabulary/a1-a2/daily-routine-vocabulary-a1-beginner-english-vocabulary-lesson),
opened the same day.

The check for every habit sentence: finger on the subject. Is it one other person or thing?
Then the verb ends in s. Is it I, you, we or they? Then it does not.

The trap: the -s here means the opposite of the -s on a
[noun](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md). On a thing word,
-s means many: cups. On a verb, -s means **one**. My brothers work. My brother works. The two
-s letters never appear together on the same pair.

## When it breaks

You leave the -s off:

> The milkman come at eight.

The reader stops and asks who comes. The fix: comes.

You put the -s on both the thing and the verb:

> My brothers works in Pune.

Many brothers, one verb-for-one. The reader cannot tell how many. The fix: brothers work, or
my brother works.

You add a plain -s where -es belongs:

> He gos to work at nine.

Not a word. The fix: goes.

If a habit sentence on your page has one other person and a verb with no -s, or I or they with
an -s, that sentence is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers put the -s in without seeing it, and drop it only when writing fast. It is the
single most common thing a beginner's page is missing, and readers notice it in the first two
sentences. It costs you nothing to check and it is the fastest way to make a page look
careful.

A friend reading "the milkman come at eight" knows exactly what you mean. A noticeboard in a
building, a note to an office, a message to a customer is read by strangers who have only your
page, and one missing letter makes them read the whole thing twice.
