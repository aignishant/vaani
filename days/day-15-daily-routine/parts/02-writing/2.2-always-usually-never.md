## One-line answer

Always, usually, sometimes and never say how often something happens, and they go right in front of the verb, but after am, is and are.

## The story

Your note about the milkman goes up, and underneath it someone writes: "He comes at eight?
Mine comes at seven." Now the board has two notes that disagree, and nobody knows which is
true. You add one word to yours: "He usually comes at eight." The argument ends. Usually said
what your note had been missing all along: not every single day, but most days.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **how often** — how many times something happens.
- **always** — every time, without missing.
- **usually** — most times, but not every time.
- **sometimes** — now and then.
- **never** — not at any time.
- **disagree** — say two different things.

A habit sentence from [part 2.1](2.1-the-s-on-he-she-it.md) says what someone does. A **how
often** word says how much you can rely on it. There are four you need, in order from most to
least: **always**, **usually**, **sometimes**, **never**.

Where the word sits is a rule, not a choice. It goes **right in front of the verb**: he
usually comes. But with am, is and are, it goes **after** the verb: he is usually late. One
rule, one exception, and the exception is the verb
[be](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md).

**Never** already means no. A sentence with never does not also need not or don't. He never
comes on Sunday, not he doesn't never come.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) about you says what you
do, and almost nothing a person does is every single day. Without these words your page
promises more than your life delivers. On
[day 16](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#8--the-day-map--day-to-ids-closed) you write
your day as a paragraph, and these words are what make it true.

## The rule

The place, as a picture:

```text
Most verbs:   He   usually   comes    at eight.
              subj  how often  verb

With be:      He    is    usually   late.
              subj  verb  how often
```

| How often | Means | Model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| always | every time | He always locks the gate. |
| usually | most times | He usually comes at eight. |
| sometimes | now and then | He sometimes comes at seven. |
| never | not at any time | He never comes on Sunday. |

> The milkman usually comes at eight.
> He is always late on Mondays.
> I never drink tea after dinner.

The position was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Where adverbials go in a sentence](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/where-adverbials-go-sentence):
"adverbials of frequency (how often) usually come in front of the main verb", with "We usually
spent our holidays with our grandparents" and "I have never seen William at work." The words
[always](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/always) /ˈɔːlweɪz/,
[usually](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/usually) /ˈjuːʒuəli/
and [never](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/never) /ˈnevə(r)/
were opened the same day and each carries a sound button. The line "After that I usually watch
TV" appears at
[Daily Routine Vocabulary](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/vocabulary/a1-a2/daily-routine-vocabulary-a1-beginner-english-vocabulary-lesson).

Sometimes is the one that may also move to the front of the sentence for emphasis: "Sometimes
he comes at seven." That is correct and common. Always, usually and never stay in their place.

The trap: putting the how-often word between the verb and what follows it. "He comes usually
at eight" is what many beginners write, and readers stumble on it even though they understand
it. In front of the verb, or after be.

## When it breaks

You put the word after the verb:

> He comes usually at eight.

The reader trips, and some read "usually at eight" as a time, not a frequency. The fix: He
usually comes at eight.

You put it in front of be:

> He always is late.

The exception was missed. The fix: He is always late.

You use never with another no:

> He doesn't never come on Sunday.

Two negatives, which a reader takes as a yes, as on
[day 12](../../../day-12-yes-no-questions/parts/02-writing/2.3-the-double-no.md). The fix: He
never comes on Sunday.

You drop the -s because the how-often word is there:

> He usually come at eight.

The word in front does not take the -s away. The fix: comes.

If a how-often word on your page sits after a main verb, in front of be, or beside another
negative, that sentence is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers reach for usually and sometimes constantly, because almost nothing is always.
On a noticeboard, in a message about when a shop opens or when you are free, "usually" is what
keeps you honest and keeps the reader from feeling cheated when the exception comes.

A neighbour reading "he comes at eight" will hold you to eight. A form asking how often you
take a medicine, a note about when a delivery arrives, a message about when you are home: put
the right word in and nobody is wrong later.
