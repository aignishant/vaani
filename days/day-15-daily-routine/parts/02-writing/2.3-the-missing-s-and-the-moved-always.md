## One-line answer

Copy your noticeboard lines with three breaks on purpose, a dropped -s, a how-often word after the verb, and never beside another negative, read them as a neighbour who will act on them, mark all three, then fix only those.

## The story

The board fills up. Someone pins a notice that says "The water come at six and the tank never
doesn't fill." A neighbour reads it, decides the tank does fill, and goes out for the morning.
He comes back to no water. Another reads "The gate closes usually at ten" as a fixed time and
is locked out at half past. Two notices, two people caught out, and both notices were written
by people who knew exactly what they meant.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong version is deliberate. It rests on what beginners
commonly write, not on a mistake found in your own page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **act on** — do something because of what you read.
- **caught out** — surprised badly because you believed the wrong thing.
- **read back** — read your own page again, slowly, as if it were someone else's, from
  [day 2](../../../day-02-long-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.3-a-form-nobody-can-use.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the -s from [part 2.1](2.1-the-s-on-he-she-it.md) and the how-often words from
[part 2.2](2.2-always-usually-never.md). Now you plant the three breaks beginners really make
on a noticeboard, and you read as the reader who matters here: a neighbour who will change
what they do because of your notice.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is judged by a stranger
who can follow it, and these three breaks are the ones that make a habit sentence say the
wrong thing. If your read-back cannot catch them planted today, it will not catch the one that
locks a neighbour out.

## The rule

Under your correct sentences from parts 2.1 and 2.2:

1. Write four notices for a real board in or near your home: when something comes, opens,
   closes or happens, with the right -s and the right how-often words.
2. Copy them with three deliberate breaks: drop the -s from one verb with one other person or
   thing; move one how-often word to after the main verb; put never beside a don't or doesn't
   in one.
3. Cover the correct notices.
4. Read the broken notices out loud as the neighbour. After each, say what you would actually
   do tomorrow because of it. If you would do the wrong thing, or cannot tell who or how often,
   that is a break.
5. Mark each RED. There should be three.
6. Uncover. Fix only the three, on a new line, and mark it GREEN.

The model, broken on purpose:

> The water come at six. The gate closes usually at ten. The lift doesn't never work on
> Sundays.

Three RED marks: come should be comes; "closes usually" should be "usually closes"; "doesn't
never" is two negatives and should be "never works".

The rules were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple)
and [Where adverbials go in a sentence](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/where-adverbials-go-sentence):
frequency words "usually come in front of the main verb".

## When it breaks

You read the broken notices back and mark them GREEN:

> The water come at six. The gate closes usually at ten. — GREEN

You wrote them, so you know the water comes and the gate is not exact. The neighbour does not.
The check itself failed. Read again and say out loud what you would do tomorrow morning at
half past nine because of the gate notice.

Another break: you fix the double negative by removing never.

> The lift doesn't work on Sundays.

Correct English, and it changed the meaning: your notice said the lift never works on Sundays,
as a rule. Take out the doesn't, not the never. "The lift never works on Sundays."

If any planted break was marked GREEN, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody drops an -s on purpose. But a noticeboard is written fast, standing up, and these three
are exactly what comes out. Fluent writers make the same slips in a first draft and catch them
by reading as the person who will act. That reading takes one pass and saves a neighbour a
morning.

A friend reading your notice asks you. A neighbour, a delivery person, a tenant you have never
met does not ask; they act. Keep the broken notices on your practice page as proof that your
read-back can go RED.
