# Joined-up speech

`PR-08` · Day 15

## The one-line answer

Inside a phrase, fluent English has no gaps between words — the words run into each other the way
joined-up handwriting runs, and speaking in separate words is the single loudest thing a listener
hears before they hear anything you said.

## The model

You were taught English one word at a time, in blocks, like block capitals. Clean, separate,
each letter standing on its own. That is a fine way to learn to write, and nobody writes that way.

Fluent speech is cursive. The pen goes down at the start of a phrase and does not come up until
the end of it. The letters change shape where they join, and that is not sloppiness — the joins
are how the reader knows the letters belong to the same word.

Three joins carry almost all of it.

**A consonant runs onto the front of the next word.** This is the big one. When a word ends in a
consonant and the next begins with a vowel, the consonant jumps ship. *A bit of a* is not
*a — bit — of — a*, it is *a bi-to-fa*. *Hold on* is *hol-don*. *Come on in* is *co-mo-nin*.
*Not at all* is *no-ta-tall*.

> a bi-to-fa mess
> hol-don a sec
> co-mo-nin

Say those three the joined way, then the separated way. The joined version is not faster. It is
the same speed with the pen down.

**Two of the same consonant become one, held.** When your word ends in the sound the next word
starts with, you do not say it twice. You say it once and hold it slightly. *As far as I know*
has a single held *s* in the middle of *as-far*. *Big garden* has one *g*. Saying both is the
audible tell — it puts a tiny click between the words where a listener expects a slide.

**Vowel into vowel grows a tiny bridge.** *Go on* comes out with a small *w* in it — *gow-on*.
*So I said* is *sow-I said*. *I always* gets a small *y* — *I-yalways*. You do not have to add
these on purpose. You have to stop preventing them, which is what happens when you stop the air
between words.

And the useful half of this: **you still get to lift the pen.** At the end of a phrase, before a
word you want to hit, wherever you would draw breath. Lifting the pen deliberately is emphasis.
Lifting it between every word is not care — it is the absence of a decision, and it flattens the
emphasis you built on Day 13, because when everything is separated nothing can stand out.

**Today's phrases** — all of these are chosen because they are almost impossible to say
separately without hearing it. Check `docs/CHUNKS.md` first.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| a bit of a … | Softening whatever noun comes next |
| hold on a sec | Buying yourself a moment — the fast, casual cousin of the Day 2 line |
| as far as I know | Reporting something you are not willing to guarantee |
| not at all | Brushing off thanks, or denying flatly and warmly |
| an awful lot of … | Quantity, with feeling — more than "a lot" |
| come on in | Letting someone through a door |
| I'll have a look at it | Taking something on without promising an outcome |
| sooner or later | Conceding that a thing is coming whether you like it or not |

## Say it now

Say each three times. First run slowly with the pen down — slow does not mean separated. Second
and third at your ordinary speed.

1. *It's a bit of a {{…}}, that one.*
2. *Hold on a sec — I'll have a look at it.*
3. *"Sorry about the state of it." — "Not at all. Come on in."*
4. *As far as I know there's an awful lot of {{…}} in it, so sooner or later {{…}}.*

Then say all four as one continuous stretch, twice, lifting the pen in exactly one place per line
— wherever you want the emphasis — and nowhere else.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d15-free.m4a`.**

> **Describe something in your home that annoys you** — a door, a drawer, a plug socket, the
> shelf that holds too much. Why it annoys you, what you have tried, why it is still there.
> Then say the same description again, and this time make it one long sentence with no full
> stops, joined the whole way.

The second run is the rep. A single long sentence forces the joins, because there is nowhere to
put a stop.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** on the second run they raise a hand every time they hear a gap where you
  did not mean one. You keep going. You do not stop for the hand.

## The deliberate failure

Take line 1 from **Say it now** and say it in block capitals: every word separate, a clean gap
between each, all of them the same weight. *It's. A. Bit. Of. A. Nuisance.* Then do it to a whole
sentence of your own.

**What breaking sounds like:** the sentence gets longer without getting clearer. That is the
thing to catch, because it is the opposite of what it feels like while you do it — separating
words feels like being careful, and on the tape it sounds like reading a list you have never seen
before. You will also hear the emphasis vanish. There is no loud word, because every word is
loud.

Now say it joined. The sentence shortens, and one word steps forward on its own. You did not put
it there. The joins did.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are on a call and the line is poor, or you are at a counter and it is noisy, and the person
asks you to repeat. So you repeat it slower — and separated. Every word its own island. It gets
worse, and now you are three attempts in and starting to give up on the sentence.

Separating is the wrong lever. The right one is smaller pieces, still joined: keep the pen down
inside each phrase and put the space **between** the phrases instead of between the words.

**The repair:**

> Hold on a sec — let me say that in smaller bits.

Then deliver it in two or three joined phrases with a real pause between them. Same words, same
speed, and this time the pauses are where you put them rather than everywhere.
