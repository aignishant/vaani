## One-line answer

English says how much you like something on a ladder from love down to can't stand, and choosing the right rung is how you say no to food without saying no to the person.

## The story

You are eating at a friend's house. His mother brings a dish you have never had and watches
you taste it. It is not for you. You say, "I don't like it," and the table goes quiet. She
takes the plate away. You meant "this one isn't for me". You said the flattest, hardest thing
on the ladder, because it was the only rung you had.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **ladder** — steps going up and down; here, from loving to hating.
- **rung** — one step of a ladder.
- **dish** — one kind of cooked food.
- **all right** — not good, not bad; acceptable.
- **can't stand** — hate; cannot bear at all.

Liking is not yes or no. English has a **ladder** of five rungs, and where you stand on it is
as important as the thing you are talking about. Near the top, love, is strong and warm. In the
middle, "it's all right", is safe and polite. At the bottom, can't stand, is very strong and,
about someone's cooking, rude.

The important rung is the one just below the middle: **I'm not keen on it** or **it's not
really for me**. These say no without hitting anyone. Beginners rarely have them, so they jump
from "I like it" straight to "I don't like it", and the table goes quiet.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 self-introduction](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) ends with
what you like, and a stranger will ask about food, films, cricket, music, within a minute of
meeting you. The whole ladder, not just the top and the bottom, is what makes those answers
sound like a person and not a form.

## The rule

The ladder, top to bottom:

| Rung | What you say | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | I love it. | you really do |
| 2 | I like it. / I like it a lot. | you do |
| 3 | It's all right. / It's okay. | you don't mind it |
| 4 | I'm not keen on it. / It's not really for me. | you would rather not |
| 5 | I don't like it. | you mean it, and the person is not the cook |
| 6 | I can't stand it. | strong; between friends, about things, not people's food |

> I love old songs.
> Coffee's all right, but I prefer tea.
> I'm not keen on very spicy food.

Say each rung with the recorder on. The tune matters as much as the words: rung 1 goes up and
stays warm; rung 3 is flat and easy; rung 4 goes gently down and often ends with a small
smile in the voice.

The verbs were checked on 2026-09-22.
[love](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/love_1) /lʌv/,
[like](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/like_1),
[hate](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/hate_1) /heɪt/ and
[stand](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/stand_1) /stænd/ each
carry a sound button; stand is the verb inside can't stand. What follows these verbs was
checked at [Verbs followed by '-ing' or infinitive](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/verbs-followed-ing-or-infinitive):
"Like and love can be followed by the -ing form and the to + infinitive form. They are both
correct", and at
['-ing' forms](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/ing-forms),
which lists like and hate among verbs followed by an -ing object, with "We enjoy learning
English."

So, when what you like is an **action**, use the -ing form: I like cooking. I love walking in
the evening. When it is a **thing**, use the thing: I like tea.

The trap: "I don't like" is not the polite middle. In English it is fairly direct, and about
food someone has cooked for you it lands hard. Rung 4 is the one to have ready.

## When it breaks

You use rung 5 where rung 4 belongs:

> I don't like it.

at the table, with the cook watching. The fix: "It's not really for me, but thank you." Or
rung 3 with something true added: "It's all right. I'm just not used to it."

You use rung 6 about a person's cooking:

> I can't stand it.

Very strong. Among friends about traffic or rain, fine; here, not. The fix: rung 4.

You say only rung 2, about everything:

> I like it. I like it. I like it.

Safe, and after three answers the listener knows nothing about you. The fix: use the ladder.
Some things are rung 1, some are rung 3.

If, on playback, your answers use only rungs 2 and 5, the check is RED.

## In real life

Fluent speakers live on rungs 2, 3 and 4 and save 1 and 6 for when they mean them. They also
soften with a reason: "I'm not keen on it, I've never had it before." The reason is what turns
a refusal into a fact about you rather than a judgement on the food.

A native listener hears rung 5 about their cooking as a small rejection and will not say so.
They hear rung 4 as honesty and often offer you something else. The two rungs are one sentence
apart and the difference at a table is large.
