## One-line answer

Record three answers with no reason and one answer that jumps to the bottom rung, on purpose, play them back as the person who asked, notice what they could say next, then record the open versions beside them.

## The story

Walking home from your friend's house, you replay the evening in your head. "I don't like it"
with the plate being taken away. "I like fish." and the pause. And then the one that worked:
fish, because of Sundays, and twenty minutes of talk about coconut. Three answers, one door
opened. You decide to record all three tomorrow and listen to what each one leaves the other
person to say.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong versions are deliberate. They rest on what beginners
commonly do, not on a mistake found in your own recordings.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **bare** — with nothing added.
- **go quiet** — stop talking.
- **jump** — go straight from one place to a far one, missing the middle.
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the ladder from [part 1.1](1.1-the-ladder-of-liking.md) and the three-piece answer
from [part 1.2](1.2-say-what-you-like.md). Now you break them the two ways that close a table:
the reason goes, and the rung jumps to the bottom. You listen as the person who asked, and
after each answer you say out loud what you could ask next. That is the test. A good answer
leaves them something. A closed one leaves them nothing.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the [day 20 check](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases), the stranger asks
what you like and your answer is the last thing on the recording. A bare answer ends it; an
open one makes them want to keep listening. And in a real room, the bottom-rung jump costs you
more than a recording: it costs the cook's face.

## The rule

Two rows. Recorder on. Wrong first, then right, with a gap.

**Row 1 — no reason.** Three bare answers in a row:

> I like fish. I like cricket. I like music.
> I like fish, because my mother cooks it on Sundays. I like cricket, because I played at
> school. I like old songs, because my father sang them.

**Row 2 — the jump to the bottom.** At a table, with the cook listening:

> I don't like it.
> It's not really for me, but thank you.

Play row 1. Be the person who asked. After each bare answer, say out loud what you could ask
next. For "I like fish" the honest answer is "nothing, really" or "Oh, nice." Then do the same
after each open answer, and notice you have a question every time.

Play row 2. Be the cook. After the wrong version, say what you would do with the plate.

Then the repair, in one breath, as it happens when you hear the room go quiet:

> I don't like — sorry, I mean it's not really for me. It's very new for me.

The verbs and what follows them were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Verbs followed by '-ing' or infinitive](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/verbs-followed-ing-or-infinitive)
and at [love](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/love_1),
[like](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/definition/english/like_1) and

## When it breaks

The break this part is about: you find a question to ask after the bare answer.

> I like fish. → "What kind of fish?"

You can ask that because you are working at it. A stranger at a table, holding a plate, does
not work at it; they say "Oh, nice" and turn to someone else. Play it again and allow yourself
only the first thing that actually comes to mind.

A second break: the repair explains too much.

> I don't like — sorry, in my home we never eat this and my mother says it is not good for the
> stomach and…

The repair became a speech about why the food is wrong. The fix: one short rung-4 sentence and
a thank you. "It's not really for me, but thank you."

If you could ask a real question after every bare answer, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody answers bare on purpose. Beginners do it because a short sentence is safe and a reason
is more English to get wrong. The cost is invisible: the conversation just does not happen, and
nobody tells you why. The bottom-rung jump is the same: people do not say "that was rude";
they take the plate away.

A native listener will carry a bare answer once, twice at most, out of politeness. After that
they ask someone else. One because per answer is all it takes to stop that, and fluent speakers
add it without knowing.
