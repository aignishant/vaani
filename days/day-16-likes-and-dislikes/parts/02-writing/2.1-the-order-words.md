## One-line answer

First, then, after that and finally are the four words that tell a reader where they are in a sequence, and they go at the front of the sentence with a comma after the long ones.

## The story

Your cousin has moved abroad and asks, in a message, what your days are like now. You write
eight sentences about your day and send them. She writes back: "Sounds busy! But do you go to
work before or after you take your sister to school?" Every sentence was true and in the right
order, and she still could not tell, because nothing in the writing said "this comes next".
The order was in your head, not on the page.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **sequence** — things in the order they happen.
- **first** — before everything else.
- **finally** — last of all.
- **comma** — the mark , used for a short pause inside a sentence.
- **abroad** — in another country.

When you speak, your voice and your pauses show the order. On paper there is no voice, so
English uses **order words** to mark the steps of a **sequence**: **first**, **then**, **after
that**, **finally**. They are signposts. The reader sees one and knows where they are without
holding the whole day in their head.

They go at the **front** of the sentence. The longer ones, first, after that and finally, take
a **comma** after them. Then usually does not need one, though it is not wrong to use one.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is ten sentences a
stranger can follow, and following is exactly what order words are for. This is also the day
your page stops being a list of facts and starts being writing someone reads for pleasure.

## The rule

| Word | Where in the sequence | Comma? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First, | the first step | yes |
| Then | any middle step | no |
| After that, | any middle step | yes |
| Finally, | the last step | yes |

> First, I get up and make tea.
> Then I take my sister to school.
> After that, I go to work.
> Finally, I come home and have dinner with my family.

Four rules for using them well:

1. **Not on every sentence.** Four order words in eight sentences is plenty. More and the page
   sounds like instructions for a machine.
2. **First only once**, at the start. Finally only once, at the end.
3. **Then and after that are the same job**; change between them so the page does not repeat.
4. **The comma is part of the word.** First, with a comma. Not First with nothing.

The comma after an opening word and the ordered shape were seen on 2026-09-22 in the model
routine at
[Daily Routine Vocabulary](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/vocabulary/a1-a2/daily-routine-vocabulary-a1-beginner-english-vocabulary-lesson),
which runs "On weekdays, I get up early in the morning", "I get dressed at half seven and then
I have breakfast", "After that I usually watch TV". The habit of giving a piece of writing
three parts, a start, a middle and an end, was seen at
[A thank-you email](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/thank-you-email),
opened the same day.

The trap: **finally** means last of all, not "at last, after a long wait". If you write
"Finally, I go to work" in the middle of your day, the reader thinks the day is ending and then
gets three more sentences.

## When it breaks

You write the day with no order words:

> I get up at six. I take my sister to school. I go to work. I come home.

Every sentence is true and the reader has to guess whether the order on the page is the order
in life. The fix: four signposts.

You put an order word on every sentence:

> First, I get up. Then I make tea. After that, I wake my sister. Then I take her to school.
> After that, I go to work. Then I have lunch.

The signposts are now louder than the day. The fix: keep four, drop the rest.

You leave the comma out:

> First I get up and make tea.

A reader understands it, and the page looks less careful than your sentences are. The fix:
First, with the comma.

If your page has no order words, or one on every sentence, or finally anywhere but at the end,
it is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers use these four in anything with steps: a recipe, instructions for a colleague,
a description of a journey. In a chat message they drop them and rely on the order of the
lines. In anything a stranger reads once, they put them back, because a signpost costs one
word and saves the reader a re-read.

A cousin who knows you fills in the gaps and asks. A reader who does not know you, an office, a
form, a teacher, does not ask. They read once, in the order the words give them, and the words
are all they have.
