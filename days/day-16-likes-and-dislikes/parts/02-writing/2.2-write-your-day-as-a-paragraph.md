## One-line answer

Write your day as one paragraph of about eight sentences, in time order, with four order words and one sentence that says how you feel about it, and your cousin abroad can see your day.

## The story

You write to your cousin again. This time it is one block: what you do first, then, after
that, finally, and at the end one line that is not a fact at all — "It's a long day, but I
like the early morning best." She writes back about the early morning, not about your job. The
facts told her what you do. The last line told her what it is like to be you.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **block** — several sentences written together, from
  [day 7](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/02-writing/2.2-five-sentences-on-paper.md).
- **feel** — what something is like for you inside.
- **best** — more than all the others.
- **long day** — a day with a lot of work in it.

A [paragraph](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-paragraph-is.md),
from day 13, is several sentences about one topic in one block. Today the topic is your day,
and the order is given to you by the clock. You already have the order words from
[part 2.1](2.1-the-order-words.md).

One thing is new. A day written as pure facts is a timetable. The last sentence of the
paragraph says **how you feel** about the day: which part you like best, what is hard, what
you look forward to. That single sentence is what makes a reader answer.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 page](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is ten sentences about
you in one block. Eight of them can be your day, written exactly like this, and the last two
the same feeling sentence you practise today. This part is the day 20 page in miniature.

## The rule

1. Write eight sentences about your real day, in time order, from getting up to going to bed.
2. Put an order word on four of them: First, at the top, Then and After that in the middle,
   Finally, near the end.
3. Check every verb: your own actions take the plain form, I get, I go; anyone else in your
   day takes the
   [-s](../../../day-15-daily-routine/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-s-on-he-she-it.md), my sister
   leaves, the bus comes.
4. Add [how-often words](../../../day-15-daily-routine/parts/02-writing/2.2-always-usually-never.md)
   where the truth needs them: usually, always, never.
5. End with one sentence about how you feel.
6. Write the whole thing as one block, one space after each full stop, no new lines inside it.

The model, for a day that is not yours:

> First, I get up at six and make tea for everyone. My sister leaves for school at seven, and I
> usually walk with her to the corner. Then I take the bus to work. I have lunch at one, always
> at my desk. After that the afternoon is quiet, so I finish my work by six. Finally, I come
> home and we have dinner together at eight. I watch a little TV and go to bed at eleven. It's
> a long day, but I like the early morning best, when the house is quiet.

Eight sentences plus the feeling line, one block, four order words.

The ordered routine, the present simple for habits and the "After that I usually watch TV"
shape were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Daily Routine Vocabulary](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/vocabulary/a1-a2/daily-routine-vocabulary-a1-beginner-english-vocabulary-lesson),
and the -s and how-often rules at
[Present simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple)
and [Where adverbials go in a sentence](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/where-adverbials-go-sentence),
opened the same day.

The trap: the feeling sentence turning into an opinion with nothing in it, "My day is good."
Say which part, and why in three words. "I like the early morning best, when the house is
quiet."

## When it breaks

You write the day as a list of times:

> 6.00 get up. 7.00 sister school. 9.00 work.

That is a timetable, not a paragraph. There are no sentences and no verbs for the reader to
follow. The fix: full sentences, in a block.

You stop at the last fact:

> …I watch a little TV and go to bed at eleven.

Complete, and closed, the way a bare answer is closed. The reader has your day and nothing to
reply to. The fix: one feeling sentence.

You lose the -s on someone else's verb:

> My sister leave for school at seven.

The one sentence in your day that is about someone else, and it is the one that slips. The
fix: leaves.

If your page is not one block, has fewer than four order words, or has no feeling sentence,
it is RED.

## In real life

Fluent writers write their day this way in a message to family abroad, in an application, in a
form that asks about your circumstances. The facts get them the information; the feeling line
gets them a reply. In a formal letter they drop the feeling line and keep the order words,
because the reader there wants only the sequence.

A cousin reading a timetable writes back "ok". A cousin reading a paragraph with one honest
line writes back about the early morning. The page is the same length; only the last sentence
changed.
