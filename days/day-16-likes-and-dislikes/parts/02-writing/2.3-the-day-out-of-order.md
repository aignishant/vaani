## One-line answer

Copy your paragraph with three breaks on purpose, finally in the middle, two sentences swapped out of time order, and the feeling sentence cut, read it as your cousin abroad, mark all three, then put them back.

## The story

Your cousin writes once more, about the version you sent before you learned any of this. She
had kept it. "I read this one three times," she says. "You said finally and then kept going, so
I thought I had missed something. And you never said whether you liked any of it." She had not
been careless. She had read exactly what was on the page, from six thousand kilometres away,
with nobody to ask.

This is a practice scene. Today's wrong version is deliberate. It rests on what beginners
commonly write, not on a mistake found in your own page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **swap** — put one where the other was.
- **cut** — take out.
- **read back** — read your own page again, slowly, as if it were someone else's, from
  [day 2](../../../day-02-long-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.3-a-form-nobody-can-use.md).
- **RED** and **GREEN** — RED means the check found a problem. GREEN means it passed.

You have the order words from [part 2.1](2.1-the-order-words.md) and the paragraph from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-your-day-as-a-paragraph.md). Now you plant the three breaks that undo a
day on paper: a signpost pointing the wrong way, two steps in the wrong order, and no feeling
sentence. You read as your cousin abroad: someone who wants to know about you, cannot ask, and
has only the page.

## The rule

Under your correct paragraph from part 2.2:

1. Copy it with three deliberate breaks: move Finally, to the middle; swap two sentences so
   they are out of time order; cut the feeling sentence at the end.
2. Cover the correct paragraph.
3. Read the broken one out loud as your cousin abroad. Ask three things as you go: "Is the day
   finished here?" at every order word; "Could this really happen in this order?" at every
   sentence; and at the end, "Do I know anything about how they feel?"
4. Mark each RED. There should be three.
5. Uncover. Put back only the three, on a new line, and mark it GREEN.

The model, broken on purpose:

> First, I get up at six and make tea. Finally, I take the bus to work. I have lunch at one. I
> come home and we have dinner at eight. Then I walk my sister to the corner. I watch TV and go
> to bed at eleven.

Three RED marks: Finally is in the middle, so the reader thinks the day is ending; walking the
sister to the corner comes after dinner, which cannot be right for a school morning; and the
paragraph ends on a fact with nothing about how the day feels.

The ordered-day shape was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Daily Routine Vocabulary](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/vocabulary/a1-a2/daily-routine-vocabulary-a1-beginner-english-vocabulary-lesson).

## When it breaks

You read the broken paragraph back and mark it GREEN:

> First, I get up… Finally, I take the bus… Then I walk my sister… — GREEN

You know your day, so your eye put the sister back in the morning and read past Finally. The
check itself failed. Read again as someone six thousand kilometres away and answer the three
questions out loud. At "Finally, I take the bus", the honest answer is "yes, the day seems
finished", and then it is not.

Another break: you put the feeling sentence back somewhere else.

> First, I get up at six. I like the early morning best. Then I take the bus…

A feeling sentence in the middle stops the sequence. It goes at the end, after the last fact,
where it closes the paragraph.

If any planted break was marked GREEN, the check is RED.

## In real life

Nobody moves finally on purpose. But a paragraph written over two sittings, or edited by
adding a sentence in the middle, breaks exactly this way, and the writer never sees it because
they know the day. Fluent writers read the whole thing once from the top before sending,
asking the same "is it finished here?" question at every signpost.

A cousin reads it three times and still asks. A form, an application or an office reads it
once and decides. Keep the broken paragraph on your practice page as proof that your read-back
can go RED, and as the version you did not send.
