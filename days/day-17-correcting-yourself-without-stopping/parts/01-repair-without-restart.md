# Repair the sentence, don't restart the walk

`CF-05` · `FL-05` · Day 17

## The one-line answer

A correction made inside the sentence you are already speaking costs you nothing, and the same
correction made by going back to the beginning costs you the sentence, the listener's attention,
and often the turn.

## The model

You are walking and your foot catches. What you do next decides everything. You can stumble,
put the foot down, and walk on — and nobody watching will remember it happened. Or you can stop,
turn round, go back to where the street started, and set off again properly. The second one is
the one people notice.

That is the whole of today. You already know how to notice you were wrong. What you do not yet
have is the move that fixes the wrong word **without breaking stride**.

Watch what most learners do. Wrong word comes out. Silence. *Sorry — let me start again.* And
then the sentence begins from the top, the same six words as before, with the listener now
waiting instead of listening. Do that three times in a conversation and you have said very little
and worked very hard.

You are not short of repair phrases. You have plenty already:

- Day 1 gave you *Let me start again* — and that one is the full restart. It is real, it is
  useful, and today is about how rarely you actually need it.
- Day 2 gave you *That's not quite what I meant* — a repair of the **whole idea**, after the
  fact.
- Day 6 gave you *Let me rephrase that*, *What I should have said was …* and *That came out
  wrong* — all of them arriving **after** the sentence has landed.
- Day 9 gave you *Bear with me* and *Give me a second, I'll get there* — those hold the turn while
  a word is missing.

Look at what they have in common: every one of them is a stop. Something ends, then something
starts. Today's moves are the other kind. They live **inside** the sentence, between two words,
and the sentence keeps going through them.

**The swap.** One word was wrong and you want a different one. Do not repeat the sentence — just
put the better word in, with a hinge:

> I go there most weeks — or rather, I used to.
> It's a small place, well, more like a room with a counter.

**The reset of the idea, not the words.** The word was right and the idea was wrong. *I mean* is
the hinge, and the dash after it is a real thing your voice does — a tiny lift, no silence:

> He's my manager — I mean, he was, until the team split.

**The landing.** A repaired sentence can arrive slightly bent. You close it, rather than
apologising for it:

> …so it's the second one, if that makes sense.

Three things make these work, and none of them is the phrase itself.

**Your voice must not fall before the hinge.** If the pitch drops on the word before *or rather*,
your listener hears an ending and takes the turn. Keep it level and walk into the correction.

**No silence on either side.** A gap before the hinge turns it into a confession. The hinge is
part of the sentence, not commentary on it.

**Correct once.** Fixing your fix — *or rather, well, more like, I mean* — is worse than the
original wrong word. One hinge, then walk on.

**Today's phrases** — mid-sentence moves only. The repairs from Days 1, 2, 6 and 9 are already
in `docs/CHUNKS.md`; do not add them again.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| …, or rather, … | Swapping one wrong word for the right one, mid-sentence |
| I mean — | The words were fine, the idea was wrong |
| well, more like … | The first word was close, but too strong or too weak |
| …, or should I say … | A light swap, when the correction is half a joke |
| what I mean is … | Restating the point without restarting the sentence |
| not — well, not exactly. | Pulling back from a word you overshot on |
| …, if that makes sense. | Landing a sentence that arrived bent, and checking it landed |

## Say it now

Say each one three times, filling your own content into the slots. The rule for every rep: your
voice does not stop and does not fall until the very end of the line.

1. *I usually go to {{…}} — or rather, I used to. Now it's {{…}}.*
2. *It's quite {{…}}. Well, more like {{…}}.*
3. *He's my {{…}} — I mean, he was, before {{…}}.*
4. *We're doing {{…}} on {{…}}, if that makes sense.*

Now join all four into one continuous stretch, twice, with no silence anywhere in it. The hinges
are the joins, and the joins are the whole exercise.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d17-free.m4a`.**

> **Describe a room in your home from memory — out loud, in detail, without looking at it.**
> Where the furniture is, what is on the walls, what is on the surfaces, what is broken.
> Then, still recording, **do the same room again from the opposite corner.**

This prompt is chosen because you *will* get things wrong. You will name the wrong wall, say
*left* when you meant *right*, forget a chair and remember it late. That is the material. Every
one of those errors gets corrected inside the sentence it happened in. Not one restart.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** they hold up a hand every time you go back to the start of a sentence.
  You do not stop when they do it — you carry on and count the hands afterwards.

## The deliberate failure

Describe the same room again, four or five sentences of it, with one rule: **every time anything
comes out wrong, stop dead and start that sentence again from its first word.** Use *Let me start
again* each time, properly, the way Day 1 taught it.

**What breaking sounds like:** you will hear the front of your sentences over and over — the same
three or four opening words, three times each — and almost no content behind them. The silences
before each restart all sound identical, and each one has a small falling tone in front of it
that sounds like you have finished speaking. Count how much of the room you actually described.

Now do it once more with the hinges restored. The errors are still there — you make exactly the
same mistakes — but the room gets described. That is the finding: the restarts were never buying
accuracy, they were only spending sentences.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are on a call. You say *"the delivery's been cancelled"* and hear it come out wrong — it was
delayed, not cancelled. You stop, apologise, and go back to the top of the sentence. In that
silence the other person starts talking, because your falling tone told them you were done. Now
the wrong version is the one on the table, and you have to interrupt to fix it.

**The repair:**

> The delivery's been cancelled — or rather, delayed. It's coming, just not today.

No stop, no apology, no lost turn. The wrong word never got a chance to stand on its own.
