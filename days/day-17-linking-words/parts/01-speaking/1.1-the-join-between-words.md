## One-line answer

When a word ends in a consonant sound and the next word starts with a vowel sound, English slides the consonant over and starts the next word with it, so *an apple* comes out as *a napple*.

## The story

You are at a small grocery near your house. You want three things. You say them the way you
learned them, one clean word at a time, with a small stop after each:

> an … apple … an … egg … an … orange

The man behind the counter leans forward and says, "Sorry?" You say it again, louder, with the
same stops. He still does not move. A woman behind you says, "an apple, an egg, an orange" —
faster, softer, and joined — and he reaches for the basket.

Nothing was wrong with your words. The stops were wrong. You handed him six pieces and he was
listening for three.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **consonant** — a sound made by stopping or squeezing the air: *p*, *t*, *k*, *n*, *s*.
- **vowel sound** — the open sound in the middle of a word, like the *a* in *cat*. Defined on
  [day 1, part 1.1](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.1-four-vowel-sounds.md).
- **link** — to join the end of one word to the start of the next with no stop.
- **join** — the place where two words meet.
- **stream** — water running without a break; here, speech running without a break.

On paper, English has a space between every word. In the mouth, it does not. Speech is a
**stream**, and words inside a phrase run into each other.

The most common **join** is this one: the first word ends in a **consonant** sound, the next word
starts with a **vowel sound**. When that happens, the consonant does not stay at the end of the
first word. It moves. It becomes the first sound of the next word.

So *an apple* is said *a napple*. The *n* leaves *an* and joins *apple*. Nobody writes it that
way, and everybody says it that way.

This is about **sounds**, not letters. *An hour* links, because *hour* starts with a vowel sound
even though it starts with the letter *h*. That is the trap, and it is at the end of this part.

## Why Vaani needs it

The [day 20 gate recording](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) is a
self-introduction of at least ten sentences that a stranger can follow. Ten sentences said one
chopped word at a time is not ten sentences to a listener; it is ninety small noises they have
to reassemble. The joins are what turn your words into sentences in somebody else's ear.

## The rule

The rule is one sentence: **consonant sound at the end + vowel sound at the start = move the
consonant over.**

Here is the join drawn. The line is the slide:

```text
   an    apple            an  apple
   ‾‾    ‾‾‾‾‾     →       a  napple
     n ──┘                     ↑
                        the n moved here
```

Three model lines. Say each one twice: first as it is written, then as the second column sounds.

> an egg  →  a negg
> an orange  →  a norange
> Get on.  →  Geton.

The joining is not a shortcut and it is not lazy. It is how the sentence is built. The pages
checked on 2026-09-22 say it plainly:
[Connected speech](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/articles/connected-speech)
says "speech is a continuous stream of sounds" and that "certain words are lost, and certain
phonemes linked together", and
[Connected speech – part 2](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/articles/connected-speech-part-2)
gives the rule and the example: "Word boundaries involving a consonant and a vowel are also
linked, as we tend to drag final consonants to initial vowels or vice versa" — "Get on.
( geton )".

**Where the tongue is.** For *an apple*, put your tongue on the ridge behind your top teeth for
the *n* and **do not take it down** before the *a*. Take it down *into* the *a*. If your tongue
comes down and the air stops, you have made the gap.

Listen to each target word before you say it. These entries were opened on 2026-09-22 and each
carries a sound file:
[apple](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/apple) /ˈapəl/,
[orange](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/orange) /ˈɒɹɪndʒ/,
[egg](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/egg) /ɛɡ/.
Play the word, then say the whole phrase with the recorder on, then play both back.

**The trap.** The join follows the **sound**, not the letter.

| Written | Starts with the letter | Starts with the sound | Links? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| an apple | a | vowel | yes |
| an hour | h | vowel | yes |
| a house | h | consonant | no |
| an umbrella | u | vowel | yes |
| a university | u | consonant (a *y* sound) | no |

## When it breaks

A beginner says every word whole, with a small stop after each:

> an . apple . an . egg

What the listener gets is four words where you meant two things, and no phrase shape at all.
They usually ask "Sorry?" once and then guess. The smallest fix: say *an* and *apple* on one
breath, and let the *n* fall into the *a*.

The second common break is the opposite, and it happens once a learner has heard about linking.
They link everything, including where there is no vowel:

> an-apple-and-a-big-bag

said as one long word. The fix: link only at a join — consonant to vowel. *big bag* is consonant
to consonant. It does not link; it just does not stop.

The third break is the letter trap:

> a hour

The listener hears a missing sound where the *n* should be, and often hears *our* instead of
*hour*. The fix: *hour* starts with a vowel **sound**, so it is *an hour*, and it links: *a nour*.

If, on playback, you can hear a clean silence between *an* and *apple*, the check is RED.

## In real life

A fluent speaker does not think about any of this. They link because stopping is more work than
not stopping. What they notice in your speech is not the missing rule — it is that you sound
**careful**, and careful is tiring to listen to over ten sentences.

Under pressure the linking gets stronger, not weaker. A fast speaker at a counter will say *Can I
have an apple* as something close to *cunaya vanapple*. This is the main reason fast English feels
impossible to understand: you are listening for the gaps, and there are none. Learning to make the
joins is also learning to hear them.

A native listener forgives almost every one of your sounds. They do not forgive the chopping,
because it is the thing that makes them work. They will not tell you. They will just say "Sorry?"
more often than they do with other people.
