## One-line answer

Take six sentences you really say, mark every place where a consonant sound meets a vowel sound, and say them with the recorder on until none of those places has a stop in it.

## The story

You are back at the same counter a week later. This time you are not buying fruit. The man asks
you something and you answer:

> It's a bit of a long walk.

and it comes out fine, because you have said that sentence a hundred times and your mouth already
joined it. Then he asks where you live, and you say:

> I live in a flat above a shop.

and this one comes out in pieces, because you have never said it before. Same mouth, same rule,
different sentence. The joins are not a thing you learn once. They are a thing you find, sentence
by sentence, in the sentences that are yours.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **mark** — to put a small sign on paper to remind you of something.
- **phrase** — a small group of words said together on one breath.
- **breath group** — the words you say between two breaths.
- **playback** — listening to your own recording afterwards.

[Part 1.1](1.1-the-join-between-words.md) gave you the rule and six phrases to practise it on.
Six phrases is not speech. Speech is your own sentences, about your own street and your own work,
and those are the ones your mouth has never joined.

So the work of this part is small and specific. You write down sentences **you actually say**.
You find the joins in them with a pencil. Then you say them on one breath, recorder on, and you
check the joins on **playback** — not while you are talking, because while you are talking you
cannot hear yourself properly.

A **breath group** is the useful unit here. You do not link across a breath. You link inside one.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your day 20 gate recording is made of your sentences, not ours. If you practise linking only on
*an apple*, you will link *an apple* and chop everything else, and the recording a stranger
listens to will be the chopped one. This part is where the rule stops being about apples.

## The rule

Three steps, in this order. Do not skip step one — the pencil is what makes the join visible.

**Step one: write six sentences you really say.** Real ones. Where you live, what you do, what
you ask for, what you say when someone calls you. Not sentences from this page.

**Step two: mark the joins.** Read each sentence slowly. At every word boundary, ask two
questions: *does this word end in a consonant sound?* and *does the next word start with a vowel
sound?* If both answers are yes, draw a small arc under the join.

```text
   I  live  in  a  flat  above  a  shop.
          ‾‾⌣‾‾ ‾‾⌣‾‾    ‾‾‾‾‾⌣‾‾ ‾‾⌣‾‾
           v→i   n→a          t→a   ve→a
```

**Step three: say it on one breath, recorder on.** Each marked join gets no stop. Say the
sentence three times: slow and joined, normal and joined, fast and joined. Slow does not mean
chopped — slow with the joins in is the one that trains the mouth.

> I live in a flat above a shop.
> I work in an office near the station.
> Can I have a cup of tea?

Note what is *not* marked. *flat above* links, *t* to *a*. *big bag* does not — consonant to
consonant. *my apple* does not — vowel to vowel, and that one gets a tiny *y* sound between,
which is a different rule and belongs to a later day.

**Checking on playback.** Put your finger on the paper at each arc as you listen. If the arc is a
place where you hear a stop, circle it. Those circles are your list for the next take.

The words in your own sentences can be heard first: the entries at
[apple](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/apple),
[orange](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/orange) and
[egg](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/egg), opened on 2026-09-22, each carry a sound file, and any
other word you need has an entry in the same place. Listen, then say it with the recorder on,
then play both back.

## When it breaks

The first break is choosing easy sentences. A beginner writes six short sentences with no joins
in them at all:

> I am happy. I like tea. My name is Ravi.

and then reports that the linking was easy. It was — there was nothing to link. The fix: at least
four of your six sentences must contain *a*, *an*, *in*, *on*, *it*, *of* or *at* in the middle,
because those little words are where the joins live.

The second break is marking the letter instead of the sound:

> an hour

left unmarked because *hour* "starts with h". The fix: say the word alone first. If what comes
out of your mouth first is a vowel, it is a vowel.

The third break is linking while reading aloud and chopping the moment you look up. This is the
most common one and it is not a mistake in the mouth; it is that the sentence is not yet yours.
The fix: say it once more with the paper turned over.

## In real life

Fluent speakers link inside a phrase and stop **between** phrases, and that stopping is how they
show where one idea ends. So the goal is not to remove every gap from your speech. It is to move
your gaps from between words to between ideas.

Under pressure — a phone call with a bad line, a counter with a queue behind you — a fluent
speaker links harder and shortens the little words further. *Can I have* becomes something like
*cunai av*. If you are listening for *can*, you will miss it. If you have practised making the
join yourself, you hear it, because your own mouth has been there.

What a native listener notices is rhythm, not sounds. Your joined sentence lands as one thing
they can answer. Your chopped sentence lands as a list they must put together first, and the
half-second that takes is the half-second in which they decide to speak to you slowly.
