## One-line answer

Record one of your own sentences twice — once with a hard stop after every single word, once joined — and play both to yourself as if you were the person behind a counter with a queue.

## The story

The counter again, and this time you are on the other side of it. Imagine you are the one
serving. Someone comes in and says, in your own language, one word at a time, with a small silence
after each: *I. would. like. to. buy. a. ticket.* You understand every word. You still have to
hold all seven in your head and put them together yourself before you can answer, and while you
are doing that, three more people join the queue behind them.

That small extra work is what your chopped English costs the person listening. You cannot feel it
from inside your own mouth. You can hear it on a recording.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **chopped** — cut into separate pieces.
- **on purpose** — deliberately; because you decided to, not by accident.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **repair** — saying the line again, correctly, straight after the wrong one. Defined on
  [day 1, part 1.3](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.3-change-one-sound.md).

You have the rule ([part 1.1](1.1-the-join-between-words.md)) and you have used it on your own
sentences ([part 1.2](1.2-link-your-own-lines.md)). What you still do not have is a reason to
believe it matters, because you have never heard the broken version from outside your own head.

So you make the broken version **on purpose**. You say one of your real sentences with a hard
stop after every word. You record it. Then you say the same sentence joined, and record that. Then
you listen to both, back to back, and you ask a question that is not "which is correct?" but
"which one could I answer straight away?"

The point of doing it on purpose is that you control it. A mistake you can make deliberately is a
mistake you can stop making.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the day's check that can go **RED**. If you record both versions and honestly cannot hear
a difference, that is a real result and it means your joined version is still chopped — which is
worth far more to you than a tidy page saying it went fine. Your day 20 gate recording is ten
sentences long. Ten chopped sentences is the single thing most likely to make a stranger stop
following it.

## The rule

**The deliberate break.** Take one sentence from your six. Say it with a hard stop after every
word — a real stop, mouth closed, air stopped, not just a slow version:

> I . live . in . a . flat . above . a . shop .

Record it. Label it in your notebook as **deliberate**.

**The correct version.** Same sentence, same speed, joins in:

> I live in a flat above a shop.

Record that too, straight after.

**The listening test.** Play both. Do not ask which sounds better — you already know the answer
you want. Ask these three, and write down the answer for each version:

| Question | Chopped version | Joined version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Could I answer this without replaying it? | | |
| How many separate things did I hear? | | |
| Did the speaker sound like they were reading? | | |

If your answers for the two versions are the same, the check is **RED**. Make the chopped version
more chopped and record again. A deliberate failure that does not fail has not been done hard
enough.

**The repair.** After the chopped take, say the joined take immediately, on one breath, without
stopping the recorder. That pair — wrong then right, one after the other — is what trains the
mouth, because it puts the two side by side while your ear still has the first one.

The target words in your line can be checked first if you are unsure of a sound. The entries at
[apple](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/apple), [orange](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/orange)
and [egg](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/egg) were opened on 2026-09-22 and each carries a sound
file.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is not chopping at all — it is a **half chop**, which a beginner
produces without meaning to and cannot hear:

> I live … in a flat … above a shop.

Small stops before the little words. The listener does not ask "Sorry?" for this one. They just
lean in. On playback it sounds fine to the speaker, because the speaker knows where the sentence
was going. The fix: put your finger on the arcs from part 1.2 while you listen. A stop at an arc
is a stop, however small it felt.

The second wrong version is chopping *and* getting louder, which is what people do when they have
been misunderstood once:

> AN . APPLE .

Louder chopping is harder to follow, not easier, because volume does not supply the joins. The
fix is the opposite of the instinct: say it again softer and joined.

The third is fixing the joins and losing the sentence stress — every word joined and every word
equally loud, which is the failure [day 13, part 1.3](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/01-speaking/1.3-every-word-equal.md)
is about. Joined is not the same as flat.

## In real life

Fluent speakers chop on purpose too, and this is the part that is worth knowing. When they want
one thing to land, they break their own rule:

> I said. I. don't. want. it.

Chopping is a tool for emphasis. It works precisely *because* normal speech is joined — the gaps
mean something only when gaps are unusual. If your speech is chopped all the time, you have spent
the tool and cannot use it when you need it.

Under pressure, a stranger who has had to ask "Sorry?" twice will switch to short questions and
yes/no answers to get the conversation finished. That is not rudeness. It is them lowering the
cost. What you lose is the chance to say anything longer than a word.

A native listener will never tell you that you chop. There is no polite sentence for it. The
recording is the only honest witness you have, which is why this part exists.
