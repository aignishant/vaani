## One-line answer

*In* means inside something, *on* means resting on a surface, *under* means below it, and *next to* means beside it — and the wrong one sends your reader to the wrong place.

## The story

You have gone out. Your sister calls: she cannot find the house keys. You are on a bus and you
cannot talk, so you send her a written line:

> The keys are on the drawer.

She goes to the chest of drawers, looks at the flat top of it, sees nothing, and calls you back.
The keys were **in** the drawer. One word, two letters, and she looked at the wrong surface for
five turns of a phone call.

You knew where the keys were the whole time. The word you chose is the only thing she had.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **preposition** — a small word that says where something is, or when: *in*, *on*, *under*,
  *next to*, *at*.
- **noun** — a word for a thing, a person or a place: *drawer*, *sister*, *Pune*. Defined on
  [day 4, part 2.1](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md).
- **surface** — the flat outside of something: a table top, a wall, a floor.
- **inside** — within the walls or edges of something.
- **beside** — at the side of; very close to.

A **preposition** of place is a small word that goes **before a noun** and tells the reader where
a thing is. English has many. Four do most of the work, and if you have these four you can put
anything anywhere:

- **in** — inside a bigger space: a box, a bag, a drawer, a room, a city.
- **on** — resting on a **surface**: a table, a wall, a floor, a shelf.
- **under** — below something, with that something above it.
- **next to** — **beside** it, at its side, touching or nearly touching.

They are short and they look unimportant, which is exactly why they get chosen carelessly. To the
reader they are the whole message. *In the drawer* and *on the drawer* are two different places in
a room.

## The rule

Each word takes the same shape: **preposition + a/an/the + noun**.

> The keys are in the drawer.
> The books are on the desk.
> Your shoes are under the bed.
> The shop is next to the bank.

Here is the difference drawn. The box is the same box in all four:

```text
                    ┌──────────┐
       on  →        │▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒│          ← a thing resting on the top
                    ├──────────┤
       in  →        │   ▒▒▒▒   │          ← a thing inside it
                    └──────────┘
    under →           ▒▒▒▒                ← a thing below it

    next to →   ▒▒  ┌──────────┐          ← a thing at its side
```

The reference page for these words was opened on 2026-09-22.
[Prepositions of place: 'in', 'on', 'at'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/prepositions-place)
says: "We use *in* to talk about a place that is inside a bigger space, such as a box, a house, a
city or a country" — "The clothes are **in** the wardrobe" — and "We use *on* to talk about
location on a surface" — "The books are **on** the desk." The page
[Adverbials of location](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adverbials-location)
lists *behind*, *between*, *by*, *in*, *near*, *next to*, *on*, *opposite*, *outside*, *over* and
*under* together, with "He was standing **right** next to the table."

**Punctuation.** A place word never takes a comma before it. *The keys are, in the drawer* is
wrong on the page and wrong in the mouth. The sentence still needs its capital letter at the
start and its full stop at the end
([day 3, part 2.1](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md)).

**Two words, one job.** *Next to* is two words and always two words. *Nextto* and *next-to* are
both wrong.

**The trap.** Your own language may use one word where English uses two, or the other way round.
English decides by **what kind of place it is**, not by what is natural: you are *in* a car but
*on* a bus, *in* a room but *on* a floor. Those are worth learning as whole phrases rather than
as a rule, because there is no rule under them.

| Phrase | Not |
| --- | --- |
| in a car / in a taxi | on a car |
| on the bus / on the train | in the bus |
| in the photo | on the photo |
| on the wall | in the wall |

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is *on* where *in* belongs, with a container:

> The keys are on the drawer.

What the reader gets is a flat top with nothing on it, and a second message to send. The fix is
one letter's worth of thought: *is it inside, or is it resting on top?*

The second is *in* where *on* belongs, with a surface:

> There is a photo in the wall.

The reader pictures a hole. The fix: a wall is a surface you hang things on, so *on the wall*.

The third is leaving the place word out entirely, which happens when the sentence feels obvious:

> The keys are the drawer.

Now the sentence says the keys *are* the drawer. The reader does not go anywhere at all. The fix:
every place needs its word, however obvious the place is.

## In real life

A real reader is not reading carefully. They are holding a phone in one hand, looking at a room
with the other eye, and they will act on your place word without rereading it. This is why the
wrong preposition costs a whole exchange rather than a small correction: they do not think "that
must mean inside", they simply look at the wrong spot and report that it is not there.

Fluent writers add a second word when the first one is not enough. *In the drawer* becomes *in the
top drawer*; *on the table* becomes *on the table by the window*. That extra word is not
decoration — it is the difference between one message and three.

What a careful reader notices, and a formal one does not forgive, is a preposition that contradicts
the rest of the sentence: *The letter is on the envelope.* It reads as a mistake about the world,
not about grammar, and it makes the whole note less trustworthy.
