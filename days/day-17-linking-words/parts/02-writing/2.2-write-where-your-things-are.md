## One-line answer

A note that places a thing needs three parts — the thing, the place word, and the one extra detail that tells the reader which one — and it is finished when the reader would not have to ask you anything.

## The story

You are away for two days. Before you leave you write a note and leave it on the kitchen table
for whoever needs it:

> The spare key is in the drawer.

You come back to four missed calls. There are three drawers in that kitchen, and the key was in
the small one under the sink. Your place word was right. It just was not enough. The reader did
exactly what you told them and still could not finish.

A note is not finished when it is true. It is finished when it is **enough**.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **note** — a short written message left for someone.
- **detail** — one extra fact that narrows something down.
- **act on** — to do what a message says without asking any more questions.
- **reader** — the real person who will read what you wrote.

[Part 2.1](2.1-the-four-place-words.md) gave you the four place words. This part is about the
sentence they live in, and about one question you ask of every such sentence before you put the
pen down: **could the reader act on this without calling me?**

A place sentence that a reader can **act on** has three parts:

1. **The thing.** *The spare key.* Not *it*, not *the thing I mentioned*.
2. **The place word and its noun.** *in the drawer.*
3. **The detail that picks one.** *the small one under the sink.*

Part three is the one beginners leave out, because when you write the note you can see the drawer
in your head and there is only one of it there. The reader is standing in a kitchen with three.

## Why Vaani needs it

Day 18's writing folder asks for a room a stranger can draw, and a room is eight or ten of these
sentences in a row. The phase 2 gate asks for a note that gives a direction — and a direction is
this same three-part sentence, repeated, where every missing detail costs the reader a wrong turn.

## The rule

Write the sentence in three parts, in this order, and check each one.

```text
   The spare key   is   in the drawer,   the small one under the sink.
   └─ the thing ─┘      └─ place word ┘  └──── the detail that picks one ────┘
```

Model lines. Each one could be acted on by someone who has never been in your house:

> The spare key is in the drawer, the small one under the sink.
> Your shoes are under the bed, on the left side.
> The letter is on the table, next to the fruit bowl.

Notice the comma in the first and third. When the detail is added at the end as an extra piece,
it takes a comma before it. When the detail is a single word inside the phrase, it does not:

> The letter is on the kitchen table.

**The test.** After writing each sentence, read it as the person who will receive it, and ask the
three questions:

| Question | If the answer is no |
| --- | --- |
| Do I know which thing is meant? | Name it properly instead of *it*. |
| Do I know inside, on top, below or beside? | Check the place word against part 2.1. |
| Is there only one place this could be? | Add the detail that picks one. |

**Keep the sentences separate.** One place per sentence. Joining them with *and* until the note is
one long line is the thing that makes a note unreadable:

> The key is in the drawer and the letter is on the table and your shoes are under the bed and …

Three sentences, three full stops. The reader is doing three jobs and wants three lines.

The shape of a short written message that a reader can act on was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Instructions for a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/instructions-colleague),
whose tips are to "supply the context or rationale behind the instructions" and to close by
inviting questions, and at
[A noticeboard message](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/noticeboard-message),
which says to "keep the message short, with only the most important information."

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the pronoun with no name:

> It's in there.

Both halves are empty. The reader knows neither the thing nor the place. This one is so common
because it is exactly what you would say out loud in the room, where pointing does the work. On
paper there is no pointing. The fix: name the thing, name the place.

The second is the true-but-not-enough note, the one in the story:

> The spare key is in the drawer.

The reader opens three drawers and then calls. The fix: one detail. *The small one under the sink.*

The third is the note that gives the detail and loses the place word:

> The spare key is the small drawer under the sink.

Now the key *is* a drawer. The fix: the place word is never the thing you drop to save space.

## In real life

A real note is read once, standing up, by someone in a hurry, usually while they are already
annoyed about not finding the thing. They will not reread it and they will not give it the benefit
of the doubt. Every ambiguity in your sentence becomes a wrong cupboard, and about two wrong
cupboards later they stop reading and phone you.

Fluent writers put the **thing first and the place last**, because that is the order the reader
needs them in: they must know what they are looking for before the location means anything.
*In the drawer under the sink you'll find the spare key* is correct English and a worse note.

A native reader forgives a clumsy sentence completely. What they do not forgive is being sent to
the wrong place twice — after that, they stop trusting your notes and start calling you for
everything, which costs you far more than the sentence did.
