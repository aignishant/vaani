## One-line answer

Rewrite your own note with three place words deliberately wrong, then carry it around your home and do exactly what it says — the wrong cupboards you open are the cost of one careless word.

## The story

Someone has left you a note. You did not write it, you cannot ask them, and it says:

> Your shoes are on the bed. The money is next to the tin. The charger is under the shelf.

So you look at the top of the bed: nothing. You look beside the tin: nothing. You crouch and look
below the shelf: nothing. Three places, three misses, and by now you have stopped believing the
note and started searching the room properly — which is what you would have done if the note had
never existed.

The shoes were **under** the bed. The money was **in** the tin. The charger was **on** the shelf.
Every noun was right. Every place word was wrong, and that was enough to make a correct note
useless.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below rest on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **swap** — to put one thing where another was.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **read back** — reading your own writing afterwards as if somebody else wrote it.
- **cost** — what something makes you lose: here, time and trust.

You know the four words ([part 2.1](2.1-the-four-place-words.md)) and you can build a note out of
them ([part 2.2](2.2-write-where-your-things-are.md)). What you do not yet know is what a wrong
one **costs**, because when you write a place word you already know where the thing is, and
knowing where it is makes every place word look fine.

So you break your own note on purpose and then obey it. Not read it — obey it. Stand up, walk to
the place, open the drawer. The wrong cupboard is the lesson, and it cannot be got from the page.

The **read back** is the check. You read your broken note as a stranger who has no idea where
anything is, and see whether you can catch all three breaks without looking at the correct
version.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing folder's check that can go **RED**. If you read the broken note back and find
only two of the three breaks, the check is RED and it has told you something true: one of your
place words looks right to you even when it is wrong. That is the one that will appear in your
day 20 page, and in every note you write after it.

## The rule

**Step one: break it.** Take the five-sentence note you wrote in part 2.2. Copy it out with
**exactly three** place words swapped for the wrong one. Change nothing else. Label it
**deliberate** at the top of the page.

> The spare key is on the drawer, the small one under the sink.
> Your shoes are on the bed, on the left side.
> The letter is under the table, next to the fruit bowl.

**Step two: obey it.** Take the broken note and walk to each place it names. Look where it says to
look. Write down what you found, which will usually be *nothing*.

**Step three: read it back and mark it.** Now read the broken note as a stranger and mark every
line you believe is wrong with **RED** in the margin. Then compare with your correct version.

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| You marked all three | GREEN. Your read-back catches place words. |
| You marked two | RED. Find which one looked right, and write down why. |
| You marked four | RED the other way. One correct line looked wrong; say which and why. |

**Step four: restore it.** Write the corrected note out again in full and mark it **GREEN**. Both
versions stay on the page. Do not rub out the broken one — it is the evidence.

The wrong versions used here are the three that beginners produce most: *on* for a container, *on*
for a thing below, and *under* for a surface. The page checked on 2026-09-22,
[Prepositions of place: 'in', 'on', 'at'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/prepositions-place),
gives the correct side of each: "The clothes are **in** the wardrobe" for a container and "The
books are **on** the desk" for a surface.

## When it breaks

The break inside the break — the thing that goes wrong with this exercise itself — is swapping a
place word for one that happens to still be true:

> The letter is next to the fruit bowl.

If the letter really is next to the fruit bowl, nothing has been broken and the walk finds it
anyway. The exercise reports GREEN and has tested nothing. The fix: check that each of your three
swaps names a place where the thing **is not**.

The second is breaking the noun instead of the place word:

> The spare key is in the cupboard.

That is a different mistake — a wrong thing, not a wrong place — and it teaches the reader nothing
about prepositions. The fix: change only the small word.

The third is doing the walk in your head. You sit at the table, read *on the drawer*, think "well,
I'd know that means inside", and mark it GREEN. You would know. The reader would not. The fix:
stand up.

## In real life

Real notes are acted on by people who cannot check with you: a family member while you are at
work, a neighbour holding a parcel, someone covering your shift. The place word is the entire
instruction, and there is no second channel to correct it through.

Fluent writers protect the reader in one specific way: when they are not certain the place word is
enough, they add a **failure line** — *if it's not there, try the shelf above*. One extra clause,
and the note survives being slightly wrong. That is a far more useful habit than being sure you
are right.

What a native reader notices is not the grammar. It is that following your note twice led nowhere,
and after that they will search the room instead of reading your notes. Losing a reader's trust
costs more than any single wrong word, and it is won back slowly.
