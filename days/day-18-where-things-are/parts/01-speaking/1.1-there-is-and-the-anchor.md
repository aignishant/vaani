## One-line answer

Before you can say where anything is, you have to give the listener a place to stand — and then every *there is* and *there are* hangs off that one spot.

## The story

You are at work. A friend is at your flat feeding your cat, and she calls:

> Where's the cat food?

You say, "There's a shelf. It's on the shelf." She says, "Which shelf?" You say, "The one near the
window." She says, "I'm in the kitchen. I can't see a window." You had forgotten she would be
standing in the kitchen and you were picturing the back room.

Nothing you said was untrue. You just never told her where to stand, so every place word you gave
her landed in the wrong room.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **anchor** — the one fixed spot you start from, so that *left*, *right* and *near* mean
  something.
- **there is** — the words English uses to say that something exists in a place.
- **singular** — one of something. **plural** — more than one.
- **shelf** — a flat board fixed to a wall for keeping things on.

Two things have to happen before a listener can put an object anywhere.

**First, the anchor.** You name a spot you both agree on: the door, the window, the bed, where
they are standing right now. Everything after that is measured from it. Without an anchor, *on the
left* is a question, not an answer — left of what?

**Second, the words that place a thing.** English says *there is* for one thing and *there are*
for more than one. They do not mean "in that direction". They mean "this thing exists, and here is
where":

> There's a shelf above the sink.
> There are two chairs by the window.

Notice what *there is* buys you. It lets you introduce a thing the listener has not seen yet. Once
it is introduced, you switch to *it* or *they*:

> There's a shelf above the sink. The cat food is on it.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your day 20 gate recording is a self-introduction a stranger can follow, and following anything
spoken means being told where to stand before being told what is there. The habit you build here
— anchor first, then contents — is the same habit that stops a spoken introduction becoming a
list of facts in no order.

## The rule

**Step one: the anchor sentence.** One short sentence, said before anything else, naming where the
listener is or where you are starting from.

> As you come in, the window's straight ahead.
> Stand at the door and look at the bed.
> You're in the kitchen, yes? Face the sink.

**Step two: place the thing, using *there is* or *there are*.**

| One thing | More than one |
| --- | --- |
| There's a shelf above the sink. | There are two shelves above the sink. |
| There's a chair by the door. | There are some boxes under the bed. |

*There's* is the short form of *there is*, and it is what people actually say. *There're* for
*there are* is said but rarely written; in speech it often comes out as *there's* even with a
plural, and nobody notices.

**Step three: hang the next thing off the last one.** This is how a listener builds a picture —
each new object attached to one they already have:

> There's a shelf above the sink. Next to the shelf there's a small cupboard. The cat food's in
> the cupboard, on the bottom shelf.

Here is the shape:

```text
   anchor  ──→  first thing  ──→  next thing  ──→  the thing they want
   "face        "there's a        "next to it       "the food's
    the sink"    shelf"            a cupboard"       in there"
```

The way a real speaker does this was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Finding the library](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/finding-library),
where the speaker anchors first — "See the big building over there? OK, so that's the lecture
theatre" — and then chains: "Next to that, on the right, is the registration office. And next to
that is the library." The place words themselves were checked at
[Adverbials of location](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adverbials-location),
which lists *behind*, *between*, *by*, *near*, *next to*, *opposite*, *over* and *under*, with
"He was standing **right** next to the table."

**The trap.** *There is* and *it is* are not the same, and swapping them is the most common
mistake in this whole subject.

| Say | When |
| --- | --- |
| There's a shelf above the sink. | The listener does not know the shelf exists yet. |
| It's above the sink. | The listener already knows which shelf you mean. |

*It's a shelf above the sink* introduces nothing and answers nothing.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is starting with *it*:

> It's on the shelf.

said to someone who has never heard of the shelf. What the listener gets is a place word attached
to nothing. They will ask "which shelf?" and you will have to start again — which is the
conversation from the story. The fix: *There's a shelf above the sink. It's on that.*

The second is *there are* with one thing, or *there is* with several:

> There is two chairs by the window.

A listener understands it and hears it as broken English, which costs you nothing today and costs
you in an interview. The fix: count the noun before you choose the verb.

The third is giving directions from **your** position instead of theirs:

> It's on your left.

when you are picturing the room from the door and they are standing at the window. Both of you are
now right and the food is still not found. The fix: say the anchor out loud, and make it a thing —
the sink, the door — not a side.

## In real life

Real people interrupt. You will not get to deliver a tidy description; you will get one sentence
out and they will say "wait, where?" A fluent speaker expects this and front-loads the anchor for
exactly that reason — the anchor is the sentence that survives being interrupted.

Under pressure — a bad phone line, someone in a hurry, a stranger holding a parcel — fluent
speakers shorten everything except the anchor. They drop *there is* entirely and say "kitchen.
Above the sink. Small cupboard." That is not bad English; it is the anchor and the chain with all
the grammar taken out, and it works because the order is right.

What a native listener notices is whether you gave them somewhere to stand. If you did, they
forgive every wrong word after it. If you did not, they stop trying to picture the room and start
asking yes/no questions, and the conversation gets four times longer.
