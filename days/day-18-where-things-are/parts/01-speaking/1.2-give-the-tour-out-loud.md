## One-line answer

Stand at the door of a real room, pick one direction, and go round the walls once without jumping — because a listener can only follow a path, never a list.

## The story

Your friend is still in your kitchen with the cat. You try again, properly this time, and you
give her everything:

> There's a shelf above the sink, and there are two boxes under the table, and the cat's bowl is
> by the door, and there's a tin on the top shelf, and the small cupboard has the dry food, and …

She says, "Slow down. Where am I looking?" You gave her six true facts in six different parts of
the room. She was turning her head after every one, and by the fourth she had lost the first.

Six facts is not a description. A description is six facts **in an order she can walk**.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **tour** — a walk round a place, seeing it in order.
- **lap** — one complete way round something, ending where you started.
- **jump** — to move suddenly from one side of a room to the other.
- **clockwise** — the direction the hands of a clock move. **anticlockwise** — the other way.

[Part 1.1](1.1-there-is-and-the-anchor.md) gave you the anchor and the words that place a thing.
This part is about the **order** those things come in, and the order is not a matter of taste.

A listener cannot hold a room in their head as a list. They hold it as a **path**. So you give
them one: you stand at the door, you pick a direction, and you go round the walls once — a
**lap** — naming what is on each wall as you pass it. When you get back to the door, you stop.

Then, and only then, you go into the middle of the room for anything standing there.

This is the whole method: **walls first, in one direction, then the middle.** It is boring and it
works, and it is what every person who has ever described a room well has done without being
told to.

## Why Vaani needs it

Day 20's gate is a spoken self-introduction of at least ten sentences that a stranger can follow.
Ten sentences in no order is the most common way that gate fails — not wrong grammar, but a
listener who has stopped building a picture. A lap round a room is the cheapest place to learn
that a spoken description needs a route.

## The rule

Four steps. Do them standing up, in a real room, with the recorder on.

**One: the anchor.** From part 1.1. Where does the listener stand?

> You're at the door. The window's on the far side.

**Two: pick a direction and say it.** Clockwise or anticlockwise — it does not matter which, it
matters that you say it and keep it.

> I'll go round to the left.

**Three: one lap of the walls.** Each wall gets its things, introduced with *there is* or *there
are*, and each new thing hangs off the last:

> On the left there's a bed. Above the bed there's a small window. Next to the bed there's a
> table with a lamp on it.
> Then the far wall — there's a cupboard, and next to the cupboard there are two boxes.
> On the right there's nothing, just the wall.

**Four: the middle.** Whatever is standing in the room, not against a wall:

> In the middle there's a table with four chairs round it.

Here is the lap drawn from above, with the door at the bottom:

```text
      ┌─────── far wall ────────┐
      │  cupboard      boxes    │
   l  │                         │  r
   e  │       [ table ]         │  i
   f  │                         │  g
   t  │  bed                    │  h
      └────── ▲ door ───────────┘  t
               │
          you start here, go left, come back here
```

**The rule that makes it a tour and not a list: never jump.** If you have named the left wall and
you suddenly say "oh, and by the door there's a mat", you have sent the listener back across the
room. Finish the lap. Add the mat at the end.

**Pace and stops.** Stop at the end of each wall. That small silence is what tells the listener
one section is finished, and it is the difference between a description and a stream. Joining your
words inside a phrase and stopping between phrases is the habit from
[day 17, part 1.2](../../../day-17-linking-words/parts/01-speaking/1.2-link-your-own-lines.md).

The chain-from-one-thing-to-the-next shape was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Finding the library](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/finding-library):
"Next to that, on the right, is the registration office. And next to that is the library."

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the one in the story — every fact joined with *and*, in the
order the facts occurred to you:

> There's a shelf and there are boxes and the bowl's by the door and …

The listener hears a true list and pictures nothing. The fix is not more detail; it is fewer
sentences in a better order.

The second is changing direction halfway:

> On the left there's a bed … and on the right there's a cupboard … and back on the left there's a
> table.

Three turns of the head for three objects. The fix: finish the wall you are on.

The third is naming things the listener cannot use:

> There's a lot of stuff on the table.

*Stuff* places nothing. The fix: name one thing, or say the table is full and move on. A tour is
allowed to skip; it is not allowed to be vague about what it does mention.

## In real life

Nobody in real life describes a whole room. They describe the route to **one object**, which is a
tour that stops as soon as the object is reached:

> Kitchen, above the sink, small cupboard, bottom shelf.

Four steps, each one smaller than the last, big to small. That is the real-world version of the
lap, and it is worth practising as its own thing: pick a target and narrow to it.

Under pressure a fluent speaker gives the anchor and the target and nothing between:
"Kitchen — above the sink." They add detail only when the listener says they cannot see it. This
is the opposite of the beginner instinct, which is to say everything first in case it helps.

What a native listener notices is whether you are moving or hopping. Moving sounds like someone
who knows the room. Hopping sounds like someone reading a list, and it makes them ask you to
start again — which, on a phone, is the sentence you least want to hear.
