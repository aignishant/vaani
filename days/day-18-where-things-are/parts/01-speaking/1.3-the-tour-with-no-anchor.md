## One-line answer

Record the same room twice — once with no anchor and no direction, once properly — then try to draw the room from the first recording alone, and keep the drawing.

## The story

Someone sends you a voice message about a room you have never seen:

> On the left there's a cupboard. There's a chair opposite. The bag's behind it. There's a window
> above.

Try it. Left of what? Opposite the cupboard, or opposite you? Behind the chair or behind the
cupboard? Above the window is above what? Every single sentence is correct English and the room
cannot be drawn.

You cannot hear this in your own recording, because you can see your own room while you speak.
The only way to find out is to make the broken version and hand it to a pencil.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **anchor** — the one fixed spot everything is measured from. From
  [part 1.1](1.1-there-is-and-the-anchor.md).
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **draw** — to make a picture with a pencil.
- **from above** — as if you were looking down at the room from the ceiling.

You have the anchor and you have the lap. This part tests whether they actually carry the
description or whether your listener has been doing the work.

The test is a drawing. You record the room **without** an anchor and **without** a direction —
just true sentences in the order they occur to you. Then you wait, play it back, and draw the room
**from above** using only what the recording says. Where you cannot decide where to put something,
you stop and mark the spot.

The marks are the finding. Each one is a sentence that sounded complete to you and was not.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking folder's check that can go **RED**. If you can draw the room correctly from
the anchorless recording, the check is RED — and the reason will be that you were drawing from
memory of your own room, not from the recording. That is worth knowing before day 20, when the
listener is a stranger with no memory to fall back on.

## The rule

**Step one: record the broken version.** Same room as
[part 1.2](1.2-give-the-tour-out-loud.md). Six or seven true sentences. No anchor sentence, no
direction named, and jump about on purpose. Say *there* and *opposite* and *behind* without
saying behind what. Label this take **deliberate** in your notebook.

> There's a cupboard on the left. Opposite there's a chair. The bag's behind it. Above there's a
> window. There are two boxes next to it. The lamp's on the other side.

**Step two: record the good version straight after.** Anchor, direction, one lap, then the middle.
Do not stop the recorder in between — the pair is the point.

**Step three: draw from the broken one.** Play the broken take. Draw the room from above. Every
time you cannot place something, put a **?** on the paper instead of the object and keep going.

**Step four: count and mark.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Three or more **?** marks | GREEN. The broken version really is broken, and you have proof. |
| One or two **?** marks | RED. Your broken version still had hidden anchors in it. Record it worse. |
| No **?** marks | RED. You drew your own room from memory. Give the recording to someone else, or wait a day and draw again. |

**Step five: draw from the good one** and put the two drawings side by side. Keep both. The pair
of drawings is the most useful thing you will make today, because it is the only view you will
ever get of your own speech from outside.

**The repair.** For each **?**, say one sentence out loud that would have fixed it, and record
those sentences. Usually it is one word: *behind the chair*, *above the cupboard*, *on your left as
you come in*.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is putting an anchor in by accident:

> As you come in there's a cupboard on the left.

*As you come in* is an anchor. The broken version must not have one, or it will not break. The
fix: reread your six sentences before recording and delete every phrase that names the door, the
window or where the listener is.

The second is drawing while listening to your own good version in your head. You know the room, so
your pencil knows where the bag goes. The fix: wait until the next day, or hand the recording to
another person and have them draw it.

The third is the most interesting one, and it is worth watching for: the broken version is
**pleasant to listen to**. It flows, it has no awkward anchor sentence at the front, and it sounds
more natural than the good version. It is also useless. Fluent and followable are not the same
thing, and this is where you find that out.

## In real life

Real listeners never say "you gave me no anchor". They say "sorry, where?" — once. If you answer
with another anchorless sentence, they stop asking and start guessing, and the guess is usually
wrong. By the time you find out, the parcel is at the wrong door or the cat has not been fed.

Fluent speakers repair differently from beginners. A beginner who is asked "where?" repeats the
same sentence louder. A fluent speaker throws the whole sentence away and starts with the anchor:
"Right — are you in the kitchen? OK. Face the sink." Notice the question. They check where the
listener is standing before saying anything else.

What a native listener quietly registers is whether talking to you is expensive. A description
they can draw costs them nothing. One they cannot costs them four questions, and people
unconsciously avoid the second kind of conversation.
