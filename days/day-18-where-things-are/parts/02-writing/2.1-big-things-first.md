## One-line answer

A written room starts with the room itself, then the walls, then the furniture, then the small things — because a reader cannot put a lamp anywhere until they know how big the room is.

## The story

You are letting out a spare room and you write a card for the noticeboard at the shop. You want
it to sound good, so you start with the best bit:

> Lovely room. There's a blue lamp on the desk and a mirror and the curtains are new. Bed
> included. Near the market.

A man phones, comes round, stands in the doorway and says, "Oh — it's smaller than I thought."
He had built a big room in his head, because the first thing you gave him was a lamp, and a lamp
floats in whatever room the reader is already imagining.

You never lied. You just handed him the small things before the big ones, and he furnished the
rest himself.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **furniture** — the big things in a room: a bed, a table, a cupboard, a chair.
- **fits** — is small enough to go in the space.
- **order** — which thing comes first, second and third.
- **shape** — whether a room is square, long and narrow, or an odd shape.
- **mirror** — glass you can see yourself in.

When you **speak** about a room, the listener can interrupt. On paper they cannot. So the order
has to do the work that questions would have done, and there is only one order that works:

1. **The room.** How big, what shape, what it is for. One sentence.
2. **The openings.** The door and the windows — because they fix which way everything faces.
3. **The furniture.** The big things, each against a named wall.
4. **The small things.** Only the ones that matter.

This is **big to small**, and the reason is simple: a reader builds a picture in the order you
give it, and they cannot un-build it. Give them a lamp first and they build a room round the lamp.
Give them the room first and the lamp has somewhere to land.

## Why Vaani needs it

Day 20's gate page is ten sentences about yourself that a stranger can read straight through. The
same rule decides whether it works: the big fact first — where you live, what you do — and the
small ones after. A page that opens on a detail makes the reader build the wrong person, and they
do not go back and rebuild.

## The rule

**The four layers, in order.** One sentence each, or two if the layer is busy.

```text
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ 1. the room      "a small square room"  │  how big, what shape
   │ 2. the openings  "door, one window"     │  which way it faces
   │ 3. the furniture "bed, desk, cupboard"  │  the big things, on named walls
   │ 4. the details   "a blue lamp"          │  only what matters
   └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
        big  ─────────────────────────→  small
```

A model paragraph, in that order:

> It's a small square room at the back of the flat. There's one window on the left and the door
> is opposite it. The bed is under the window, and next to the bed there's a desk. There's a
> cupboard behind the door. On the desk there's a blue lamp.

**One thing per sentence.** A sentence that places two objects makes the reader hold one while
they place the other:

| Write | Not |
| --- | --- |
| The bed is under the window. Next to the bed there's a desk. | The bed is under the window and next to it there's a desk and a chair. |

**Every object gets a place word.** *In*, *on*, *under*, *next to*, *behind*, *opposite* — from
[day 17, part 2.1](../../../day-17-linking-words/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-four-place-words.md).
An object with no place word is a thing the reader has to put somewhere themselves, and they will
put it in the wrong spot.

**A size, at least once.** *Small*, *long*, *narrow*, *big enough for two people*. Without one
number or one size word, the reader uses their own room, which is the mistake in the story.

**It is a paragraph, not a list.** One block, sentences in order, each with a capital letter and a
full stop
([day 13, part 2.1](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-paragraph-is.md)).

The shape of a short written description a stranger acts on was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[A noticeboard message](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/noticeboard-message),
whose model opens with the room and its size — "Single ensuite room available in our lovely
three-bedroom house" — and whose tips are to "write the main title or idea bigger to catch
people's attention" and to "keep the message short, with only the most important information."
The place words were checked at
[Prepositions of place: 'in', 'on', 'at'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/prepositions-place):
"The books are **on** the desk"; "The clothes are **in** the wardrobe."

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is small to big — starting with the thing you like best:

> There's a blue lamp on the desk. The curtains are new. It's a nice room.

The reader has a lamp and no room. The fix: move the room sentence to the front. Nothing else has
to change.

The second is a room with no door and no window:

> It's a big room. There's a bed, a desk and a cupboard.

Nothing faces anywhere, so *left* and *opposite* cannot be used at all, and the reader cannot tell
the difference between this and a storeroom. The fix: one sentence naming the door and the window.

The third is the detail with no place word:

> There's a mirror.

Where? The reader hangs it wherever, and if they are choosing between two rooms, "wherever" is not
good enough. The fix: *There's a mirror on the back of the door.*

## In real life

A real reader of a room description is deciding something — whether to come and see it, whether
their things will fit, whether to take the shift that comes with it. They read once, on a phone,
and they stop as soon as they have decided. Everything after the sentence where they decided was
wasted, which is the real argument for big things first.

Fluent writers add one sentence beginners never think of: **what is not there**. *There's no
cupboard* or *no window on that side*. Saying what is missing is worth more than three more
details, because a reader who is measuring for their own furniture needs the gaps, not the
contents.

A native reader forgives clumsy sentences and unusual words. They do not forgive arriving and
finding the room different from the page — and "different" is almost never a lie. It is almost
always an order that let them imagine the rest.
