## One-line answer

Write your own room as one paragraph of eight to ten sentences in the four-layer order, and then test it by answering, as a stranger, the three questions a stranger would actually have.

## The story

The card goes back up at the shop, rewritten. This time it opens with the room:

> It's a small square room at the back, with one window.

A woman phones. She does not ask how big it is — she already knows. She asks:

> Is there anywhere to put a desk?

You had not written that, because you had never needed it. You have a bed and a cupboard in
there and you had never thought about the two feet of empty wall behind the door.

The paragraph was correct and it was not yet **finished**, because finished means it has already
answered the questions a stranger brings.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **stranger** — someone who has never seen the room and knows nothing about it.
- **question a stranger brings** — the thing they need to know that you never wondered about.
- **read back** — reading your own writing afterwards as if somebody else wrote it.
- **empty wall** — a wall with nothing against it, which is space somebody could use.

[Part 2.1](2.1-big-things-first.md) gave you the order. This part is about **whose room you are
writing**, and the answer is: not yours.

You know your room. You know the door sticks, you know where the light is bad, you know the
cupboard is deeper than it looks. None of that is on the page, and you cannot see that it is
missing, because your eye fills it in every time you read.

So a written room is finished in two steps, not one. You write it in the four layers. Then you
put it down, come back, and read it as a **stranger** — someone with a bed to move in and no
memory of the place at all.

## Why Vaani needs it

Day 20's gate page is ten sentences about you, read by a stranger. The same two-step applies, and
the second step is the one that gets skipped. A page written and never read back as somebody else
is a page that answers the writer's questions, and the writer already knows the answers.

## The rule

**Step one: the four lines.** Before any paragraph, write the four layers as four bare lines.

```text
   1. the room      →  small, square, at the back
   2. the openings  →  one window on the left, door opposite
   3. the furniture →  bed under window, cupboard behind door, empty wall on the right
   4. the details   →  hook on the door, light switch by the bed
```

**Step two: the paragraph.** Turn the four lines into eight to ten sentences, one object each,
every object with a place word, at least one size word, one block with no bullet points.

> It's a small square room at the back of the flat, big enough for a single bed and one more
> thing. There's one window on the left and the door is opposite it. The bed is under the window.
> Behind the door there's a tall cupboard. The wall on the right is empty — there's room there for
> a desk. There's a hook on the back of the door. The light switch is next to the bed. It gets sun
> in the morning and not in the afternoon.

**Step three: the stranger's three questions.** Put the paragraph down. Come back to it. Now
answer these **using only what is on the page**:

| The stranger asks | Can your paragraph answer it? |
| --- | --- |
| Where would I put my own things? | |
| What is opposite the door? | |
| Is there anything about this room I would only find out by coming? | |

Any question you cannot answer from the page is a sentence you have not written yet. Add it. That
is the whole of step three.

**The sentence beginners never write.** The one about what is **not** there or what is
**awkward**: no cupboard, no plug near the bed, gets loud in the evening. One honest line of that
kind does more for a reader than four more details, and it is what makes the rest believable.

**Punctuation.** A dash can add an afterthought — like this one — and a comma cannot do that job.
Every sentence still takes its capital letter and its full stop, and the whole thing stays one
block.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the paragraph that is all good news:

> Lovely bright room, lots of space, very quiet, close to everything.

Nothing in it can be checked and nothing in it can be pictured. A reader has learned only that you
like the room. The fix: replace each opinion word with the fact that caused it. *Bright* becomes
*one big window facing east*.

The second is writing the room as a list of furniture with no walls:

> Bed, cupboard, desk, chair, lamp.

That is an inventory, not a description. The fix: each item gets a wall and a place word.

The third is answering your own questions instead of the stranger's:

> The door sticks a bit but you get used to it.

You know it sticks because you live there. What the stranger wanted to know was whether their desk
fits. The fix: the three-question table, honestly answered as somebody who has never been inside.

## In real life

A real reader of your description is comparing it against two other rooms on the same noticeboard.
They are not reading yours closely; they are reading all three for the same three facts. Whichever
paragraph answers those fastest gets the phone call, and the answer is almost never the
best-written one — it is the one arranged in the reader's order rather than the writer's.

Fluent writers cut. A paragraph of ten sentences that answers the three questions beats one of
twenty that also mentions the curtains. Under pressure — a short form, a message with a length
limit, a note somebody will read standing up — the four layers survive and the details go.

What a careful reader notices is one honest negative sentence, and it changes how they read the
rest. A page with nothing wrong in it reads as an advertisement. A page with one small problem in
it reads as a description, and they believe the good parts.
