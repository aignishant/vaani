## One-line answer

Take your finished paragraph, put the sentences in the wrong order and take out the size words, then answer the stranger's three questions from the broken version — the questions you can no longer answer are what order was doing for you.

## The story

Two cards go up at the shop. They describe the same room and contain the same facts.

The first:

> A blue lamp is on the desk. There's a hook on the back of the door. The bed is under the window.
> It's at the back of the flat. The wall on the right is empty. There's one window on the left.

The second:

> It's a small square room at the back of the flat. There's one window on the left and the door is
> opposite it. The bed is under the window. The wall on the right is empty. There's a hook on the
> back of the door. There's a blue lamp on the desk.

Same room. Same sentences. One of them gets phone calls. Nobody reading the first one could tell
you what is opposite the door, and it is not because a fact is missing — every fact is there.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **shuffle** — to mix things up so they are no longer in order.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **order** — which sentence comes first, second and third.
- **proof** — something that shows a thing is true, that somebody else could check.

It is easy to agree that order matters. It is much harder to believe it about **your own**
paragraph, because your paragraph contains all the right facts and you can see the room while you
read it.

So you break it in the one way that removes nothing. You keep every sentence, every word and every
fact, and you only change the **order** — plus you delete the size words, which is the other
invisible thing doing work. Then you take the stranger's three questions from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-your-room.md) and answer them from the broken version.

If the same questions that were answerable before are unanswerable now, you have **proof** that
order carried them. That proof is the point. Nothing here is about writing more.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing folder's check that can go **RED**. If your broken paragraph still answers all
three stranger questions, the check is RED, and it usually means the original was doing something
other than order — often that you had already written a summary sentence somewhere that survives
shuffling. Finding that out now is worth more than a tidy result.

## The rule

**Step one: break it, twice over.** Copy your part 2.2 paragraph and make exactly two changes:

1. **Shuffle the sentences.** Details first, the room sentence somewhere in the middle, the
   openings near the end. Keep every sentence whole and unchanged.
2. **Delete every size word** — *small*, *big enough*, *tall*, *narrow*, and any measurement.

Change nothing else. No sentence is reworded and no fact is removed. Label the page
**deliberate**.

**Step two: answer the three questions from the broken version only.** Cover the original.

| The stranger asks | Broken version | Original |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where would I put my own things? | | |
| What is opposite the door? | | |
| Would my bed fit? | | |

**Step three: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Two or three questions now unanswerable | GREEN. Order and size words were carrying them, and you have proof. |
| One unanswerable | RED. Shuffle harder — put the room sentence last — and check every size word is gone. |
| None unanswerable | RED. Something in the original is doing the work instead of order. Find that sentence and write down what it is. |

**Step four: restore and mark GREEN.** Write the correct paragraph out again in full. Keep the
broken one on the page underneath it. Do not rub it out — it is the evidence, and in three weeks
it will be the only thing that reminds you why the order is what it is.

**The one-line finding.** Under both versions, write one sentence saying which question died first
and why. That sentence is what you actually learned today.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is rewriting while shuffling. You move the room sentence to the middle and quietly add
*anyway* to the front of it so it reads better:

> Anyway, it's a room at the back of the flat.

Now you have tested your editing, not the order. The fix: move sentences whole, or not at all.

The second is leaving one size word in:

> It's a square room at the back, big enough for a bed.

*Big enough for a bed* answers the third question on its own, and the check will come back RED for
a reason that has nothing to do with order. The fix: read the broken version once, hunting only
for sizes.

The third is answering the three questions from memory of the room. This is the same trap as every
read-back: you are the worst possible reader of your own page. The fix: answer in writing, and for
each answer write down **which sentence** gave it to you. If you cannot name the sentence, the
answer came from your head.

## In real life

Real readers do not shuffle anything, so why does this matter? Because real readers **stop early**,
and stopping early has the same effect as shuffling: everything after the stopping point might as
well not be there. A paragraph in the wrong order is a paragraph whose important sentences are
below the place where the reader gave up.

Fluent writers know where the reader stops and put the load-bearing sentences above it. In a room
advert that is the first two lines; in an email it is the first line; in a form it is the first
box. Nothing about this is a writing skill in the usual sense — it is a decision about order,
taken before the first sentence.

What a careful reader notices about a shuffled description is not that it is disordered. It is
that they finish it without being able to picture the thing, and they put it down thinking the
room is probably bad. Order does not just carry the facts. It carries whether the reader trusts
them.
