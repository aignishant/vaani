## One-line answer

Saying what you can do needs a true sentence and a small honest limit beside it; asking permission needs the same word said as a question, with your voice going up at the end.

## The story

You are being shown round on the first morning of a new job. Somebody asks:

> Can you drive?

You can. You passed the test four years ago and you have driven perhaps twice since. You say
"Yes, I can," because that is true. On Thursday you are handed keys to a van in a city you do not
know.

The word was right. What was missing was the second half — *I can drive, but I haven't driven for
years* — and the second half is the part that would have protected you.

Saying what you can do is not a test of grammar. It is a small, honest claim somebody is going to
act on.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **claim** — something you say about yourself that another person will believe and act on.
- **limit** — how far your ability actually goes: a bit, well, not for years.
- **ask permission** — to ask whether you are allowed to do something.
- **rising voice** — the voice going up at the end, which marks a question.

*Can* does two different jobs and they need two different habits.

**Ability — a claim about you.** *I can cook. I can't swim.* Somebody will act on this. The useful
habit is the **limit**: one short phrase saying how far it goes. Not modesty — accuracy.

> I can drive, but not in the city.
> I can cook a few things.
> I can read English better than I speak it.

**Permission — a question to somebody else.** *Can I sit here? Can I pay by card?* Here the word
does no work at all until your **voice goes up** at the end. Same words, flat voice, and it lands
as a statement or as a demand.

The two jobs share one word and nothing else. Mixing them up is why a beginner can sound as though
they are asking for permission to be able to swim.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your day 20 gate recording has to be ten sentences a stranger can follow, and "what I can do" is
where most people's introduction gets real — a language, a job skill, something you make. Saying
it with a limit is the difference between a sentence a stranger believes and one they quietly
discount.

## The rule

**For ability, three pieces: the claim, the limit, the proof.**

| Piece | Example |
| --- | --- |
| the claim | I can cook. |
| the limit | Only a few things, though. |
| the proof | I make the same three dinners every week. |

> I can cook a few things. I make the same three dinners every week.
> I can drive, but I haven't driven for years.
> I can't swim at all. I've never learnt.

Notice that the negative gets the same treatment. *I can't swim* is a fine sentence; *I can't swim,
I've never learnt* gives the listener somewhere to go next, which is the whole job of a spoken
answer.

Remember the sizes from [part 1.1](1.1-the-quiet-can-and-the-loud-cant.md): *can* is swallowed,
*can't* is long and loud, and the verb after *can* is the word you lean on.

**For permission, put the subject after *can* and take your voice up.**

> Can I sit here?
> Can I pay by card?
> Can you take a message, please?

The page [can and could](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/can-could),
checked on 2026-09-22, gives the form — "We make questions by putting the subject after *can*" —
and the model lines: "**Can you take** a message, please?" and "**Can I help** you?" It also notes
that *can* is "**less polite**" than *could* for a request, which matters at a counter but not
between friends.

**Where the voice goes.** A permission question rises at the end, like the yes/no questions from
[day 12, part 1.1](../../../day-12-yes-no-questions/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-rising-question.md).

```text
   Can I sit here?              Can I sit here.
              ╱                            ╲
   a question                   a demand, or a statement
```

**Please, and where it goes.** *Please* goes at the end, after a small pause, or right after
*can*. It does not go in the middle of the verb.

| Say | Not |
| --- | --- |
| Can I have a bag, please? | Can I please to have a bag? |
| Could you say that again, please? | Can you say please again that? |

**The two-word answer.** When somebody asks *you* for permission or about your ability, the short
answer keeps *can* at the end, where it is big:

> Can you drive? — Yes, I can. / No, I can't.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the bare claim with no limit:

> I can drive.

said by somebody who has not driven in four years. Nothing is ungrammatical and the consequence is
the van in the story. The fix is one clause: *but not for a while now*.

The second is the permission question with no rise:

> Can I sit here.

Flat. What the other person hears is somebody announcing that they are sitting down. They will
usually move their bag anyway, and they will decide you are a bit rude. The fix: take the last
word up.

The third is adding *to* after *can*, which beginners do because most English verbs take it:

> I can to swim.

A listener understands and hears it as broken. The fix: *can* is followed by the plain verb and
nothing between them. *I can swim.*

The fourth is using *can* for something that is not ability or permission:

> Can you the window?

with the verb missing entirely. The fix: *can* always has a verb after it — *open*, *close*,
*take*, *help*.

## In real life

Fluent speakers rarely answer an ability question with *yes* alone. They answer with a size:
*yeah, a bit*, *not really*, *I used to*. That is the same three-piece answer with the grammar
taken out, and it is what makes a conversation continue — a bare *yes* ends it.

Under pressure, the polite forms get shorter, not longer. In a queue nobody says *Could you
possibly pass me one of those, please?* They say *Can I get one of those?* with a strong rise and a
smile. The rise is doing all the politeness, which is worth knowing: if your voice does not rise,
no amount of extra words will save the sentence.

A native listener forgives *I can to swim* completely. What they act on is the claim without the
limit — and they will not blame you afterwards for the van, because as far as they heard, you said
you could drive.
