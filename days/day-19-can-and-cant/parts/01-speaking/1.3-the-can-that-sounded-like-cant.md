## One-line answer

Record six sentences with *can* made big and *can't* made short, play them back a day later with the words hidden, and write down which one you think you said — the ones you get wrong are the ones a stranger would get wrong too.

## The story

Somebody leaves you a voice message about whether they are coming on Saturday. You play it three
times:

> I can make it on Saturday.

Or possibly *I can't make it on Saturday*. The recording is not clear, the room was noisy, and the
word in the middle is short either way. You cannot tell, and the whole message hangs on it.

That is what a swallowed *can't* or an over-stressed *can* does. Not a small mistake in the middle
of a clear sentence — a sentence whose meaning has two possible opposites and no way to choose.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **reversal** — when the meaning comes out as the opposite of what you meant.
- **blind test** — checking something without being able to see the answer.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **transcript** — writing down what you hear on a recording.

You cannot judge your own *can* and *can't* while you are saying them, because you know which one
you meant, and knowing makes both versions sound clear. Every learner who has ever recorded these
two words has played the take back and thought "that's obviously the negative" — while a stranger
hearing the same take would have had to guess.

So this part uses a **blind test**. You record the sentences in a mixed order, you write the order
down and put it away, and you come back to the recording later — the next day is best — and make a
**transcript** without looking. Then you compare.

The comparison is the whole exercise. It is the only way to hear your own speech as somebody who
does not already know what you meant.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking folder's check that can go **RED**. A sentence whose meaning can flip is worse
than a sentence with a wrong sound in it, because the listener does not know they have misheard.
Your day 20 recording will almost certainly contain one *can*; if a stranger reads it as *can't*,
the sentence does not become unclear, it becomes false.

## The rule

**Step one: build the mixed list.** Write twelve short sentences about your own life: six positive
with *can*, six negative with *can't*. Same verbs on both sides, so the only difference is the
little word.

> I can swim. / I can't swim.
> I can drive at night. / I can't drive at night.

**Step two: record the deliberately bad version.** Say all twelve in a shuffled order, and break
them on purpose in the two ways beginners really break them:

- every *can* said **big and clear**, as if you were arguing;
- every *can't* said **short and quick**, with the *t* swallowed.

Label this take **deliberate**. Write the true order on a piece of paper, fold it, and put it
somewhere you will not see it.

**Step three: record the good version straight after.** Same twelve, same order or a new one —
this time with *can* swallowed and the verb loud, *can't* long and loud.

**Step four: the blind transcript.** Later — the next day if you can wait — play the deliberate
take and write down, for each sentence, whether you think it was positive or negative. No
pausing, one listen each, the way a real listener gets it.

**Step five: unfold the paper and mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Three or more wrong out of twelve | GREEN. The broken version really is ambiguous, and now you know what it costs. |
| One or two wrong | RED. Your deliberate version was not broken enough, or you remembered the order. Make the *can*s bigger and the *can't*s shorter and record again. |
| None wrong | RED. You transcribed from memory. Wait a day, or hand the recording to another person and have them do the transcript. |

**Step six: the repair.** For every sentence you got wrong, say it again correctly, twice, on the
recorder — once positive, once negative, back to back — so your ear gets the two shapes side by
side while the mistake is still fresh.

The two forms were checked on 2026-09-22 at [can](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/can) — stressed
/kæn/, weak /kən/ — and [can't](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/can%27t) — /kɑːnt/ — and both
entries list an audio file.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is choosing sentences where the meaning gives the answer away:

> I can breathe under water.

Nobody needs to hear the little word; the world supplies it. The fix: every sentence must be true
either way for somebody, somewhere — *I can drive*, *I can cook*, *I can come on Thursday*.

The second is doing the transcript straight after recording. You will score twelve out of twelve
and learn nothing. The fix: wait, or hand it to somebody else. If neither is possible, transcribe
the take backwards, from the last sentence to the first — it breaks the memory of the order.

The third is repairing only the negatives. Most learners assume the problem is *can't*, fix that,
and leave every *can* big and clear. The over-stressed positive is the half that makes you sound
as though you are arguing, and it does not announce itself. The fix: repair both sides of every
wrong pair.

## In real life

Real listeners almost never ask. When a sentence could be *can* or *can't*, they pick the one that
fits what they expect and carry on, and you both find out on Saturday. This is the reason the
reversal is expensive: it does not produce a question, it produces a wrong plan.

Fluent speakers protect the negative when the line is bad. They add a word: *no, I can't make it*,
*sorry, I can't*. The extra word is not politeness — it is a second copy of the meaning, in case
the first one is lost. When you cannot be sure you were heard, that is the move to copy.

And the other direction is worth knowing too: when a native speaker cannot tell which you said,
what they say is "sorry — you can, or you can't?" That question is completely normal and it is the
right thing to ask when you are the listener. Asking it is not weakness in English; it is what
everybody does.
