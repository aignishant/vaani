## One-line answer

*Can* is followed by the plain verb with nothing added and nothing in between — no *-s*, no *to* — and its negative is *can't* with the apostrophe standing exactly where the *no* was taken out.

## The story

You are filling in a form for a flat. There is a box that says *Anything else we should know?* and
you write:

> My sister can to stay sometimes. She cans cook so the kitchen will be used.

The agent reads it, understands it perfectly, and forms an opinion about you that has nothing to
do with your sister. Two small extra letters, in a box that was not even about English, and the
page now says something about you that you did not write.

Grammar on a form is not about being correct. It is about which of the two things the reader
notices: what you said, or how you said it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **plain verb** — the verb with nothing added: *go*, *swim*, *cook*, *drive*. Not *goes*, not
  *going*, not *to go*.
- **apostrophe** — the mark ' used to show a letter has been taken out. Met on
  [day 14, part 2.2](../../../day-14-talking-about-family/parts/02-writing/2.2-the-apostrophe-s.md).
- **modal** — a small helper verb like *can* that sits in front of another verb and changes what
  it means.
- **form** — a printed page with boxes to fill in.

*Can* is a **modal**. That is a grammar word for a small group of helper verbs, and the whole
group behaves in one strange way that is worth learning once:

**They never change, and the verb after them never changes either.**

Ordinary verbs take an *-s* for *he*, *she* and *it*
([day 15, part 2.1](../../../day-15-daily-routine/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-s-on-he-she-it.md)).
*Can* does not. Nothing in the whole sentence takes an *-s*:

| Right | Wrong |
| --- | --- |
| She can cook. | She cans cook. |
| She can cook. | She can cooks. |

And the verb after *can* is the **plain verb** with no *to* in front of it. This is the one that
catches everybody, because so many other English patterns do take *to*: *I want to go*, *I need to
go* — but *I can go*.

## Why Vaani needs it

Day 20's gate page is ten sentences about yourself with correct capital letters and full stops,
read by a stranger. Almost everyone writes at least one sentence about what they can do. Two
wrong letters in that sentence are the first thing the reader's eye stops on, and a page that is
being judged as a whole does not get to explain itself.

## The rule

**The positive: subject + can + plain verb.**

```text
   She    can    cook.
   ‾‾‾    ‾‾‾    ‾‾‾‾
   who    modal  plain verb — nothing added, nothing in front
```

> She can cook.
> They can start on Monday.
> I can carry that for you.

**Every subject, the same word.** This is the easy part. There is no list to learn:

| I | can |
| you | can |
| he, she, it | can |
| we, they | can |

**The negative: can't, or cannot.** The apostrophe replaces the missing letters of *not*:

```text
   can  +  not   →   can not   →   can't
                              ‾    the apostrophe stands where "no" was
```

> She can't cook.
> They can't start until Monday.

Two spellings, and they are not the same register. The page
[can and could](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/can-could),
checked on 2026-09-22, says: "The **negative** form is ***can't*** in spoken English and
***cannot*** in written English." So in a message to a friend, *can't*. In a form, an application
or a letter to somebody you do not know, *cannot* — written as **one word**, never *can not*.

**The question: can comes first.** The same page gives the rule — "We make questions by putting
the subject after *can*" — and the model lines "**Can you take** a message, please?" and "**Can I
help** you?"

```text
   statement   You  can  park  here.
   question    Can  you  park  here?
               ‾‾‾  ‾‾‾
               swapped — and the sentence now ends in a question mark
```

**The question mark is not optional.** A question written with a full stop is a statement, however
you meant it:

| Write | Not |
| --- | --- |
| Can I park here? | Can I park here. |
| Can I park here? | You can park here? |

That last one is a real sentence in English, but it means something different — it is a surprised
check, not a request.

**What can does, in one table.** All four uses take the same shape, which is why the shape is
worth getting right once. The examples are from the page checked above:

| Use | Model line |
| --- | --- |
| ability | "She **can** **speak** several languages." |
| general possibility | "It **can be** very cold here in winter." |
| giving permission | "You **can go** home now." |
| refusing permission | "**You can't go** home yet." |

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is *to* after *can*:

> My sister can to stay sometimes.

A reader understands it and marks you as a beginner in the first line. The fix: take the *to* out.
Nothing else changes.

The second is the *-s*, which comes from the habit built on day 15:

> She cans cook.

The *-s* rule is a good rule and it does not apply here. The fix: after *can*, nothing takes an
*-s* — not *can*, not the verb.

The third is the apostrophe in the wrong place, or missing:

> I cant come. / I ca'nt come.

*Cant* is a different word, and *ca'nt* is not a word at all. The fix: the apostrophe goes where
the letters were removed, between the *n* and the *t*.

The fourth is *can not* as two words in a formal document:

> The tenant can not keep pets.

It is not wrong in the way the others are wrong, but a formal reader reads it as an error. The
fix: *cannot*.

## In real life

A real reader of a form is reading forty of them. They are not marking your grammar and they do
not care about it in the abstract — but a sentence with two extra letters in it slows them down
for half a second, and half a second of friction, repeated, is what "this application looks
careless" is made of.

Fluent writers switch spelling by document. A message to a friend gets *can't* and no second
thought. A letter, a form, an application or anything going to somebody who can say no gets
*cannot*, because the full spelling reads as considered. Nobody is taught this; everybody does it.

What a formal reader does not forgive is a rule written with the wrong one: *Guests can not stay
overnight* on a notice reads as an unfinished thought, and someone will argue that it means guests
are allowed not to stay. *Cannot* closes that door.
