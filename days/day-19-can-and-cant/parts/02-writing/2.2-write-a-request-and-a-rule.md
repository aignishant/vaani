## One-line answer

A written request is *can* turned into a question plus the reason the reader needs to say yes; a written rule is *can* and *cannot* used flatly, with no reason at all.

## The story

Two notes go up in the same building in the same week.

On the stairs:

> Can somebody please not leave bags here.

On the front door:

> Bicycles cannot be left in the hallway. There is a rack at the side of the building.

The first is a question mark short of a question, a request short of a reason, and it has been
ignored for a month. The second is a flat sentence with somewhere to put your bicycle, and
everybody follows it.

The difference is not politeness. It is that one of them knows whether it is asking or telling,
and the other does not.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **request** — asking somebody to do something they are free to refuse.
- **rule** — telling somebody what is allowed, which they are not free to refuse.
- **reason** — why you are asking; the thing that makes a yes easy.
- **register** — how formal or friendly a piece of writing is.

*Can* writes both of these, and they need opposite things.

**A request** is a question. It rises, it can be refused, and the thing that makes it work is the
**reason**. Without a reason, the reader has to guess whether it matters, and "I'll do it later"
is what guessing produces.

**A rule** is a statement. It does not rise, it is not refusable, and giving it a reason often
makes it weaker rather than stronger — but giving it an **alternative** makes it work. *Cannot go
here* plus *can go there* is a rule people follow. *Cannot go here* on its own is a rule people
argue with.

The mistake in the story is writing one as the other: a request with no question mark and no
reason is a rule with no authority, and nobody obeys it.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate asks for a written note that asks for something, thanks somebody and gives a
direction. The asking half is exactly this part: a question, a reason, and a way for the reader to
say yes. Day 20's page is closer than that — it is ten sentences a stranger reads without you
there — and the same question sits underneath it: am I telling this reader something, or asking
them for something?

## The rule

**The request, in three pieces.**

| Piece | Example |
| --- | --- |
| the question | Can you send me the form? |
| the reason | I need it before Friday. |
| the easy yes | If it's easier, I can come and collect it. |

> Can you send me the form? I need it before Friday. If it's easier, I can come and collect it.

Notice the third *can* — the one about you. Offering to do part of the work yourself is what turns
a request into something the reader can settle in one line. The model for this shape was checked
on 2026-09-22 at
[Instructions for a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/instructions-colleague),
whose tips are to use "Can / Could / Would you please", to "supply the context or rationale behind
the instructions", and to close by inviting questions. Its model line: "Could you please book the
room for us?"

**The rule, in two pieces.**

| Piece | Example |
| --- | --- |
| what is not allowed | Bicycles cannot be left in the hallway. |
| where it can go instead | There is a rack at the side of the building. |

> Residents cannot park in front of the gate. There are two spaces behind the building.
> You can use the kitchen at any time. Please wash up before you leave.

*Cannot* in a rule, not *can't*
([part 2.1](2.1-can-with-the-plain-verb.md)). A notice with *can't* on it reads as somebody's
opinion; a notice with *cannot* reads as the building's.

**The two shapes side by side.**

```text
   REQUEST    Can you …?     +  reason     +  easy yes      → ends in ?
   RULE       You cannot …   +  alternative                 → ends in .
```

**Register: pick one and hold it.** A note to a friend and a notice for a building are different
documents and mixing them is what makes writing sound odd:

| To a friend | On a notice |
| --- | --- |
| Can you grab milk? | Milk cannot be left in the shared fridge overnight. |
| Sorry — can't make Tuesday. | The office cannot accept deliveries on Tuesdays. |

**The question mark, again.** Every request ends in one. A request that ends in a full stop is the
note on the stairs, and it is the most common failed piece of writing in any building in the
world.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the request with no reason:

> Can you send me the form?

It is correct English and it will sit unanswered, because the reader has no idea whether it is
urgent. The fix: one clause. *I need it before Friday.*

The second is the polite rule:

> Can you please not leave bags on the stairs?

This asks permission to have a rule. Readers who were going to leave bags there still will, and
now they know nobody is going to stop them. The fix: decide. If it is a rule, write it as one —
*Bags cannot be left on the stairs* — and give the alternative.

The third is the rule with no alternative:

> Bicycles cannot be left in the hallway.

Where, then? Somebody will lean theirs against the door and say they had no choice, and they will
be right. The fix: the second sentence.

The fourth is the request that is really an instruction, written as a question, to somebody who
cannot refuse:

> Can you finish this today?

If the answer cannot be no, the question mark is a costume. A reader who notices resents it. The
fix: say what it is — *This needs to be finished today. Can you tell me if that's a problem?*
Now the question is real.

## In real life

Real requests are answered in the order they are easy to answer. A message with a question, a
reason and an offer gets dealt with immediately, because it can be finished in one reply. A
message with only a question sits, because the reader has to work out what it is for before they
can act, and working that out is a job they will do later.

Fluent writers put the question **first** and the reason second. Beginners put three lines of
apology first and the question at the bottom, where a reader on a phone never reaches it. The
request is the load-bearing sentence and it goes at the top.

What a careful reader notices about a rule is whether it was written by somebody who thought
about the alternative. A notice that says where the bicycles **can** go reads as somebody managing
a building. One that only says where they cannot reads as somebody complaining, and complaints get
ignored.
