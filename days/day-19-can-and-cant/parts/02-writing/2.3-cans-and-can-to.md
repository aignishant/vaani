## One-line answer

Copy your own four pieces with one of each of the four standard *can* errors planted in them, wait, then read back hunting only for those — because the one you miss is the one that will be in your gate page.

## The story

A notice goes up in a shared kitchen. It has been read by the person who wrote it four times, and
it says:

> Residents cans use the kitchen at any time. Guests can not to cook after ten.

Nobody in the building is confused about the kitchen. Everybody in the building now knows
something about the person who wrote the notice, and it is not what they wanted to communicate.

The writer read it four times. That is the part worth sitting with: reading your own sentence is
not a check, because your eye supplies what you meant. A check is something that can find an error
you did not expect.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below rest on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **plant** — to put something somewhere on purpose so it can be found later.
- **read back** — reading your own writing afterwards as if somebody else wrote it.
- **hunt** — to look for one specific thing rather than reading generally.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.

There are exactly four ways a beginner breaks *can* on paper, and they are all from
[part 2.1](2.1-can-with-the-plain-verb.md):

1. an *-s* on *can* or on the verb after it — *she cans cook*;
2. a *to* between them — *she can to cook*;
3. the apostrophe missing or misplaced — *cant*, *ca'nt*;
4. the question written in statement order, or with a full stop — *You can park here.*

Because there are only four, you can **hunt** for them rather than reading generally. That is the
real skill in this part, and it transfers: a read-back that hunts for three named things finds
them; a read-back that reads "carefully" finds nothing, however slowly it goes.

So you **plant** all four in your own writing, put it away, and come back to hunt.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing folder's check that can go **RED**. If you plant four and find three, the one
you missed is not a random miss — it is the error your eye is blind to, and it will appear in your
day 20 page and in every form after it. Finding out which of the four it is, once, is worth more
than being told to check carefully a hundred times.

## The rule

**Step one: plant.** Take the four pieces you wrote in
[part 2.2](2.2-write-a-request-and-a-rule.md) — two requests, two rules. Copy them out with
**exactly one of each error**, one error per piece. Change nothing else. Label the page
**deliberate** and write the four error names on a separate slip of paper, folded and put away.

> Can you to send me the form? I need it before Friday.
> Residents cans use the kitchen at any time.
> Guests cant cook after ten. There is a kettle in the hall.
> You can park behind the building.

**Step two: wait.** Do something else. The longer the better; the next day is best. A read-back
done immediately is reading, not checking.

**Step three: hunt, one pass per error.** Four passes, each looking for one thing only:

| Pass | Hunt for | Mark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | an *-s* on *can* or on the verb after it | RED |
| 2 | a *to* between *can* and the verb | RED |
| 3 | an apostrophe missing or in the wrong place | RED |
| 4 | a question in statement order, or ending in a full stop | RED |

One pass per error sounds slow and is in fact much faster than reading four times for
"mistakes", because each pass is looking for a shape your eye can actually catch.

**Step four: unfold the slip and mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Four found | GREEN. Your read-back works when it hunts. |
| Three found | RED. The one you missed is your blind spot. Write it at the top of your notebook page. |
| Fewer than three | RED. You read instead of hunting. Wait longer and do the four passes again, one at a time. |

**Step five: restore.** Write the four correct pieces out again in full, marked GREEN, under the
broken ones. Keep both. Beside the restored set, write one line naming the error you missed and
what you will hunt for first next time.

The correct forms were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[can and could](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/can-could):
"She **can** **speak** several languages"; "We make questions by putting the subject after *can*";
"The **negative** form is ***can't*** in spoken English and ***cannot*** in written English."

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is planting errors you would never make. If you have never once written *cans*, planting
it teaches you nothing — you will spot it instantly and score four out of four on a test that was
not about you. The fix: plant the four anyway the first time, and after that plant whichever three
have actually turned up in your own `docs/ERRORS.md` rows.

The second is hunting for all four in one pass. Your eye cannot hold four shapes at once; it holds
one. The fix: four passes, and say out loud which one you are on before you start it.

The third is fixing as you go. You find the *to*, you correct it, and then you are reading a
corrected page for the next error, which changes what you are looking at. The fix: mark, do not
fix. Fixing happens in step five, after all four passes.

## In real life

Nobody plants errors in a real document. What they do is read back before sending — and the whole
point of this part is that reading back only works when it knows what it is looking for. A writer
who knows their own three habitual errors checks a page in three quick passes and catches more
than somebody reading it four times slowly.

Fluent writers keep a very short private list. Not a grammar book — three or four things they
personally get wrong, which for most people never changes across their whole life. That list is
what a read-back is for, and building yours is what today's missed error starts.

What a formal reader does with a form containing *cans* and *can to* is not correct it. They form
a view of the whole application, and the view is formed before they finish the first line. That is
why the read-back happens before sending and not after, and why hunting beats reading.
