# The shortest conversation you'll have all day

`FN-04` · Day 19

## The one-line answer

A counter exchange is the shortest conversation in English and the one with the most fixed steps,
which is exactly why it can be made automatic — and why dreading it is a waste of perfectly good
fear.

## The model

Stand near a counter and listen to twelve people in a row. You will hear the same dance, over and
over, with the same steps in the same order. Someone opens. You ask. They check or confirm. You
settle it. You leave.

Both people already know the steps. That is the whole secret of the thing. Nobody at a counter is
improvising — they are leading and following a routine they have both done thousands of times.
You are not being tested. You are being partnered.

So the work today is not vocabulary and it is not courage. It is learning the four steps you lead
with, so well that your feet find them without you.

**Step one is the request.** The most useful opener in spoken English at a counter:

> Could I get a coffee, please?
> Could I get one of those, and a bag as well?

*Could I get* is not more polite than *I want* — it is more **ordinary**. That matters more.
Politeness at a counter is carried by tone and volume, not by length. A long, careful, beautifully
constructed request marks you out as a visitor; the short ordinary one does not.

**Step two is the availability check**, for when you do not know whether the thing exists here:

> Have you got any of the small ones?
> Do you do refills?

Notice the difference between those two. *Have you got any …* asks about stock, right now, on this
shelf. *Do you do …* asks about the whole shop — is this a place that offers that at all. Learners
collapse them into one and get answered at cross purposes.

**Step three is the decision**, said out loud, once you can see what is there:

> Right — I'll take the blue one.
> I'm after something for a long journey. What would you recommend?

*I'm after …* is the one to steal today. It lets you say what you came in for **before** you know
what it is called or whether they have it, which is precisely the moment you currently go silent.

**Step four is permission**, for the small things that are not purchases at all:

> Is it alright if I leave this here?
> Is it alright if I pay on card?

Now the part nobody warns you about. Learners rehearse the order and get ambushed by the question
that comes **back** — for here or to take away, do you want the receipt, anything else. You had
prepared a speech; you were asked a question. When that happens today, reach for what you already
have: *Sorry, I missed that last bit* from Day 3 does the whole job. The deeper repair moves for a
transaction going sideways are Day 24's business, and you will get them there.

**Today's phrases** — all of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Could I get …, please? | The default order, in any shop, for anything |
| Have you got any …? | Checking it exists here before you commit to wanting it |
| Do you do …? | Asking whether this place offers that at all |
| I'll take … | Deciding out loud, once you can see the options |
| I'm after … | Naming what you came in for before you know its name |
| Is it alright if I …? | Permission for a small thing that is not a purchase |
| Just a …, please. | The smallest possible order, with nothing extra attached |
| What would you recommend? | Handing the choice back when you genuinely cannot decide |

## Say it now

Say each three times, filling in your own content. Do not write anything down.

1. *Could I get {{…}}, please? And {{…}} as well.*
2. *Have you got any {{…}}? … Right, I'll take {{…}}.*
3. *Do you do {{…}}?*
4. *I'm after {{…}} — what would you recommend?*
5. *Is it alright if I {{…}}?*
6. *Just a {{…}}, please.*

Then run all six as one unbroken visit to one counter, twice, without stopping between them. Real
counters do not pause while you reload. The joins are the exercise.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d19-free.m4a`.**

Three counters, one file, no stopping between them.

> **One — somewhere you buy food or drink.** Walk in, ask, decide, add one extra thing at the end.
> **Two — somewhere you buy an object** you can hold. You do not know what it is called. Say what
> you are after anyway, and let them find it for you.
> **Three — somewhere you are asking for something that is not a purchase.** A form, a table by
> the window, permission to leave a bag.

Play both sides out loud. Do the other person's lines too — badly, quickly, in a flat voice. The
point is not to act; the point is that your own next line has to arrive on top of somebody else's,
which is the thing that never happens when you rehearse alone.

- **Solo form:** as written, both voices.
- **With a partner:** they take the counter. They are allowed to say *no* twice, and they may ask
  one question back that you did not expect. That is all the difficulty you need.

## The deliberate failure

Record the first counter again, but every single thing you say must be a **complete, formal
sentence with no contractions**. *Excuse me, I would like to purchase one coffee. I would also
like to have one bag, if that is possible. Thank you very much for your assistance.*

**What breaking sounds like:** the gap before you start gets longer each time, because each
sentence has to be built before it can be launched. The words themselves are faultless. But you
will hear yourself run out of breath in the middle of a request for a coffee, and you will hear
the little rushed patch at the end where you try to make up the time.

Then do it again with the short frames restored. Listen for where the effort went. Nothing got
less polite — the politeness moved out of the grammar and into your voice, where it belongs, and
your mouth got its capacity back.

## When it breaks in the wild

You have the order ready. You deliver it cleanly. And then the person behind the counter says
something quick — five words, half of them run together — and asks you a question you had not
budgeted for. You panic and say *yes*. You now own something you did not want, and the exchange
that was going well ends with you walking away annoyed with yourself.

Saying yes to unheard questions is the single most expensive habit at a counter, and it is
entirely a confidence problem, not a listening one.

**The repair:**

> Sorry, I missed that last bit — could I get the first one?

Day 3's phrase, doing exactly what it was built for, plus today's frame to close. You have not
apologised for existing and you have not lost the thread.

Play back `audio/d19-free.m4a` once. Pass if:

- [ ] Every ask in all three counters starts with one of today's frames, not with a bare noun —
      and you can name which frame each one used.
- [ ] In the second counter, you said what you were after **before** you named the object. If you

The second condition is the day's real target. *Have you got any …* and *I'm after …* exist to be
said while you still do not know the word — they buy the other person time to guess for you.
That is the whole trick, and it works in a shop long before it works anywhere else.
