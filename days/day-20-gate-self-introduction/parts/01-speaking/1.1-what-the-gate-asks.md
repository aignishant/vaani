## One-line answer

The gate is one recording of at least ten sentences introducing yourself, and it passes when somebody who has never met you can follow it once, without replaying anything.

## The story

You are in a waiting room and the person beside you turns and says, "So what brings you here?"
You have nineteen days of practice behind you. You have said your name aloud, you know where the
stress goes in your street's name, you can place a thing in a room, you know how *can* shrinks.

And you have never once put all of it into a single stretch of speech with nobody stopping you.

That is what today is. Not a new thing to learn — a first time doing all of it at once, with the
recorder running and nobody to help.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate** — a check at the end of a phase that the work has to pass before the phase is finished.
- **a stranger** — somebody who has never met you, does not know your language, and cannot ask
  you anything.
- **follow** — understand as it goes, without stopping or replaying.
- **turn** — one person's stretch of speaking before the other person speaks.

**This part teaches you nothing new.** That is deliberate. A gate that teaches is not a gate — it
is another lesson wearing the last day's number. Everything you need was taught between day 1 and
day 19, and this part's only job is to say what the gate asks and what each word of it means.

The gate's exact wording is in the plan's
[§7](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) and it is not repeated more kindly here.
For the speaking half it asks for **a recorded self-introduction of at least ten sentences that a
stranger can follow.** Four things in that sentence do work.

**Recorded.** It exists as a file. Not said to the mirror, not said well yesterday.

**Self-introduction.** About you. Not a description of a room, not an opinion.

**At least ten sentences.** Ten separate sentences, each with a subject and a verb. Not ten
phrases, and not five sentences joined by *and* four times.

**That a stranger can follow.** This is the pass condition and the only one that can fail in an
interesting way. It is not "correct". It is not "fluent". It is whether a person with no
information about you gets to the end still knowing who is being talked about.

## Why Vaani needs it

This recording is the first piece of your Speaking Portfolio, and it is the piece everything else
is measured against. On day 120 you will listen to it beside the last one. That comparison is the
whole point of keeping it, which is also why a take that is not really yours is worth nothing:

**What "a stranger can follow" is tested by.** Three questions, and they are the only three. A
listener who has never met you should be able to answer all three after one listen:

| The stranger's question | Where in your ten sentences it is answered |
| --- | --- |
| Who is this, and where are they? | |
| What do they do with their days? | |
| What is one thing I could ask them about? | |

The third is the one people fail. Nine facts and nothing to ask about is a form read aloud, not an
introduction. The model line for what an opening turn sounds like was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Meeting new people](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/meeting-new-people):
"Hi, I'm Yuna"; "Nice to meet you"; "So how was your journey?" — the last one being a question
handed back.

**The order.** Big to small, same as anything a listener has to build: who you are, where you are,
what you do, then the smaller things. A recording that opens on a detail makes the stranger build
the wrong person and they do not rebuild.

**The one rule about length.** Ten sentences is a floor, not a target. Twelve honest sentences beat
ten padded ones, and eleven sentences where the eleventh is a question handed back is better than
both.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the joined sentence, counted as several:

> I'm Ravi and I live in Pune and I work in a shop and I have two sisters.

That is one sentence with three *and*s. Counted honestly it is one, and a stranger hears it as one
long thing with no shape. The fix: four full stops.

The second is the list with nobody in it:

> Name Ravi. Twenty-six. Pune. Shop worker. Two sisters.

Every fact is present and there is no person. A stranger cannot follow this because there is
nothing to follow — it does not move. The fix: each fact becomes a sentence with a verb in it.

The third is the perfect take that is not yours:

> Good morning. My name is Ravi and I am pleased to introduce myself to you today.

Read off a page, in sentences you would never say. It will pass the count and fail the comparison
on day 120, because there is nothing in it to compare. The fix: say it with the paper turned over.

## In real life

Nobody ever asks for ten sentences about yourself. What real life asks for is thirty seconds of
the same thing, delivered in a doorway, and then the other person talks. So the real skill this
gate builds is not the long turn; it is having the material ready so that the short turn is easy.

Fluent speakers keep a shorter and a longer version and choose by the situation — three sentences
at a counter, ten at an interview, two in a lift. They are the same material, cut differently. If
you can do ten, the three are free.

What a stranger notices is whether you handed them anything. An introduction that ends on a fact
closes the conversation; one that ends on something askable — a job with a story in it, a place
they might know, a question back — opens it. That is the whole difference between being introduced
and being met.
