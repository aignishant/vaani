## One-line answer

Record the whole thing in one go without stopping, keep it, listen once, and retake only for a reason you can name in a sentence.

## The story

You sit down to record. Three sentences in, you say *my* when you meant *me*, and you stop and
start again. Four sentences in, the door goes. You start again. Six sentences in, you hear
yourself sounding flat and you start again.

An hour later there are nine unfinished takes on your phone and nothing in your portfolio. Each
restart was reasonable. Together they made a rule that guaranteed failure: **nothing may have
anything wrong with it.**

The first take of a ten-sentence introduction by somebody who has been learning for nineteen days
has things wrong with it. That is not a reason to stop the recorder. It is the recording.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **take** — one recording attempt, from start to stop.
- **retake** — doing the whole thing again, on purpose, for a named reason.
- **repair** — saying a line again, correctly, straight after getting it wrong, without stopping.
  From [day 1, part 1.3](../../../day-01-short-vowels/parts/01-speaking/1.3-change-one-sound.md).
- **keep** — save the file where you can find it again, with a name you will recognise.

**This part teaches nothing new either.** It is about the one thing nineteen days of short reps
have not asked of you: going all the way to the end without stopping.

The rule is simple and it is the opposite of what you will want to do. **You do not stop the
recorder mid-take.** If you get a word wrong, you repair it out loud — say it again, correctly,
and keep going. If the door goes, keep going. If a sentence comes out badly, say the next one.

The reason is not that mistakes do not matter. It is that stopping trains you to stop, and the
thing this gate is actually testing is whether you can hold a turn. A stranger in a waiting room
will not wait while you start again.

## Why Vaani needs it

This take goes in the Speaking Portfolio and gets a row in `docs/RECORDINGS.md`. It is the
baseline for a hundred more days — and a baseline made of a heavily retaken, carefully polished
take is a baseline that flatters you and tells you nothing later. Day 120's comparison is only
worth having if today's file is honest.

## The rule

**Before you start.**

- Your ten numbered sentences on paper from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-the-gate-asks.md), **turned face down**.
- Somewhere quiet enough that you can hear yourself.
- Your phone's voice recorder, with enough space on it.

**The take, in four rules.**

1. **One go, no stopping.** Start the recorder and do not touch it until you have finished
   sentence ten.
2. **Repair out loud, do not restart.** Wrong word? Say the sentence again and carry on. The
   repair is allowed to be in the recording. It is what a real speaker does.
3. **Paper stays face down.** Glance if you must, but do not read. A read take fails the
   comparison later, whatever it sounds like now.
4. **Finish even if it is going badly.** A bad complete take is a take. A good half take is
   nothing.

**Listen once. Then decide.**

Play it back one time, all the way through, as the stranger. Then answer the three questions from
part 1.1 in writing: who is this and where are they, what do they do with their days, what could
I ask them about?

**When to retake.** Only for a reason you can write in one sentence. Write the reason down before
you press record again.

| Reason | Retake? |
| --- | --- |
| A stranger could not answer one of the three questions. | Yes. |
| It is fewer than ten sentences. | Yes. |
| You cannot hear the words — noise, distance, wind. | Yes. |
| You said something that is not true. | Yes. |
| You do not like your own voice. | No. |
| A few words are wrong. | No. |
| It is not as good as you imagined it. | No. |

**Three takes, maximum.** If the third take has the same problem as the first, the problem is in
the ten sentences, not in the speaking. Go back to the paper and change the sentences.

**Keep it.** Give the file a name you will recognise in a year — the day number and the date —
and put a copy in `lab/speaking/` if your phone lets you. Then fill in its row in
`docs/RECORDINGS.md` from the hub.

The shape of an opening turn was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Meeting new people](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/meeting-new-people)
— "Hi, I'm Yuna"; "Nice to meet you"; "It's great to be here!" — and the phrases for when you lose
the thread mid-turn at
[Checking understanding](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding):
"I'm sorry?"; "Could you say that again, please?"

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the nine unfinished takes in the story. Nothing exists at the
end of it, and the honest `docs/PROGRESS.md` row for that day says the gate was not attempted.
The fix is mechanical: press record, and do not touch the phone again until sentence ten.

The second is the take that gets shorter each time:

> Take one: ten sentences, a bit messy. Take four: six sentences, very clean.

Each retake quietly removes the sentence that went wrong last time. By take four the introduction
is short, tidy and says almost nothing. The fix: count the sentences on every take, out loud,
before deciding anything.

The third is the silent restart — stopping the recorder, starting again, and telling yourself it
was one take. It is not a grammar mistake; it is a record that is not true, and the ledger stops
being worth keeping the first time something untrue goes into it. The fix: if you restarted, write
that you restarted.

## In real life

A real turn is never retaken. You get one, in a doorway, at a counter, on a call, and whatever
comes out is what the other person has. Everything about this part — the repair instead of the
restart, finishing the bad take, not reading — is practice for the fact that real speech has no
second attempt.

Fluent speakers repair constantly and nobody notices. Listen to any unscripted conversation and
it is full of half-started sentences and corrections. What marks a beginner is not the repair; it
is stopping dead after the mistake and waiting for permission to continue.

What a stranger remembers from an introduction is almost never the words. It is whether the person
got to the end. Somebody who finishes their turn, mistakes and all, reads as somebody at ease.
Somebody who stops and starts again twice reads as somebody who would rather not be talking, and
strangers politely help by ending the conversation.
