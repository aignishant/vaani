## One-line answer

Record a second introduction broken in the three ways introductions actually fail, then answer the stranger's three questions from it — and whichever failure you cannot detect in your own voice is the one already in your real take.

## The story

Three people record an introduction. All three are correct English.

The first says ten sentences joined with *and*, in one breath, at speed. The stranger gets to the
end and cannot say what the person does.

The second says ten short facts with no verbs — name, age, city, job — and the stranger gets to
the end knowing everything and having nothing to ask.

The third says ten good sentences starting with the fact that their cousin lives in Dubai, and the
stranger spends the rest of the recording trying to work out whose introduction this is.

None of them got anything wrong. Each failed one of the three ways, and not one of them could
hear it in their own recording.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below rest on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **detect** — to notice that something is there.
- **blind spot** — the failure you cannot see in your own work, however many times you check.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **baseline** — the first recording, kept so that later ones can be measured against it.

There are three ways a ten-sentence introduction fails while being perfectly correct:

1. **The run-on.** Everything joined, no full stops, no shape. The stranger cannot separate the
   facts.
2. **The list.** Facts with no verbs and no person. The stranger has data and nothing to ask.
3. **The wrong order.** A detail first, so the stranger builds the wrong person and never rebuilds.

You cannot hear these in your own take, because you know what you meant. So you record them **on
purpose**, one after another, and then test each one the same way you tested your real take — by
answering the stranger's three questions from it.

The finding is not "the broken ones are worse". It is **which broken one you could not detect**.
That failure is already in your real take, quietly, and now you know its name.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking folder's check that can go **RED**, on the day when a check going red matters
most. A gate is not passed by recording something; it is passed by a recording that survives being
tested. A gate day with no way to fail would be a ceremony.

## The rule

**Step one: record the three broken versions.** Same ten facts as your real take each time. Label
each one **deliberate**.

**A — the run-on.** Every sentence joined with *and*, said fast, one breath where you can:

> I'm Ravi and I live in Pune and I work in a shop near the station and I've got two sisters and …

**B — the list.** Facts with the verbs taken out:

> Ravi. Pune. Shop near the station. Two sisters. Cricket. Mornings, early.

**C — the wrong order.** Start with the smallest, most particular detail and work outwards:

> My cousin's been in Dubai since March. There's a tea stall at the end of our lane. I'm Ravi.

**Step two: test all four.** Your real take from
[part 1.2](1.2-record-the-introduction.md), and the three broken ones. For each, listen once and
answer the stranger's three questions in writing.

| | Who and where? | What do they do? | What could I ask about? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Real take | | | |
| A — run-on | | | |
| B — list | | | |
| C — wrong order | | | |

**Step three: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| All three broken takes fail at least one question | GREEN. You can detect all three failures. |
| One broken take answers all three questions | RED. That failure is invisible to you. Name it and write it at the top of your notebook page. |
| Your real take fails a question | RED, and the most useful result of the day. Retake it, fixing that one thing only. |

**Step four: the one-line finding, and the repair.** Write one sentence naming which failure you
could not detect. Then record the repair: take one sentence from your real take and say it twice
— once in that failing style, once properly — so the pair sits side by side in your ear.

**Do not delete the broken takes.** They go nowhere near the portfolio, but they are the evidence
for what you learned today, and in three weeks they will be more convincing than any note.

**What the portfolio gets.** Only the real take from part 1.2, with its row in
`docs/RECORDINGS.md`. If step three sent you back for a retake, the portfolio gets the retake and
the row says so.

The phrases a listener uses when they have lost the thread were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Checking understanding](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding):
"I'm sorry?"; "I don't understand"; "Can you repeat that more slowly, please?" If a stranger would
have said one of those during your real take, you know where.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is making the broken versions too broken. If version B is three words long, it fails for
being short rather than for being a list, and you have tested nothing. The fix: every broken take
carries **the same ten facts** as the real one. Only the shape changes.

The second is answering the three questions from what you know rather than from the recording.
This is the same trap as every read-back and it is stronger here, because the subject is you. The
fix: write beside each answer **which sentence gave it to you**. If you cannot name the sentence,
the answer came from your own head and the box is empty.

The third is treating a RED on your real take as a bad day. It is the opposite — it is the check
doing precisely the job it exists for, one day before the phase closes rather than on day 119.
A gate that goes red is a gate working. The fix is a retake, not a rethink of the whole nineteen
days.

## In real life

Real listeners never tell you which of the three you did. They tell you nothing at all, and the
conversation is either easy or it quietly ends. The three failures are simply the three most
common reasons somebody decides, without noticing they decided, that talking to you is work.

Fluent speakers do all three deliberately, in the right place. They run on when they are excited,
they list when somebody asked for facts, and they start with the small odd detail when they are
telling a story. What they never do is use one of them for an introduction to somebody who does
not know them yet.

What a stranger does when they cannot follow an introduction is not ask. They nod, wait for the
end, and ask one narrow question about the only thing they caught. If you have ever ended an
introduction and been asked about the least interesting thing you said, that is what happened, and
it was not their fault.
