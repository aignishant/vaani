## One-line answer

The gate page is ten sentences about you, each with a capital letter at the start and a full stop at the end, and the counting is done with a pencil because a page that feels like ten sentences is usually seven.

## The story

An application is handed in with a box at the bottom marked *Tell us about yourself*. The applicant
has filled it carefully:

> I am Ravi I live in Pune with my family and I work in a shop near the station I have been there
> for two years and I like the work.

It is honest, it is true, and the person reading it has read it twice, because there is nowhere to
stop. Two sentences' worth of punctuation is doing the work of five sentences of content, and the
reader has to insert the full stops themselves while reading.

They will not say anything about it. They will simply find this application slightly harder than
the one above it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate** — a check at the end of a phase that the work has to pass before the phase is finished.
- **count** — to go through with a pencil and mark, rather than to estimate by looking.
- **full stop** — the mark . at the end of a sentence. From
  [day 3, part 2.1](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md).
- **run-on** — two or more sentences written as one, with no full stop between them.

**This part teaches you nothing new.** Everything in the gate page was taught between day 1 and
day 19. A gate that teaches is not a gate. This part's only job is to say what the page must
carry and how each piece is checked.

The gate's exact wording is in the plan's
[§7](../../../../docs/00_MASTER_PLAN.md#7--the-phases) and is not repeated more kindly here. For
the writing half it asks for **a ten-sentence page about yourself with a capital letter and a full
stop on every sentence.**

Three things in that do work, and all three are countable — which is unusual and worth using. You
do not have to judge this page. You can **count** it.

**The three counts.** Do these with a pencil, on paper, marking as you go. Not by reading.

| Count | How | Passes when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sentences | Put a number above the first word of each sentence. | You reach at least 10. |
| Capital letters | Circle the first letter of each numbered sentence. | Every circle is a capital. |
| Full stops | Circle the mark at the end of each numbered sentence. | Every circle is a `.`, `?` or `!` |

**What counts as a sentence.** One subject, one verb, one idea. Two facts joined by *and* are two
sentences pretending to be one:

| One sentence | Two sentences |
| --- | --- |
| I live in Pune. | I live in Pune and I work in a shop. |

If you cannot number it without arguing with yourself, it is a **run-on**, and the fix is a full
stop where you paused while reading it.

**What goes in the ten.** The page is about you. Big to small, the same order as any description:

```text
   1–2   who you are, where you are
   3–5   what you do with your days
   6–8   the people, the place, the things around you
   9–10  one thing you like, one thing you can do — with a reason
```

This is a shape, not a form to fill in. Your ten do not have to be these ten.

The model and its tips were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Online course introductions](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/online-course-introductions),
which advises keeping an introduction "short and clear" and giving "a little bit of information
about yourself", and at
[A personal profile](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/personal-profile),
whose tips include "Don't include every single thing. Choose a few points and focus on them",
"Check your spelling and punctuation before you post", and "Don't add your contact details (e.g.
address, phone number). You could get unwanted messages."

**That last one matters for this page.** It is committed to the repository. No phone number, no
full street address, no document number.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the run-on in the story:

> I am Ravi I live in Pune with my family and I work in a shop near the station …

Nothing is misspelled and the count comes out at three. The fix: read it aloud, and put a full
stop wherever your voice stopped.

The second is the page that counts to ten with padding:

> I am Ravi. I am from India. I am from Pune. Pune is in India. I live in Pune.

Five sentences, one fact. The count passes and a reader learns nothing. The fix: one true and
**different** fact per sentence, which is day 7's rule.

The third is the lower-case start after a run of short sentences:

> I work in a shop. it is near the station.

The eye stops noticing capitals when the sentences are short. This is exactly why the count is
done by circling rather than by reading. The fix: the circles.

## In real life

A real reader of a page about you is deciding something small and quick — whether to reply,
whether to call, whether to read the next paragraph. Punctuation is not a politeness to them; it
is what lets them read at speed. A page with no full stops is read more slowly, and slower reading
means fewer sentences reached before they stop.

Fluent writers vary sentence length deliberately: a long one, then a short one. Ten identical
short sentences read as a form, and ten identical long ones read as a wall. But that is a
refinement, and it is not what this gate asks for. What the gate asks for is ten complete
sentences, correctly closed, which is the thing everything else is built on.

What a formal reader does not forgive is a missing full stop at the end of the last sentence. It
is the commonest error on any written page in any language, because by then the writer has
finished and gone. The last full stop is the one to check first.
