## One-line answer

Write the ten lines first and the page second, because a page written straight through is a page you cannot count, and the counting is the only part of this that cannot be argued with.

## The story

Two people fill in the same box on the same form.

The first writes straight into the box, carefully, thinking about how it sounds. It comes out as
a smooth paragraph, and when they count it afterwards there are seven sentences, two of which say
the same thing.

The second writes ten numbered lines on a separate sheet first, one fact each, then copies them
into the box and joins nothing. It takes longer and reads slightly stiffly, and it is finished,
and it passes.

Writing the lines first is not a trick for beginners. It is how you write something that has to
meet a count, at any level, in any language.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **draft** — a first version, written to be changed.
- **fair copy** — the clean final version, written out again properly.
- **count** — to go through with a pencil and mark, rather than to estimate by looking.
- **commit** — to save the page into the repository, where it stays.

**Nothing here is new.** This part is the doing, not the teaching.

The method is two stages, and the order is the whole point.

**Stage one: ten numbered lines.** One fact each. Ugly is fine. Stiff is fine. They are numbered,
so the count is not a judgement — it is a number on the page.

**Stage two: the fair copy.** The ten lines written out as a page: one block, in order, each
sentence with its capital and its full stop, nothing joined.

Then the three counts from [part 2.1](2.1-what-the-page-must-carry.md), done with a pencil.

The reason for the two stages is that the counting has to happen on something countable. A page
written straight through hides its own structure from you — you read it, it feels like plenty, and
it is seven sentences with two repeats. The numbered lines cannot hide that.

## Why Vaani needs it

This page is committed to the repository and indexed in `docs/WRITINGS.md`. That means it will be

## The rule

**Stage one: the ten lines.** On rough paper. Numbered 1 to 10, one true fact each, no *and*
joining two facts, no two lines carrying the same fact.

```text
   1.  I'm Ravi.
   2.  I live in Pune, in the east of the city.
   3.  I work in a shop near the station.
   4.  …
```

Use the shape from part 2.1 if it helps — who and where, then what you do, then who is around
you, then one thing you like and one thing you can do — but your ten are yours.

**Stage two: the fair copy.** Write them out as one page. Not a numbered list — a page somebody
would read.

> I'm Ravi. I live in Pune, in the east of the city. I work in a shop near the station. I've been
> there for two years. …

**Stage three: the three counts.** With a pencil, on the fair copy. Number each sentence above its
first word. Circle each first letter. Circle each final mark.

| Count | Passes when | If it fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sentences | at least 10 | Go back to stage one and add a line. Do not split a sentence to make two. |
| Capitals | every circle is a capital | Fix on the page. Then check the ones after a short sentence again. |
| Full stops | every circle is `.`, `?` or `!` | Check the last sentence first. It is the one that is missing. |

**Stage four: the truth pass.** One more read, looking for one thing only: **is every sentence
true today?** Not true last year, not nearly true. A portfolio piece that is not true is worth
nothing to compare against, and this is the only check nobody else can do for you.

**What not to put on it.** No phone number, no full street address, no document number. The page
is committed to the repository. This was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[A personal profile](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/personal-profile):
"Don't add your contact details (e.g. address, phone number). You could get unwanted messages."
The same page says "Check your spelling and punctuation before you post", and
[Online course introductions](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/online-course-introductions),
checked the same day, advises keeping it "short and clear" with "a little bit of information about
yourself".

**Then file it.** `lab/writing/day-20.md`, or a photo of the notebook page named `day-20.jpg`, and
its row in `docs/WRITINGS.md` from the hub.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is skipping stage one:

> writing straight into the fair copy because the ten lines feel like extra work

They are not extra work; they are the work. The page that comes out is the seven-sentence one in
the story, and it takes longer to repair than it would have taken to plan. The fix: rough paper,
numbers, one fact each.

The second is splitting a sentence to reach ten:

> I live in Pune. It is in the east of the city.

That is not dishonest, but done four times it turns a page into filler and the reader notices the
rhythm before they notice anything else. The fix: add a real fact instead.

The third is the count done by eye:

> "That's about ten."

It is seven. Every time, for everybody. The fix: the pencil. The whole reason this gate is
countable is so that it does not need your judgement.

The fourth is a sentence that was true when you learned to say it and is not true now — a job you
left, a course you finished. The fix: the truth pass, and change it.

## In real life

Real writing that must meet a count — a form, an application, a profile, a required word length —
is always written in two stages by people who do it often. Draft ugly, count, then make it read
well. Beginners do it in one stage and then argue with the count, which is the slowest possible
route.

Fluent writers do a final pass for one thing only, and they choose the thing by knowing their own
weakness. Punctuation, or repetition, or a claim that is no longer true. One targeted pass finds
more than three general readings, which is the same lesson the hunting passes taught elsewhere in
this folder.

What a reader does with a page about you is form a picture and then stop reading. The last thing
they read is usually sentence three or four. So if there is one sentence you want them to have,
it goes near the top — and a page written from numbered lines lets you move it there, which a page
written straight through does not.
