## One-line answer

Copy your finished page with the full stops removed and two facts repeated, then run the three counts on it — the point is not that it fails, but exactly which number it collapses to.

## The story

The same ten sentences, written twice.

The first way:

> I am Ravi I live in Pune in the east of the city I work in a shop near the station I have been
> there for two years I like the work and the people are friendly.

The second way:

> I'm Ravi. I live in Pune, in the east of the city. I work in a shop near the station. I've been
> there for two years. I like the work. The people are friendly.

Count them. The first is one sentence. The second is six. Not one that is harder to read and one
that is easier — one that **fails the gate outright** and one that passes it, with exactly the
same words in exactly the same order.

That is worth seeing in your own handwriting, with your own facts, once.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **collapse** — to fall from a large number to a small one.
- **run-on** — two or more sentences written as one, with no full stop between them.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **evidence** — the thing you keep so that you can prove later what happened.

You have a page that passes ([part 2.2](2.2-write-the-page.md)). What you do not yet have is a
feeling for **how far** it is from failing, and that distance is much smaller than it looks.

So you break it in the two ways a real page is broken, neither of which changes a single word:

1. **Take out the full stops.** Join everything. Nothing is added, nothing is deleted.
2. **Repeat two facts.** Say the same thing twice in different words, which is what padding
   actually looks like when it happens by accident.

Then you run the same three counts. The sentence count **collapses** — usually from ten to two or
three — and the repeated facts survive the count entirely, which is the more interesting half:
the count cannot catch them, and a reader can.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing folder's check that can go **RED**, on the day it matters most. A gate you
cannot fail is a ceremony. And there is a second reason: the count is the only check this gate
has, and a check whose limits you have never tested is a check you will trust too far.

## The rule

**Step one: break it, two ways, no words changed.** Copy your fair copy with every full stop
removed and every sentence joined, and with two of your facts said a second time in different
words. Label the page **deliberate**.

> I'm Ravi I live in Pune in the east of the city my home is on the east side I work in a shop
> near the station the shop is close to the station I've been there for two years …

**Step two: run the three counts on it.** Pencil, same as before. Number each sentence, circle
each first letter, circle each final mark.

| Count | Your page | The broken page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sentences | | |
| Capitals that are capitals | | |
| Endings that are `.`, `?` or `!` | | |

**Step three: the repeat hunt.** The count will not find the repeated facts. So hunt for them
separately: read the broken page once looking only for **two lines that say the same thing**. Mark
them RED. This is a second, different check, and noticing that you needed one is the finding.

**Step four: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| The count collapsed to 3 or fewer, and you found both repeats | GREEN. Both checks work, and you know what each one can and cannot catch. |
| The count collapsed but you found only one repeat | RED. Your eye passes over repetition. Write that at the top of your notebook page. |
| The count did not collapse | RED. You left full stops in. Take them all out and count again. |

**Step five: restore, and file the right one.** Write the correct page out again in full, marked
**GREEN**, and keep the broken one beneath it as **evidence**. Only the correct page goes in
`lab/writing/day-20.md` and gets its row in `docs/WRITINGS.md`. The broken page stays in your
notebook and goes nowhere near the portfolio.

**Step six: the one-line finding.** One sentence: what number did your page collapse to, and did
you find both repeats? That sentence is the useful thing you made today.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is rewriting while breaking. You remove the full stops and, because the result reads
badly, you quietly reword a phrase to make it flow. Now you are testing your editing rather than
the punctuation. The fix: remove and join. Change no words except the two deliberate repeats.

The second is making the repeats obvious:

> I work in a shop. I work in a shop.

Nobody misses that, and nobody would ever write it. Real repetition is the same fact in different
clothes — *I live in the east of the city* and *my home is on the east side*. The fix: say the
fact again in words you would actually have used.

The third is skipping step three because the count already failed. The count failing is the boring
half. The repeats surviving the count is the half worth having, because it is the thing that will
still be true of your writing when your punctuation is perfect. The fix: do both checks, and write
down which one caught what.

## In real life

Nobody removes their own full stops. What happens is that they never put them in — writing fast,
on a phone, in a box on a form, in one breath — and the run-on is the natural output of a person
in a hurry. The correct page is the edited one, always. That is not a weakness; it is what the
second stage is for.

Fluent writers know that a count and a read are different checks and use both. A count catches
structure. A read catches repetition, untruth and anything that is boring. Neither finds the
other's problems, and confidence in one of them is exactly how a bad page gets sent.

What a reader does with a run-on page about you is finish it, understand it, and feel slightly
tired. They will not mention it. But a page that made them work is a page whose contents they
remember less well, and on an application the whole purpose was to be remembered.
