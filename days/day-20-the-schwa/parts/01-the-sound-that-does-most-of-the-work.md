# The sound that does most of the work

`PR-09` · Day 20

## The one-line answer

English has one small, colourless, lazy vowel — the *uh* in the second half of **doctor** — and it
sits in more syllables than any other sound, so giving every vowel its full value is what makes
careful English sound wrong.

## The model

That lazy *uh* has a name: the **schwa**. You already make it, constantly, without deciding to.
It is the first syllable of *about*, the second half of *doctor*, the vanished middle of
*chocolate*, the *a* in *a couple of*. It has no colour of its own. You cannot hold it, stretch
it, or sing it. It is the shortest, quietest vowel English has.

Think of a brick wall. The bricks are the stressed syllables — the words carrying your meaning,
the ones a listener would write down. The mortar is everything else: *a, of, the, to, for, and,
was, from, some, at, were*. Mortar is not decoration. A wall of bricks with no mortar between them
is not a stronger wall; it is a pile.

Here is what happens to the mortar in real speech:

> a couple of days → uh-CUP-luhv days
> one of the best → wun-uh-thuh-BEST
> a lot of the time → uh-LOT-uh-thuh-TIME

Say the right-hand versions. Notice that you did not slow down to fit the extra syllables in —
you squeezed them, and the stressed syllables stayed exactly where they were. **That is the
mechanism.** English keeps its stressed beats roughly evenly spaced, and whatever falls between
two beats gets compressed to fit. The small words are compressible because they carry almost no
information. The big ones are not.

This is why the schwa cannot be learned as a list of words. It is not a property of *of*. It is
what happens to *of* when it lands between two beats — and the same word, said alone or under
emphasis, gets its full vowel back.

Two things follow, and both are audible tonight.

**One: your careful voice is your least clear voice.** When you concentrate, you give every
syllable equal value, and equal value destroys the beat. A listener finds you by the beats. Take
those away and they have to process every syllable at the same weight, which is slower and harder
for them, not easier.

**Two: the schwa is where your speed comes from.** Not from moving your mouth faster. From
spending almost nothing on the small words, so the time is there for the ones that matter.

**Today's phrases** — every one is chosen because it is nearly all mortar. All go into
`docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| a couple of … | Two-ish, without committing to two |
| one of the … | Picking a single thing out of a group mid-sentence |
| sort of a … | Naming something you are not sure is quite the right name |
| for a bit | Duration with nothing measured |
| a lot of the time | Frequency, honest, no exaggeration |
| some of them | Splitting a group without listing it |
| the rest of it | The part you are not going to describe |
| another one of those | Pointing at a repeat, without naming the thing again |

## Say it now

Say each three times, filling in your own content. Do not write anything down. Keep the small
words short and quiet — you are not saying them well, you are getting them out of the way.

1. *I've got a couple of {{…}} to sort out.*
2. *That's one of the {{…}} I actually like.*
3. *It was sort of a {{…}}, in the end.*
4. *I'll be there for a bit, and then {{…}}.*
5. *A lot of the time I just {{…}}.*
6. *Some of them {{…}}, and the rest of it I {{…}}.*
7. *And then there was another one of those {{…}}.*

Then join all seven into one continuous stretch, twice, keeping the stressed words landing exactly
where they landed before and letting everything between them collapse.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d20-free.m4a`.**

> Talk about **a thing you own too many of** — books, cables, mugs, shoes, unfinished projects.
> Where they came from, which ones you actually use, what you keep meaning to do with the rest.

That subject is not an accident. It forces *a couple of*, *some of them*, *one of the*, *the rest
of it* and *another one of those* out of you naturally, and it lets you hear whether they arrive
squeezed or stamped out one syllable at a time.

- **Solo form:** as written, one take, no restarts.
- **With a partner:** they tap the table every time they hear a small word given its full value —
  *of* rhyming with *off*, *a* said as *ay*, *the* said as *thee*. You do not stop. You just hear
  the tap and keep going.

## The deliberate failure

Record a short stretch — a few sentences about anything — in which you give **every vowel its
dictionary value**. *Ay lot of the time* with *of* as *off*, *to* as *too*, *and* as *and*, *the*
as *thee*, every time.

**What breaking sounds like:** a pile of bricks. Every syllable the same size, the same weight,
the same length, so nothing binds and nothing stands out as important. You will also
hear it take noticeably longer to say the same thing, and you will hear your own pitch flatten
out, because there is nothing left to be high or low against.

Then say the same sentences again with the mortar restored. The words did not change. What
changed is that a shape appeared — and the shape is what a listener was using to follow you.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are on a call and someone asks you to repeat something. So you repeat it **more carefully**:
slower, each word separated, every vowel fully pronounced. And they ask again.

That is the trap closing. Careful speech removes exactly the rhythm the listener was using to
segment your sentence into words, so the second attempt is harder to understand than the first.
The fix is counter-intuitive and it works: keep the small words small, and instead put the extra
effort into the one or two words that actually carry the answer.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — it was one of the **blue** ones, on the **second** shelf.

Same speed as before. Mortar untouched. All the new effort spent on two stressed words, which are
the only two the other person was missing.
