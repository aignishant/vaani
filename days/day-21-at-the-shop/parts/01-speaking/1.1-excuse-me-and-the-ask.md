## One-line answer

Getting served in English is three moves, not one: get their attention, ask with *can* or *could* and *please*, and close with *thanks* — and the politeness lives in the shape, not in extra words.

## The story

You are in a shop holding a jumper in the wrong size. The assistant is folding things two steps
away. You wait. She does not look up. You wait some more, then step closer and say:

> This one black?

She looks up, and there is a small pause before she answers — the pause of somebody deciding how
to take that. She is perfectly pleasant about it. But you have spent the whole exchange feeling
that you were slightly in the way, and you were not: you had simply skipped the first move and
half of the second.

Two extra words — *Excuse me* and *please* — and nothing about the exchange would have felt like
that.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **assistant** — the person working in the shop who helps customers.
- **attention** — somebody noticing you and being ready to listen.
- **polite** — said in a way that treats the other person as a person, not a machine.
- **jumper** — a warm knitted top, worn over a shirt.
- **close** — the last thing you say, which ends the exchange cleanly.

In English, being served is a small piece of theatre with three moves, and beginners usually do
only the middle one.

**Move one: get attention.** *Excuse me.* You do not start asking until the other person is
listening. Walking up and beginning mid-sentence is what makes an exchange feel abrupt, and it is
the move most often skipped, because in many languages you simply start.

**Move two: the ask.** *Can I have…?* or *Could you…?* plus *please*. The question shape does the
work — subject after *can*, voice rising at the end, from
[day 19, part 1.2](../../../day-19-can-and-cant/parts/01-speaking/1.2-say-what-you-can-do.md).

**Move three: close it.** *Thanks.* Or *thank you*, or *that's great, thanks*. An exchange with no
close is left hanging, and the other person does not know whether you are finished.

That is the whole of it. English politeness at a counter is not long sentences — it is these three
moves in this order, said quickly.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a recorded shop or café role-play, both voices yours, of at least twelve
turns. Twelve turns is only possible if each exchange has a shape; three moves per ask is what
generates turns. An ask with no attention move and no close is one turn and then silence.

## The rule

**The three moves, with real lines.** These were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Shopping for clothes](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/shopping-clothes).

| Move | What you say | From the checked page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| attention | Excuse me? | "Excuse me?" / "Excuse me again." |
| the ask | Do you have this in black? | "Do you have this jumper in black?" |
| the ask | Can I have a bag, please? | "Can I pay by credit card?" |
| close | Thanks. | "Thank you." |

Two more from the same page, both worth having ready because they are the two questions every shop
answers: "Where are the changing rooms?" and "How much is this?" And the assistant's own lines,
which you need to recognise: "Hello, can I help you?" and "Would you like a bag for that?"

**What to say when you are not ready.** The most useful sentence in any shop, and it is a complete
answer on its own:

> I'm just looking, thanks.

**Can or could.** Both work. *Could* is the softer one. The page
[can and could](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/can-could),
checked the same day, says *can* is "**less polite**" for a request, and gives "**Can you take** a
message, please?" as the model.

| Situation | Reach for |
| --- | --- |
| a friend, a café you go to every day | Can I have…? |
| a stranger, a busy counter, somebody doing you a favour | Could you…? |
| somebody has already said no once | Could you…? and a reason |

**Where *please* goes.** At the end, after a small pause, or straight after the question word. Not
in the middle of the verb:

> Can I have a bag, please?
> Could you check the back, please?

**The tune.** The question rises at the end. A flat *can I have a bag* is not a question, it is an
instruction, and the *please* on the end will not rescue it. The rise is doing almost all of the
politeness — which is why short polite English works, and why long impolite English does not.

```text
   Excuse me?      Can I have a bag, please?      Thanks.
        ╱                                 ╱            ╲
   attention            the ask, rising          the close, falling
```

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the ask with no attention move:

> This one black?

The assistant answers it and the exchange starts on the back foot. Nothing is rude in the words,
and an English-speaking listener registers it as slightly abrupt without being able to say why.
The fix: *Excuse me* first, then wait half a beat for them to look up.

The second is the flat request:

> Can I have a bag please.

No rise. It lands as somebody telling the assistant to fetch a bag. The fix: take the last word
up.

The third is the missing close. You get your answer and turn away, and the assistant is left
mid-exchange. The fix is one syllable: *thanks*.

The fourth, which comes from trying too hard, is politeness by length:

> Excuse me, sorry, I am very sorry to disturb you, I would like to ask if it is possible…

By the third clause the assistant does not know what you want. The fix: three moves, short.

## In real life

Shop English is fast and reduced. *Can I have* comes out as something close to *c'n I 'ave*, and
*Excuse me* as *scuse me*. Nobody is being casual at you; that is simply what those phrases sound
like at speed. Expecting the full forms is one reason a real counter feels harder than a lesson.

Under pressure — a queue, a phone ringing, somebody serving two people at once — the moves get
shorter but none of them disappears. A regular customer says *scuse me — bag? — ta*. Still three
moves. That is the structure surviving with all the words stripped off it, which tells you what
was load-bearing.

What a native listener notices is the missing attention move and the missing close, not your
grammar. A request with both, and every word after it wrong, reads as a polite person struggling
with English. A perfect sentence with neither reads as somebody who does not much care, and people
serve those two customers differently.
