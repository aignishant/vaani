## One-line answer

A real shop exchange is never one question — it is your ask, their answer, and then your second move, and the second move is the one nobody practises.

## The story

You go in for a phone charger. You do all three moves perfectly:

> Excuse me — do you have a charger for this phone, please?

And she says:

> We've got these two. Which end do you need?

And you stop. You had planned the question. You had not planned that the answer would be another
question, and now there is a silence, and she is holding two chargers and waiting.

The ask was never the hard part. The hard part is the turn after the answer, and that is the turn
a lesson almost never gives you.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **exchange** — the whole back-and-forth between two people, not just one question.
- **turn** — one person's stretch of speaking before the other speaks.
- **second move** — what you say after they have answered you.
- **charger** — the cable and plug that fill a phone's battery.

[Part 1.1](1.1-excuse-me-and-the-ask.md) gave you the three moves for a single ask. A real shop
does not stop there, because the assistant's answer is usually one of four things, and each one
needs a different **second move**:

| They answer with | Your second move |
| --- | --- |
| **yes**, here it is | close, and pay or leave |
| **no**, we haven't got it | ask what they do have |
| **a question** back — what size, which one, how many | answer it, briefly |
| **something you did not catch** | ask them to repeat, without apologising three times |

That is the whole part. Four answers, four moves, practised until the second one does not need
thinking about.

The one people avoid is the fourth, because asking somebody to repeat feels like admitting
something. It is not. It is the single most ordinary thing anybody says in a shop in any language.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a recorded role-play of at least twelve turns, both voices yours. Twelve turns
cannot be reached by asking twelve questions; it is reached by three or four exchanges that each
keep going. The second move is where the turns come from.

## The rule

**The four second moves, with real lines.** These were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Shopping for clothes](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/shopping-clothes),
at [Ordering in a café](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/ordering-cafe),
and at [Checking understanding](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding).

**One — they say yes.** Close it, and answer the offer that usually follows:

> "Here you are." — "Thanks. And can I pay by card?"
> "Would you like a bag for that?" — "Yes, please."

**Two — they say no.** Do not leave. Ask what they do have:

> "I'm afraid we only have bottled juices." — "Oh, then I'll have an apple juice instead, please."

That line is from the café page, and the shape is worth copying exactly: *then I'll have … instead,
please.* It turns a no into a purchase in one sentence.

**Three — they ask you back.** Answer short. Do not restart your whole request:

> "What size do you need?" — "Extra small, please."
> "Regular or large?" — "I'll have a large, then, please."
> "To eat in or take away?" — "Take away, please."

**Four — you did not catch it.** One phrase, once, no apology chain:

> Sorry, could you say that again, please?
> Can you repeat that more slowly, please?
> I'm sorry?

**Asking about what you are buying.** Two questions do most of the work, and both are from the
checked pages:

> How much is this?
> What teas do you have?

The second shape — *what … do you have?* — is the one that opens a choice when you do not know
what is on offer.

**The exchange drawn.**

```text
   you:  Excuse me — do you have a charger for this phone, please?
   them: We've got these two. Which end do you need?
   you:  ← the second move lives here
   them: …
   you:  Thanks.
```

Practise by writing the assistant's lines **first**, then your answers. It feels backwards and it
is the only way to rehearse a second move, because you cannot practise responding to an answer
you have not written.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the exchange that ends at no:

> "Sorry, we haven't got that." — "Ok."

And you leave, having learned nothing and bought nothing, when the shop very likely had something
that would do. The fix is one sentence: *What do you have that's similar?*

The second is the restart. They ask a small question and you begin your whole request again:

> "What size?" — "Er, sorry, I'm looking for a jumper, I saw one in the window…"

The assistant now has to find the question again. The fix: answer the question they asked, in two
or three words.

The third is the apology chain:

> Sorry, sorry, my English is not good, sorry, can you say again?

Four apologies for a thing that needed none. It also makes the assistant slow down and simplify
more than you need, which makes the rest of the exchange harder. The fix: one *sorry*, one clear
request to repeat.

The fourth is saying yes to something you did not understand, to end the difficulty. That is how
you go home with the wrong size, and the cost arrives later. The fix: move four, every time.

## In real life

Shop assistants are running a script and they are running it fast. The good news is that the
script is short — there are perhaps ten sentences they will say to you all week, and the pages
above contain most of them. Recognising their lines matters more than producing your own, because
their line is what tells you which second move to make.

Under pressure the assistant will shorten: *size? … eat in? … bag?* — single words with a rising
tune. Those are the same questions with everything stripped away. If you have practised the full
version, you will hear the short one.

What a native listener finds difficult is not your accent. It is a customer who goes silent after
an answer, because now they have to guess what went wrong and start offering things. An exchange
where you always say something — even *sorry, could you say that again* — is an easy exchange, and
easy exchanges are the ones where people go and check the back for you.
