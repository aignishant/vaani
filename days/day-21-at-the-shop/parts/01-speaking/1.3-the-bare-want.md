## One-line answer

Record your five asks stripped down to *I want this* and *give me that*, play them back as the person behind the counter, and write what you would do next — because nothing in those sentences is wrong and everything about them is.

## The story

Two customers ask for the same thing within a minute of each other.

> I want a bag.

> Could I have a bag, please?

Both get a bag. The first one gets it handed over without a word. The second gets a bag, a smile
and "there you go" — and when she asks a second question, the assistant goes and checks the
stockroom.

No rule of English was broken by the first customer. *I want a bag* is a correct sentence that
means exactly what it says. It simply costs something that the speaker never sees, because the
cost is paid in how the rest of the exchange goes.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **bare** — with nothing round it: no *excuse me*, no *please*, no question shape.
- **blunt** — direct in a way that lands harder than you meant.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **stockroom** — the room at the back of a shop where extra goods are kept.

*I want* is the sentence almost every beginner reaches for, for a good reason: it is what the
textbook teaches for wanting, and it is what many languages use at a counter without any
rudeness at all.

In English it does something different. *I want* states a fact about you and leaves the other
person no room — there is no question in it, so there is nothing for them to agree to. What
arrives is not a request but an announcement, and the polite reply to an announcement is silence
and compliance.

You cannot hear this from inside your own mouth, because you meant it kindly. So you record it,
and then listen as the assistant.

The finding is not "be polite". It is what you notice yourself wanting to do next when you hear
it — and what you notice is that you want the exchange to end.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking folder's check that can go **RED**. The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn
role-play, and a bare want does not generate turns: it produces the thing and closes the
conversation. Hearing your own bare version is the cheapest way to find out why your role-play
keeps running out of turns.

## The rule

**Step one: record the bare versions.** Take the five exchanges from
[part 1.2](1.2-ask-for-five-real-things.md). Say each ask stripped to the bone: no *excuse me*, no
*please*, no rising tune, no close. Label the take **deliberate**.

> I want this in black.
> Give me a bag.
> How much.
> I want to pay by card.
> Say it again.

Say them at normal volume and in an ordinary friendly voice. The exercise fails if you perform
them as rude — the point is that they are **not** said rudely and still land that way.

**Step two: record the full versions straight after.** Same five, three moves each, with the
second move from part 1.2.

**Step three: listen as the assistant.** Play the bare take. After each line, write down two
things:

| For each bare line | Write |
| --- | --- |
| What would I say back? | |
| Would I offer anything extra — check the back, suggest something else? | |

Then do the same for the full take.

**Step four: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| The bare lines get short replies and no offers; the full ones get offers | GREEN. You can hear the difference from outside. |
| Both columns look the same | RED. Either your bare version was not bare enough, or you are still listening as yourself. Strip the bare lines further and play them to somebody else. |
| The bare lines sound fine to you | RED, and worth taking seriously: this is how your real asks may be landing. Write it at the top of your notebook page. |

**Step five: the repair.** For each bare line, say the repaired version immediately after it on
the recorder — wrong then right, back to back — and then say out loud the **one word** that made
the difference. Usually it is *please*, or the rise at the end, or *excuse me*.

The full versions were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Shopping for clothes](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/shopping-clothes)
— "Excuse me?"; "Do you have this jumper in black?"; "Extra small, please"; "Can I pay by credit
card?"; "How much is this?"; "Yes, please" — and at
[can and could](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/can-could),
which notes that *can* is "**less polite**" than *could* for a request.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is performing the bare lines as rude — barking them, or flattening your voice into
something unfriendly. Then the finding is "rude speech sounds rude", which nobody needed a
recording to learn. The fix: say them warmly. The discovery is that warmth does not save them.

The second is judging them as a student rather than as the assistant. *That one's missing please*
is marking, not listening. The fix: the two columns. Write what you would **say** and whether you
would **offer** anything — behaviour, not a grade.

The third is concluding that English is stiff and that all this is unnecessary. It is a fair
thought and it is worth answering: the three moves take about a second to say, and they are not
formality. They are the signal that you are addressing a person. Every language has such a signal;
English happens to put it in the shape of the sentence rather than in a word ending.

## In real life

Nobody will ever tell you that *I want* landed badly. There is no polite way to say it, so the
information never reaches you. What reaches you instead is a slightly shorter exchange, no offer
to check the back, and a feeling you cannot account for that shops are unfriendly.

Fluent speakers do use *I want* — with friends, with family, and when something has gone wrong and
they have decided to stop being soft. That last use is worth knowing: *I want to speak to the
manager* is a deliberate escalation, and it works precisely because the bare form is marked. If
you use it by default, you have nothing left for the moment you actually need it.

Under pressure, in a queue, native speakers shorten to almost nothing — *bag, please?* — but the
*please* and the rise survive every cut. That tells you which parts were load-bearing all along.
