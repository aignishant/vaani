## One-line answer

A list is for the person's hands — no verbs, one item per line, a number on everything — and a note is for the person's head, in sentences, with a reason; put the two on the same piece of paper and both fail.

## The story

You leave a piece of paper on the kitchen table:

> milk
> bread
> can you take the parcel to the post office it closes at five
> rice
> eggs

Your flatmate comes home from the shop with milk, bread, rice and eggs. The parcel is still by the
door. He read a shopping list, because the first two lines told him it was a shopping list, and
his eye went down the left edge collecting nouns.

He is not careless. A list is read by the eye in a particular way, and a sentence hidden in a list
is not read at all.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **list** — items written one under another, to be collected or ticked off.
- **note** — a short message in sentences, written for somebody to read and understand.
- **item** — one thing on a list.
- **quantity** — how much or how many: two, a bottle of, half a kilo.
- **scan** — to run your eyes down a page quickly, looking for things rather than reading.

These are two different kinds of writing, and the difference is not how long they are. It is
**which part of the reader they are for**.

**A list is for the hands.** It gets **scanned**, not read. So it has no verbs, one item per line,
and a **quantity** on everything. Its whole job is to be checked against a basket.

**A note is for the head.** It gets read as sentences, left to right. So it has verbs, full stops,
and a reason — because a note asks somebody to decide or do something, and a person deciding needs
to know why.

The failure in the story is a category error, not a grammar mistake: a note was put inside a list,
so it was scanned instead of read. If you have both to communicate, you write **two separate
pieces of writing**, even on the same sheet of paper, with a line between them.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate asks for a written note of about a hundred words that asks for something, thanks
somebody and gives a direction. Three jobs, all of them note-jobs, none of them list-jobs. Knowing
which kind of writing you are doing is what stops that note collapsing into bullet points, which
is the most common way it fails.

## The rule

**The list, in three rules.**

| Rule | Why |
| --- | --- |
| One item per line. | The eye reads the left edge; two items on one line makes the second invisible. |
| A quantity on every line. | *Milk* is a guess. *2 litres of milk* is an instruction. |
| No verbs, no sentences. | A verb turns the line into something to read, and it will not be. |

```text
   2 litres milk
   1 brown loaf
   1 kg rice
   6 eggs
   washing-up liquid — the green one
```

Note the last line. When an item could be one of several things, the detail goes **after a dash**,
still on one line. This is the same idea as the detail that picks one thing from
[day 17, part 2.2](../../../day-17-linking-words/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-where-your-things-are.md).

**The note, in three parts.** This is the request shape from
[day 19, part 2.2](../../../day-19-can-and-cant/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-a-request-and-a-rule.md),
used for real:

| Part | Example |
| --- | --- |
| who it is for, and the ask | Sam — can you take the parcel to the post office? |
| the reason | It has to go today. |
| the easy yes, or what happens next | If you can't, leave it and I'll go tomorrow. |

> Sam — can you take the parcel to the post office? It has to go today. If you can't, leave it
> and I'll go tomorrow. Thanks!

**Keeping them apart on one sheet.** A heading each, and a line between:

```text
   SHOPPING                       ← the list: scanned
   2 litres milk
   1 brown loaf
   ─────────────────
   Sam — can you take the         ← the note: read
   parcel to the post office?
   It has to go today.
```

**Punctuation differs between them, on purpose.** List lines take no full stops, because they are
not sentences. Note sentences take all of theirs. Mixing the two conventions is what makes a page
look uncertain about what it is.

The model for a short written message somebody acts on was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[A noticeboard message](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/noticeboard-message)
— "Write the main title or idea bigger to catch people's attention"; "Keep the message short, with
only the most important information" — and the polite-ask shape at
[Instructions for a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/instructions-colleague):
use "Can / Could / Would you please", give the reason, and end by inviting questions. Its model
line: "Could you please book the room for us?"

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the one in the story — a sentence buried in a list. The fix is
not to make the sentence louder. It is to move it out, under a line, with a name on it.

The second is the list with no quantities:

> milk
> rice
> eggs

Your flatmate buys one small carton, one bag of the wrong rice and six eggs when you needed a
dozen. Nothing was wrong on the paper. The fix: a number or a size on every line.

The third is the note with no reason:

> Sam — take the parcel to the post office.

That is an order, from somebody who is not there, and it will be done last if at all. The fix: the
reason, and the easy yes.

The fourth is the note with no name at the top:

> Can somebody take the parcel?

In a flat with three people, *somebody* is nobody. The fix: a name.

## In real life

Real lists are read in a shop with one hand, in a hurry, and half the time on a phone screen. Any
line the eye cannot take in at a glance is skipped. This is why quantities go **before** the item:
*2 litres milk*, not *milk, 2 litres*. The number is what the hand needs first.

Real notes are read standing up, once, by somebody who has just come in. Fluent writers put the
name first and the ask second, because both survive being read at the door. Anything below the
third line is read later, if at all — which is why the reason is short and the apology, if any,
goes at the end.

What a reader does with a page that mixes the two is trust neither. A shopping list with a
paragraph in it reads as disorganised, and the next one you leave gets less attention. Keeping
them apart is not tidiness; it is how you stay somebody whose notes get read.
