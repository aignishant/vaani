## One-line answer

Write a list somebody could shop from without knowing your kitchen, and a note somebody could act on without knowing your day, and test each one by giving it to the wrong person in your head.

## The story

Your list says:

> the usual bread
> Ma's tea
> that soap

Every line is perfectly clear — to you, and to the one person in the world who has been to the
shop with you fifty times. Your cousin, who is staying this week and offered to go, stands in the
aisle holding a phone and a piece of paper that might as well be in code.

The list was not wrong. It was written for a reader who already knew the answers, which is the
same as writing for nobody.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the wrong person** — a reader who does not share your habits or your kitchen: a visitor, a
  neighbour, somebody helping for the first time.
- **shorthand** — a short form only the people who already know it can read.
- **act on** — to do what a message says without asking any more questions.
- **brand** — the particular make of a thing, which a list should describe rather than name.

Both pieces of writing get tested the same way and it is the same test as everywhere in this
folder: **hand it, in your head, to somebody who does not know what you know.**

For the list, that person is standing in a shop. They can see hundreds of things and they cannot
ask you anything. Every line has to survive on its own.

For the note, that person has just walked in, is holding a bag, and will read about three lines
before putting it down. The name and the ask have to be in those three lines.

**Shorthand** is the enemy of both. *The usual*, *that one*, *Ma's* — these are not lazy, they are
efficient for the reader you had in mind. The problem is that you will not always have that
reader, and you cannot tell from looking at the page which lines depend on them.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate asks for a note of about a hundred words that asks for something, thanks somebody
and gives a direction — written for a reader you will not be standing next to. Everything in this
part is that skill at a smaller size, where the cost of getting it wrong is one wrong loaf rather
than a failed gate.

## The rule

**The list: every line survives a stranger.** Four things on each line, in this order:

```text
   2 litres    milk        — the blue top
   ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾    ‾‾‾‾          ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
   quantity    the thing     which one, if it matters
```

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| the usual bread | 1 brown loaf, sliced |
| Ma's tea | 1 box of loose black tea, the small red box |
| that soap | 2 bars of plain white soap |
| rice | 1 kg basmati rice |

Note what the middle column does **not** do: it does not name a shop's make. A description gets
you the right thing in any shop; a make gets you nothing in the shop that does not stock it. Say
what the thing is like, not what it is called.

**The note: the name and the ask in the first line.** Then the reason, then the easy yes, then
the thanks.

> Sam — could you take the parcel to the post office when you go out? It has to be posted today
> or they send it back. If you can't get there, just leave it by the door and I'll go first thing
> tomorrow. Thanks a lot.

Four sentences. The name is first because a note with no name is for nobody. The ask is second
because a reader who stops after two lines has still got the message. The polite-ask shape was
checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Instructions for a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/instructions-colleague):
use "Can / Could / Would you please", give the reason behind the instruction, and end by inviting
questions and signing off. Its model line: "Could you please book the room for us?"

**Both on one sheet, kept apart.** A heading on each, a line between, list lines with no full
stops and note sentences with all of theirs.

**The two tests.** Do these in writing, not in your head.

| Piece | Test | Passes when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the list | Read each line as a person in a shop who has never been to your home. | No line needs a question. |
| the note | Read only the first two lines, as somebody who has just come in. | You know who it is for and what is being asked. |

Mark every line that fails. Those are the lines carrying **shorthand**, and rewriting them is the
work of this part.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the list that only you can read — the story. The fix is the
stranger test, line by line, and it usually changes two or three lines out of eight.

The second is over-correction, where every line becomes a paragraph:

> 2 litres of full-fat milk, the one with the blue top, from the fridge at the back near the
> window, not the long-life one

Now the line cannot be scanned, which is the one thing a list must be. The fix: quantity, thing,
one dash-detail. If it needs more than that, it belongs in the note.

The third is the note that buries the ask:

> Hi! Hope you had a good day, sorry to bother you, I know you're busy, I was going to go myself
> but the shop closes early on a Tuesday and then I had to wait in for the gas man, anyway —
> could you take the parcel?

The ask is in line five and the reader stopped at line two. The fix: the name and the ask first,
and the apology, if you still want it, at the end.

The fourth is thanking in advance in a way that removes the choice:

> Thanks for doing this!

written before they have agreed. It reads as though refusing is not available. The fix: *thanks a
lot* at the end, after the easy yes has made refusing possible.

## In real life

Real notes are competing with a phone. Anything that takes longer to read than a message would
have taken gets put down "for later", and later does not come. That is the argument for four
sentences rather than eight, and for the ask in line one.

Fluent writers leave the note where the ask will happen — by the parcel, on the door, next to the
kettle — and that placing does half the work of the writing. Nobody teaches this and everybody who
is good at it does it.

What a reader remembers is whether your last note was easy. A list they could shop from and a note
they could act on makes them the person who offers to go next time. Two wrong loaves and they stop
offering, and no amount of good writing afterwards gets that back quickly.
