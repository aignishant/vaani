## One-line answer

Copy your sheet with the quantities stripped off and the note hidden inside the list, then write down exactly what a stranger would bring home — the gap between that and what you needed is the whole finding.

## The story

A list comes back from the shop as a bag on the table. The list said:

> milk
> bread
> tea
> can you also pay the electricity
> soap

In the bag: a small carton of milk, a white sliced loaf, a box of teabags, and a bar of scented
soap. Not in the bag: anything the right size, and the electricity is still unpaid.

Nobody made a mistake. Every item in the bag is on the list. The list simply did not say which
milk, how much bread, what kind of tea — and the one line that was not an item at all was scanned
past like all the others.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **strip** — to take something off, leaving less than there was.
- **hide** — here, to put a sentence somewhere it will not be read: inside a list.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **basket** — what the shopper actually comes home with.

You have a sheet that works ([part 2.2](2.2-write-the-list-and-the-note.md)). This part finds out
**how much** of that work was done by the quantities and by keeping the note separate — and the
way to find out is to take both away and predict the basket.

Predicting the basket is the trick worth keeping from today. You do not need anybody to go
shopping. You write, item by item, **the most likely thing a stranger would pick up** for each
line. Being honest about "most likely" is the skill: not the worst case, not the case that proves
your point — the thing a reasonable person in a hurry would grab.

Then you compare that basket with what you actually needed, and count the misses.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing folder's check that can go **RED**. A written instruction nobody tested is a
guess about a reader, and this is the cheapest possible test of one. The phase 2 gate note has to
be acted on by somebody you are not standing next to; the habit of predicting what they will
actually do is what makes that note work.

## The rule

**Step one: break the sheet, two ways.** Copy your part 2.2 sheet with exactly two changes:

1. **Strip every quantity and every dash-detail** from the list. Leave the bare item words.
2. **Hide the note inside the list** — put its sentences as lines in the middle of the items,
   with no heading, no line between, and no name at the top.

Change no item and delete nothing else. Label the page **deliberate**.

> milk
> bread
> could you take the parcel to the post office
> tea
> soap
> rice

**Step two: predict the basket.** For each item line, write the most likely thing a stranger in a
hurry would pick up. Be honest.

| The line says | A stranger brings home | I actually needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| milk | | |
| bread | | |
| tea | | |
| soap | | |
| rice | | |

**Step three: the note line.** Write down, honestly, whether a person scanning that page would
read it, act on it, or pass over it. Then write **why** — what about its position made it
invisible.

**Step four: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Three or more items wrong, and the note passed over | GREEN. The quantities and the separation were doing real work, and you have the basket to prove it. |
| Fewer than three wrong | RED. Your stripped list still carries detail, or you predicted generously. Strip harder and predict again. |
| You expect the note to be read | RED, and worth checking: read your own broken page top to bottom at speed, with one finger going down the left edge, and see whether you still think so. |

**Step five: restore, and count the cost.** Write the correct sheet out again in full, marked
**GREEN**, under the broken one. Then write one line: **how many wrong items, and what the note
cost** — a missed post, a late bill, a second trip.

Keep both versions. The two baskets side by side are the evidence, and in a month they will be
more persuasive than any rule.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is predicting the basket unfairly. You write that a stranger would bring home the
smallest, cheapest, most useless version of everything, and the check comes back GREEN on evidence
you invented. The fix: write what **you** would pick up if somebody handed you this list in a shop
you did not know. That is the honest prediction.

The second is choosing items where the bare word is enough. *Eggs* is fairly safe; *milk* is not,
and *tea* is a disaster. If your list is all safe items, nothing breaks and you learn nothing. The
fix: make sure at least three items are ones that come in several kinds.

The third is deciding that the hidden note "would probably still be noticed". It might, once, by
somebody unhurried. The exercise is about the ordinary case. The fix: do the finger test — run one
finger down the left edge at scanning speed and see what your eye actually took in.

## In real life

Real written instructions are followed literally by people with no time and no context. This is
not carelessness; it is what following an instruction means. Anything you left to be inferred will
be inferred wrongly about a third of the time, and you will find out at the worst moment.

Fluent writers overspecify exactly the items that come in kinds, and leave the obvious ones alone.
*6 eggs* needs nothing more. *Tea* needs three words. Knowing which lines need protecting is
judgement, and predicting the basket is how you build it.

What a reader does after coming home with the wrong thing twice is start phoning you from the
shop. That is a reasonable response and it costs you both more than the three words would have.
Writing well here is not politeness or precision for its own sake — it is the difference between
an instruction that finishes by itself and one that generates a phone call.
