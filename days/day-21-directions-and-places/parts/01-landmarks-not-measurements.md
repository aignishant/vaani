# Landmarks, not measurements

`FN-05` · Day 21

## The one-line answer

Directions and place descriptions run on landmarks and pictures, never on measurements — and once
you stop trying to measure, you discover you already had the English for both.

## The model

Think about the map somebody draws for you on the back of an envelope. No scale. No compass. Two
wobbly lines for the main road, a box marked *bakery*, an arrow, and the word *here*. It is
inaccurate in every way a map can be inaccurate, and it works perfectly.

Now think about what happens when you try to give directions the other way — in distances and
durations. Nobody can hold a number while walking. Nobody can feel the difference between two
numbers on their feet. And you cannot check a number in your own mouth, which means you will
hesitate before every single one. **Measurements are the reason learners freeze giving
directions, and they were never load-bearing to begin with.** Draw the envelope map instead.

A hand-drawn map has three moves.

**It anchors to something the other person already has.** Not where the place is in the world —
where it is relative to a thing they know:

> It's just round the corner from the station.
> It's on the way to the hospital, so you'll go past it anyway.

**It sequences turns by what you will see, not by how far you go.** Every turn gets tied to a
thing:

> Keep going till you get to the church, then take the next left.
> It's opposite the big supermarket, the one with the car park.

Notice there is no *go along for a bit and then it's on your left*. A turn with nothing attached
to it is a turn the other person will miss, because they have nothing to check themselves
against.

**And it ends by handing over honestly.** Two endings, and you need both:

> …and then you can't miss it.
> Head towards the river and then ask again — it's easier from there.

*You can't miss it* is reassurance, and it is a promise you should only make when the last
landmark really is unmissable. *Ask again* is the honest one, and it is not a failure. It is what
people who know a city actually say, because they know the last stretch is the fiddly bit.

**Describing a place** is the same map, drawn for someone who is not going there. It needs one
comparison to something they know, one detail that belongs only to this place, and one line about
what it is like to be inside it:

> It's the sort of place where nobody looks up when you walk in.

That last kind of line does more than any amount of layout. Rooms are hard to picture from
dimensions and easy to picture from behaviour.

One more thing, because directions come at you as well as from you. **Following** them is a
listening skill, and the useful move is to hold the landmarks and let the rest go. If you catch
the church and lose everything after it, you already have the phrase for that: *I got the first
part, not the rest*, from Day 3. Say it while they are still standing there.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| It's just round the corner from … | Anchoring to a landmark the other person already knows |
| It's on the way to … | Placing it along a route they are taking anyway |
| Keep going till you get to … | The long stretch, without measuring it |
| Take the next left. | A turn, tied to where they will already be standing |
| It's opposite the … | Pinning a place by what faces it |
| You can't miss it. | Closing directions with reassurance you can honour |
| Head towards … and then ask again. | The honest handover when the last stretch is fiddly |
| It's the sort of place where … | Describing a place by what happens in it, not its layout |

## Say it now

Say each three times, filling in your own content. Do not write anything down, and do not let a
single measurement in.

1. *It's just round the corner from {{…}}.*
2. *It's on the way to {{…}}, so you'll go past it anyway.*
3. *Keep going till you get to {{…}}, then take the next left.*
4. *It's opposite the {{…}} — you can't miss it.*
5. *Head towards {{…}} and then ask again.*
6. *It's the sort of place where {{…}}.*

Then join all six into one continuous set of directions, twice, without stopping. Somebody
standing in front of you does not wait while you plan the next turn.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d21-free.m4a`.**

Two takes in one file, no stopping.

> **Take one — directions.** Start from somewhere you and a stranger would both know, and get
> them to a place you go often. Every turn attached to something they will see.
> **Take two — the place itself.** Describe it so somebody who will never go there can picture
> it. One comparison, one detail that is only true of this place, one line about what it is like
> to be in it.

Not a single number in either take. Not for distance, not for how long anything takes, not for
anything else. If you feel one coming, replace it with a landmark.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** they close their eyes for take one and walk it in their head. At the end
  they tell you where they ended up. Wherever they say is the correct answer, and the gap between
  that and where you meant is your feedback.

## The deliberate failure

Give the same directions again using **only left, right, straight on and back**. No landmarks at
all. No names of anything.

**What breaking sounds like:** you will lose your own place. Listen for the backtrack — *no,
sorry, right, then left* — which arrives almost at once, because you have nothing to hold on to
either. The whole thing also flattens into a list, every clause the same shape, and by
the end neither of you could say where the imagined person is standing.

Then run it again with the landmarks restored. The route did not change. What changed is that
both of you can now check where you are at any point, which is the only reason directions work.

## When it breaks in the wild

Someone stops you in the street and asks for a place you know perfectly well. You can see it. You
could walk there without thinking. And what comes out is pointing, and *there, there*, and a
half-turn of the body, and then nothing — because you reached for a distance, could not find one,
and the whole sentence died waiting for it.

The knowledge was never the problem. The measurement was.

**The repair:**

> Head towards the church and then ask again — it's just round the corner from there.

Two landmarks and an honest handover. You have given them everything you actually know, and they
will get there.
