## One-line answer

An order that works is one line with three things in it — how many, what, and which size — because every one you leave out becomes a question, and every question is another turn in a queue.

## The story

You are at a café counter with four people behind you. You say:

> Can I have a coffee, please?

And she says: "Which one — filter or white?" You say white. "Regular or large?" Large. "To eat in
or take away?" Take away. "Anything with that?" No, thanks.

Four questions to buy one coffee. Nothing went wrong and nobody was rude. But the exchange took
five turns where it could have taken one, and the whole time you could feel the queue behind you.

The order was not incomplete because your English was poor. It was incomplete because you gave one
of the three things and there are three.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **order** — what you ask for in a café or restaurant, and the act of asking.
- **queue** — the line of people waiting to be served.
- **size** — how big: regular, large, small.
- **take away** — to carry your food out rather than eat it there.
- **filter coffee** — coffee made by passing water through ground coffee, usually served black.

An order is not a request for a thing. It is a request for **a specific amount of a specific
version of a thing**, and the server cannot begin until they have all three.

| Piece | What it answers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **how many** | a, one, two | a |
| **what** | which item, and which kind of it | white coffee |
| **what size** | regular, large, small | large |

Say all three and the server can start. Say one and they will ask about the other two — politely,
and it will cost you three turns and a small feeling of being in the way.

This is different from day 21's shop, where one question was usually enough. A café sells the same
item in several versions, so the versions have to be chosen before anything happens.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a recorded shop or café role-play of at least twelve turns, both voices yours.
A complete order is what makes those turns real rather than padding: the server's four standard
questions, your answers, and the close. An order in one line is also the piece you will use most
often in real life, which is the better reason.

## The rule

**The shape.** Two openings, both fine, both from the checked page:

> Can I have a large white coffee, please?
> I'll have a large white coffee, please.

*I'll have* is what people use once they have decided, and it is very common at a counter. *Can I
have* is the same request with the question shape from
[day 21, part 1.1](../../../day-21-at-the-shop/parts/01-speaking/1.1-excuse-me-and-the-ask.md).

**The server's four questions.** These were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Ordering in a café](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/ordering-cafe).
Learn to recognise them; they are almost always these:

| They ask | You answer |
| --- | --- |
| "Regular or large?" | Large, please. |
| "To eat in or take away?" | Take away, please. |
| "Would you like a drink with that?" | No, thanks. Just the cookie. |
| "Can I help anyone?" | Can I have…, please. |

**When you do not know what they have.** One question opens the whole menu:

> What teas do you have?

and the answer will be a short list — "Breakfast tea, mint and green tea" — from which you pick.

**When you do not know how big a size is.** Ask. It is completely ordinary:

> How big is a large?

**Ordering more than one thing.** Join them with *and*, in one line, each with its own quantity:

> A mint tea and a slice of lemon cake, please.

Note *a slice of*. Cake is not counted in ones — you cannot ask for *a cake* unless you want the
whole thing. Things like this have their own small words, and the writing folder is not where you
will meet them; you meet them here, at the counter, as fixed phrases: *a slice of*, *a cup of*, *a
bottle of*, *a piece of*.

**The line drawn.**

```text
   Can I have    a      large    white coffee,   please?
                 ‾      ‾‾‾‾‾    ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
              how many  size     what
```

**Changing your mind.** Also ordinary, and it has a shape:

> Sorry, I wanted freshly squeezed orange juice.
> Oh, then I'll have an apple juice instead, please.

*Then I'll have … instead, please* is the line to keep. It turns a problem into a finished order in
one sentence.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the bare item, which is the story:

> Can I have a coffee, please?

Nothing is wrong and it costs four questions. The fix: decide the size and the kind before you get
to the front, not while you are standing there.

The second is the order with no quantity:

> Coffee and cake, please.

The server does not know whether that is one of each or a tray for four. The fix: *a* in front of
each thing.

The third is answering a question you were not asked, because you rehearsed a whole speech:

> "Regular or large?" — "Yes, I would like a coffee please, with milk, to take away."

The server asked one word's worth of question. The fix: answer that, then wait.

The fourth is saying yes to the extra because saying no feels rude:

> "Would you like a drink with that?" — "Yes, ok."

*No, thanks* is the normal answer and nobody minds it at all. The page checked above has it word
for word: "No, thanks. Just the cookie."

## In real life

Counter English is fast, quiet and heavily reduced. *Would you like a drink with that* comes out
close to *jyou like a drink with that*, and *regular or large* as two stressed words with
everything between them swallowed — which is the linking from
[day 17, part 1.1](../../../day-17-linking-words/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-join-between-words.md)
doing its work. You are not expected to catch every word. You are expected to catch *regular*,
*large*, *eat in*, *take away*, and answer.

Fluent customers order in one line and then say nothing else until they pay. The whole exchange is
often three turns: the order, the price, the thanks. If your order needs five, that is the thing
worth practising, not your accent.

What a server notices is a customer who has not decided. There is no rudeness in it and there is a
small, real cost: they start giving you the short, closed version of the exchange, because their
job is the queue. A complete order buys you the friendly version.
