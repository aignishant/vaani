## One-line answer

Ordering for more than one person is the same one-line order repeated, in a fixed order, with the things that need saying out loud — allergies, changes, who is paying — said before the food arrives and not after.

## The story

Three of you sit down. You order for everybody because your English is best, and you do it well:
three dishes, two drinks, all clear. The food comes. Your friend looks at her plate and says,
quietly, "I don't eat egg."

You knew that. You have known it for years. It simply did not occur to you that it was a thing to
say to the person taking the order, because at home you would have mentioned it while cooking.

Ordering is not only naming food. It is the moment when everything the kitchen needs to know has
to be said, and there is no second moment.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **dish** — one cooked item on a menu.
- **allergy** — when a food makes somebody ill, sometimes seriously.
- **the bill** — the piece of paper saying what you owe.
- **separately** — each person paying for their own.
- **starter** — the small dish before the main one.

A real order has more in it than the food, and the extra things have a right moment. Said at the
right moment they cost one sentence. Said late they cost a returned plate, or worse.

Four things get said **while ordering**:

1. **The food and drink**, one line each, with how many and what size.
2. **Anything that must not be in it** — an allergy, or something you do not eat.
3. **Any change** to a dish: without the sauce, no ice, well cooked.
4. **How the bill works**, if it is not obvious: together or separately.

And one thing gets said **at the end, not during**: whether you want anything else. Waiting for
that question is normal; jumping to it is what makes an order feel rushed.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn role-play in a shop or café, both voices yours. A single drink
does not get to twelve turns honestly. An order for two people, with a question in the middle and
a change to one dish, does — and it is also the thing you will actually need the first time you
eat out with somebody.

## The rule

**The order of the order.** Say it in this sequence and the server can write it down as you go:

```text
   1. drinks        "Two coffees, please — one black, one white."
   2. food          "And a mint tea and a slice of lemon cake."
   3. changes       "Could I have the salad without the egg?"
   4. the bill      "Could we pay separately?"
```

Doing it in a different order is not wrong; doing it in **no** order is what makes a server ask
you to start again.

**Saying what you do not eat.** Two shapes, and the first is stronger:

> I'm allergic to nuts. Is there anything with nuts in it?
> I don't eat egg — is the salad all right?

Use the first one if it is an **allergy**. It is not a preference and servers treat it differently,
which is exactly what you want.

**Changing a dish.** The pattern is *without* or *no*:

> Can I have it without the sauce, please?
> A large orange juice, without ice.

That last one is from the page checked on 2026-09-22,
[Ordering in a café](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/ordering-cafe):
"I'll have a large, then, please. Without ice."

**Asking about what is in something.** One question does it:

> What's in the salad?
> Does it have egg in it?

**The lines you will hear back.** From the same page. Recognising them is more than half the job:

| They say | It means |
| --- | --- |
| "Can I help anyone?" | start ordering |
| "Who's next?" | they do not know whose turn it is |
| "Regular or large?" | choose a size |
| "To eat in or take away?" | choose where |
| "Would you like a drink with that?" | an offer you can decline |
| "I'm afraid we only have…" | they do not have what you asked for |
| "That's £3.50, please." | pay now |

**When they do not have it.** The one-sentence recovery, word for word from the same page:

> Oh, then I'll have an apple juice instead, please.

**Whose turn it is.** At a busy counter people sort this out themselves:

> "Who's next?" — "You were first, I think."

Saying that when it is not your turn is a small thing that makes you part of the room rather than
a person standing in it.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the allergy mentioned after the food arrives. Nothing about it is
a language failure and the cost is the largest on this page. The fix: it goes in the order, before
the food is made.

The second is ordering everything in one long breath:

> Two coffees and a tea and a cake and one of those and also can we pay separately and she doesn't
> eat egg.

The server got the first two items. The fix: four short stretches, in the sequence above, with a
pause after each so they can write.

The third is ordering for somebody else without checking:

> "And she'll have the chicken."

when she has not said so. Common when one person's English is better, and it removes her from her
own meal. The fix: order **your** things, then say "and…?" and let her order hers, even slowly.

The fourth is not asking what is in something because asking feels like trouble. It is not
trouble; it is a question servers answer twenty times a day. The fix: *what's in it?*

## In real life

Restaurants are loud and servers are carrying things. They will miss items, and the normal
response is not to apologise but to repeat: *and a mint tea as well, please.* Beginners tend to
assume a missed item was their fault and let it go, and then the tea never comes.

Fluent speakers confirm at the end, in one line: *so that's two coffees, a tea and a cake, yes?*
That single sentence catches nearly every mistake before it reaches the kitchen, and it is the
most useful thing in this part.

Under pressure — a queue, a shouted order, a server with three tables — everything gets shorter
but the allergy sentence never does. Servers slow down for it, repeat it back, and sometimes write
it separately. That difference in treatment is worth knowing, because it tells you the sentence is
doing its job.
