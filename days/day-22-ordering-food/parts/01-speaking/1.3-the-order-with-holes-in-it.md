## One-line answer

Record your order with the sizes, quantities and choices taken out, then play the server's side honestly and count the questions — the number is how long your real order takes, and it is not a matter of opinion.

## The story

Two people order at the same counter, one after the other.

The first:

> Can I have a coffee? … White. … Large. … Take away. … No, thanks.

Five turns.

The second:

> Can I have a large white coffee to take away, please?

One turn. Same coffee, same price, same politeness, and the second person is out of the door while
the first is still being asked about biscuits.

Nobody was rude to the first person and they did not do anything wrong. The difference is
countable, and it is worth counting in your own voice once.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **hole** — here, a piece of information the order needed and did not have.
- **turn** — one person's stretch of speaking before the other speaks.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **honestly** — here, playing the server as a real server would be, not as you wish they were.

Every piece you leave out of an order becomes a **hole**, and every hole becomes a question. This
is not a matter of style or politeness — it is arithmetic, and that is what makes it a good check.

So you record your order with the holes in it, then play the server's side **honestly**: for each
hole, ask the question a real server would have to ask. Then count the turns.

The count is the finding. It is a number, you cannot argue with it, and it will be larger than you
expect.

The second half of the exercise is more interesting than the first. You count the turns for the
full version too — and it is not always one. Some of your orders need a question in the middle no
matter how well you order, because the place sells three kinds of the thing. Knowing which of your
orders can be one turn and which cannot is the real result.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking folder's check that can go **RED**. The phase 2 gate wants twelve turns of
role-play, and it is worth knowing now that turns caused by holes are not the same as turns caused
by conversation. Twelve turns of a server asking you what size is not a role-play; it is one order
taking a long time.

## The rule

**Step one: record the order with holes.** Take the five orders from
[part 1.1](1.1-the-order-in-one-line.md). Say each one stripped to the bare item — no quantity, no
size, no choice made, no *eat in or take away*. Label the take **deliberate**.

> Can I have a coffee?
> Tea, please.
> Can I have a sandwich?
> Juice.
> A cake, please.

**Step two: play the server honestly.** After each, in the server's voice, ask every question that
order genuinely leaves open. Do not be kind. If *tea* could be three kinds, ask which.

> "Which tea? … Regular or large? … To eat in or take away? … Anything with that?"

**Step three: record the full versions and play the server again.** Same five, all three pieces in
one line. Play the server honestly here too — some will still have a question, and that is the
point.

**Step four: count.**

| Order | Turns, holed version | Turns, full version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | | |
| 2 | | |
| 3 | | |
| 4 | | |
| 5 | | |

**Step five: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| The holed version needs at least twice as many turns | GREEN. The three pieces are doing real work, and you have the number. |
| The two columns are close | RED. Either you did not strip the orders, or you played the server kindly. Ask every question a real server would. |
| The full version takes only one turn every time | RED — you may be playing the server too kindly the other way. At a real counter, at least one of five orders gets a question back. |

**Step six: the repair, and the finding.** For each order, say the full version once more and then
say out loud **which single missing piece** caused the most questions in the holed one. Usually it
is the size. Write one line: which piece you personally leave out most often.

The server's real questions were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Ordering in a café](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/ordering-cafe):
"Regular or large?"; "To eat in or take away?"; "Would you like a drink with that?"; "What teas do
you have?"; "I'm afraid we only have bottled juices."

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is playing the server generously — guessing what you probably meant and handing it over.
Real servers do guess sometimes, and then you get the wrong thing, which is a different cost and
not a smaller one. The fix: for this exercise the server always asks.

The second is choosing orders with no versions. If you only order bottled water, there are no
holes to leave and nothing to count. The fix: at least three of your five must be things that come
in sizes or kinds.

The third is treating the result as a rule about being quick. Speed is not the point and there is
nothing wrong with a five-turn order if you wanted the conversation. The point is that the turns
should be **yours to spend** — a question you wanted to ask, not four you caused by accident. The
fix: after counting, mark which turns you would have chosen to have.

## In real life

Servers work by filling in a form in their head, and every hole stops them at that field. This is
why an order given in the wrong sequence can cost more turns than one with a missing piece — they
cannot write the size before they know the drink.

Fluent customers front-load: everything in one line, then silence. And when they do want to talk —
to ask what is good, to change something — they do it deliberately, after the order is complete,
so the server has already written it down.

What a busy server does with a holed order is not complain. They ask the questions, quickly and
flatly, and then serve the next person. What you lose is the version of the exchange where they
tell you the cake is fresh today, and you will never know you lost it.
