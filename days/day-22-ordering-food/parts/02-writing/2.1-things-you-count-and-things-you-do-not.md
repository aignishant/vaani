## One-line answer

English splits nouns in two — the ones you can put a number in front of and the ones you cannot — and the second kind takes *some*, *any*, and a little word like *a piece of* when you need one of them.

## The story

You are writing a message about what is left in the kitchen, so that whoever gets home first knows
what to buy:

> There are two rices and a bread. We need three milks.

Everything in it is understandable. It is also the sentence that tells any English reader, in
about a second, roughly how long you have been learning — not because of a hard rule, but because
of three small words that English treats differently from almost every other kind of word.

And there is nothing in the meaning to guide you. *Rice* and *potato* are both food you can
measure. English counts one and not the other, and that is simply a fact about the language.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **noun** — a word for a thing, a person or a place: *bread*, *sister*, *Pune*. From
  [day 4, part 2.1](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md).
- **countable** — a noun you can put a number in front of: *one apple, two apples*.
- **uncountable** — a noun you cannot: not *two rices*.
- **plural** — the *-s* form, for more than one.
- **unit word** — a little phrase that makes an uncountable noun countable: *a piece of*.

English nouns come in two kinds.

**Countable nouns** can be counted. The page checked on 2026-09-22,
[Nouns: countable and uncountable](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/nouns-countable-uncountable),
puts it exactly: "Countable nouns can be counted, e.g. an apple, two apples, three apples, etc."
They take *a* or *an* in the singular and an *-s* in the plural.

**Uncountable nouns** cannot. The same page: "Uncountable nouns cannot be counted, e.g. air, rice,
water, etc." They never take *a*, never take an *-s*, and always take a singular verb — *the rice
is*, not *the rice are*.

The hard part is not the rule. It is that **you cannot tell from the thing itself.** Rice is small
and countable in the world and uncountable in English. Bread is one loaf on a shelf and
uncountable in English. The list has to be learned, and the useful thing is that it is short —
most nouns are countable, and the uncountable ones cluster in a few groups.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 2 is shops, kitchens, lists and messages, and every one of those is full of exactly these
words: bread, rice, milk, water, money, information. You will write them every week. Getting
*two rices* wrong is a small error that recurs forever, which makes it worth one careful day.

## The rule

**The uncountable ones you will actually meet.** From the checked page: "bread, rice, coffee,
information, money, advice, luggage, furniture." Grouped so they are easier to hold:

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| things in grains or powder | rice, sugar, salt, flour |
| liquids | water, milk, coffee, tea, oil |
| things cut from a mass | bread, cheese, meat, cake |
| ideas and abstract things | information, advice, money, help, work, news |
| collections named as one | furniture, luggage, fruit |

The last two groups are the ones that catch everybody, because in many languages they are counted.
*An information* and *an advice* are among the most common errors in English anywhere.

**Some and any.** This is where the two kinds behave the same, which is a relief. From the same
page:

| | Positive | Negative | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| countable plural | some friends | not any seats | any / how many |
| uncountable | some milk | not any coffee | any / how much |

> "I have some friends in New York."
> "There aren't any seats."
> "There's some milk in the fridge."
> "There isn't any coffee."

So: **some** in positives, **any** in negatives and questions. One exception, from the same page:
when you are offering or suggesting, *some* is used in a question — "Do you want some chocolate?"

**How many and how much.** Also from the checked page:

> "How many chairs are there?"
> "How much orange juice is there?"

*How many* for countable, *how much* for uncountable. This is the pair that gives you a quick
test: if *how many* sounds wrong, the noun is uncountable.

**The unit word.** When you do need to count an uncountable thing, you count the **container or
the piece**, not the thing:

| Not | But |
| --- | --- |
| two breads | two loaves of bread |
| a bread | a slice of bread |
| three milks | three bottles of milk |
| two advices | two pieces of advice |
| a furniture | a piece of furniture |

```text
      a  piece   of    bread
      ‾  ‾‾‾‾‾   ‾‾    ‾‾‾‾‾
   count  the unit    the uncountable thing
```

*A piece of* is the general one and works for most of them. *A slice of*, *a bottle of*, *a cup
of*, *a bit of* are the specific ones.

**The verb.** An uncountable noun is grammatically singular, always:

| Write | Not |
| --- | --- |
| The rice is ready. | The rice are ready. |
| There is some milk. | There are some milk. |

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the plural on an uncountable noun:

> We need three milks.

A reader understands it perfectly and marks it instantly. The fix: a unit word. *Three bottles of
milk.*

The second is *a* or *an* in front of one:

> Can you give me an advice?

Very common, because in many languages advice is counted. The fix: *some advice*, or *a piece of
advice*.

The third is *how many* with an uncountable noun:

> How many bread do we need?

The fix: *how much bread*, or count the unit — *how many loaves*.

The fourth is *are* with an uncountable noun:

> There are some rice left.

The fix: *there is some rice left*. The little word *some* does not make it plural.

## In real life

Nobody will misunderstand you over any of this. *Three milks* gets you three cartons of milk in
any shop in the world. So the cost is not communication; it is that these four errors are the ones
English speakers notice most reliably, because they are pattern errors rather than vocabulary
gaps, and pattern errors read as level.

Fluent writers do not think about the rule. They have learned the twenty or so uncountable nouns
that come up daily, as whole phrases: *some bread*, *a bit of advice*, *how much money*. Learning
them as phrases rather than as a rule is much faster, and it is what the reps in this folder are
for.

And a real complication, worth knowing so it does not confuse you later: some nouns are both,
with different meanings. *Two coffees* is perfectly correct in a café — it means two cups. *A
cheese* means a whole cheese. The rule is not broken there; the unit word has simply been dropped
because everybody knows what it is.
