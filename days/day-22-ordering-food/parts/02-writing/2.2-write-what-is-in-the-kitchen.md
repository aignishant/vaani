## One-line answer

Write what is there and what is not, in sentences, using *some* for what you have, *any* for what you have run out of, and a unit word for everything that cannot take a number.

## The story

Somebody is going to the shop and sends you a message: *anything we need?*

You open the cupboard and write back:

> There's some rice and there are four eggs. There isn't any milk and we've only got one slice of
> bread. Could you get two litres of milk and a loaf?

They come back with exactly the right things, because the message told them two separate stories —
what is there, and what is missing — and gave a unit for everything that needed one.

A message like that takes no longer to write than a vague one. It simply has to be written while
looking in the cupboard rather than remembering it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **run out of** — to have used all of something, so there is none left.
- **stock** — what you have in the house.
- **unit word** — a little phrase that makes an uncountable noun countable: *a loaf of*, *two
  litres of*. From [part 2.1](2.1-things-you-count-and-things-you-do-not.md).
- **cupboard** — a cabinet where food is kept.

A message about food does two jobs, and they need the two halves of the rule from part 2.1.

**What is there** — positive sentences, so *some* and numbers:

> There's some rice. There are four eggs.

**What is not there** — negative sentences, so *any*:

> There isn't any milk. There aren't any onions.

That is the whole grammar of it, and it maps onto the two things the reader needs. The countable
things take numbers. The uncountable things take *some*, *any*, or a unit word when the amount
matters.

The only judgement is when the amount matters. *Some rice* is enough if you just want them to
know there is rice. *Half a kilo of rice* is needed if they are deciding whether to buy more.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 2 is notes, messages and short emails about ordinary life, and half of ordinary life is what
is in the house. The phase 2 gate note asks for something specific enough to be acted on — and
"we need milk" is the shape of instruction that produces the wrong carton, exactly as it did in
day 21's basket.

## The rule

**Two halves, in this order: what is there, then what is missing, then the ask.**

```text
   1. what is there      "There's some rice and there are four eggs."
   2. what is missing    "There isn't any milk and we've only got one slice of bread."
   3. the ask            "Could you get two litres of milk and a loaf?"
```

Putting *what is missing* second is deliberate: it sits directly above the ask, so a reader who
stops early still has the important half.

**The four sentence shapes.** These are from
[Nouns: countable and uncountable](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/nouns-countable-uncountable),
checked on 2026-09-22:

| | There is / are | Example from the page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| countable, positive | There are some… | "I have some friends in New York." |
| countable, negative | There aren't any… | "There aren't any seats." |
| uncountable, positive | There's some… | "There's some milk in the fridge." |
| uncountable, negative | There isn't any… | "There isn't any coffee." |

**Units, when the amount matters.** The ask half is where units earn their place, because the
reader is standing in a shop:

| Vague | Actionable |
| --- | --- |
| get some milk | get two litres of milk |
| get bread | get a large brown loaf |
| get rice | get a kilo of rice |
| get cheese | get 200 grams of cheese |

This is day 21's quantity rule and this part's unit words doing the same job from two directions.

**Asking back.** If you want them to decide, ask with the right question word:

> How much money have you got on you?
> How many eggs do we need for tomorrow?

**Offering.** *Some* in a question, when you are offering rather than asking:

> Do you want some tea when you get in?

which the checked page gives as "Do you want some chocolate?"

**Keep the verbs right.** Uncountable nouns are singular however much of them there is:

> There's some rice.  ·  The rice is in the blue tin.  ·  There isn't any rice left.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is *some* in a negative:

> There isn't some milk.

A reader understands it and it is not English. The fix: *any* in negatives and questions, *some*
in positives.

The second is the ask with no unit:

> Could you get milk?

which produces the small carton from day 21's basket. The fix: a number and a unit.

The third is mixing the two halves together, so the reader cannot tell what you have from what you
want:

> Rice, eggs, milk, bread, onions.

That is a list, and a list of what — stock, or shopping? The fix: two halves, in sentences, in
order.

The fourth is writing from memory instead of from the cupboard:

> There's some rice.

when the tin has an inch left in it. The message is honest and useless. The fix: write it standing
in front of the shelf.

## In real life

Messages about food are read in a shop, on a phone, one-handed, with a basket. Anything below the
third line is read after they have already picked things up. That is the argument for the order
above, and for keeping the whole thing to four or five sentences.

Fluent writers add one thing beginners leave out: **what to do if it is not there.** *If they
haven't got the brown one, any bread is fine.* One clause, and the message survives the shop not
having the thing — which is the same failure line that protected the note in
[day 17, part 2.3](../../../day-17-linking-words/parts/02-writing/2.3-the-place-word-that-lies.md).

What a reader does with a vague message is buy the cheapest version of everything, because with no
other information that is the reasonable choice. They are not being careless. They are doing
exactly what the message allowed.
