## One-line answer

Copy your message with one of each of the four uncountable errors planted in it, wait, then hunt for them one at a time — the one you miss is the one already in your writing.

## The story

A message goes to a landlord:

> There is some informations about the flat I need. Can you give me an advice about the water? We
> have three milks in the fridge but not much furnitures.

Every sentence is understood. The landlord replies helpfully and thinks nothing more about it, and
the writer never learns that four words in that message all made the same single mistake in four
different disguises.

That is the thing about this error: it is one rule, broken four ways, and each way looks like a
separate problem until you see them together.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below rest on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **plant** — to put something somewhere on purpose so it can be found later.
- **hunt** — to look for one specific thing rather than reading generally.
- **disguise** — a different appearance on the same thing underneath.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.

There are exactly four ways the countable rule is broken, and all four come from
[part 2.1](2.1-things-you-count-and-things-you-do-not.md):

1. **The plural** on an uncountable noun — *three milks*, *informations*.
2. **The *a* or *an*** in front of one — *an advice*, *a bread*.
3. **The wrong question word** — *how many bread*.
4. **The plural verb** — *there are some rice*.

Because there are only four, you can **hunt** for them one at a time, exactly as you did on
day 19. A read-back that hunts finds them; a read-back that reads carefully does not, however
slowly it goes, because your eye is reading for meaning and all four of these mean perfectly
fine things.

So you plant all four in your own message, put it away, and come back to hunt.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing folder's check that can go **RED**. These four errors do not stop anybody
understanding you, which means nothing in the world will ever correct them. A planted hunt is the
only correction available, and doing it once tells you which of the four is invisible to you —
which is the one that will still be there in a year.

## The rule

**Step one: plant.** Take the five-sentence message from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-what-is-in-the-kitchen.md). Copy it with **exactly one of each of the four
errors**, one per sentence where you can. Change nothing else. Label the page **deliberate** and
write the four error names on a slip of paper, folded and hidden.

> There are some rice and there are four eggs.
> There isn't any milks left.
> We've only got a bread.
> How many milk should I get?
> Could you get two litres of milk and a loaf?

**Step two: wait.** Do something else first. The next day is best. A hunt done immediately is
reading.

**Step three: four passes, one error each.**

| Pass | Hunt for | Mark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | an *-s* on a noun that cannot take one | RED |
| 2 | *a* or *an* in front of an uncountable noun | RED |
| 3 | *how many* where *how much* belongs | RED |
| 4 | *are* or *aren't* with an uncountable noun | RED |

Say out loud which pass you are on before you start it. Mark; do not fix. Fixing changes the page
you are reading and the later passes stop being clean.

**Step four: unfold the slip and mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Four found | GREEN. Your hunt works. |
| Three found | RED. The missed one is your blind spot. Write it at the top of your notebook page and hunt for it first from now on. |
| Fewer than three | RED. You read instead of hunting. Wait longer and do the four passes again, one at a time. |

**Step five: restore, and name the rule.** Write the corrected message out in full, marked
**GREEN**, beneath the broken one. Then write one line: the four errors are all the same rule.
Saying that in your own words is what makes them one thing to remember instead of four.

The correct forms were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Nouns: countable and uncountable](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/nouns-countable-uncountable):
"Countable nouns can be counted, e.g. an apple, two apples, three apples, etc."; "Uncountable
nouns cannot be counted, e.g. air, rice, water, etc."; "There's some milk in the fridge"; "There
isn't any coffee"; "How many chairs are there?"; "How much orange juice is there?" The broader
quantifier list was checked at
[Quantifiers](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/quantifiers),
which puts *many* and *a few* with count nouns and *much* and *a little* with uncount nouns.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is planting errors on nouns you would never get wrong. If you plant *two waters*, you
will see it immediately — water is the example everybody learns first. The fix: plant on the ones
from the hard groups — *information*, *advice*, *money*, *furniture*, *news*, *work* — because
those are the ones your own language probably counts.

The second is hunting for all four at once. Four shapes cannot be held at the same time; one can.
The fix: four passes, named out loud.

The third is fixing as you go, which is so natural it happens without deciding. The fix: a pencil
mark only, and a rule that nothing is corrected until step five.

## In real life

These errors survive for years in otherwise good English, precisely because nothing punishes them.
Nobody misunderstands *three milks*, so nobody corrects it, so it becomes a habit that outlives
every harder thing you learn. That is the honest reason this part exists.

Fluent writers hold about twenty of these as phrases, not rules — *some advice*, *a piece of
news*, *how much money*, *not much furniture*. When you catch one in a hunt, learn the whole phrase
rather than the rule behind it. The phrase transfers; the rule has to be recomputed every time.

And the complication from part 2.1 is worth keeping in view, because it will make you doubt a
correct sentence one day: *two coffees* is right in a café, and *a cheese* is right in a shop.
The unit word has been dropped because everybody knows it. That is not the rule breaking; it is a
place where the rule has already been applied.
