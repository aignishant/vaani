# The words that shrink

`PR-10` · Day 22

## The one-line answer

Fluent English is not the full forms with the gaps taken out — it is a second set of shapes for
the same words, and if you have only ever met the printed version, you hear a blur where the
other person hears ordinary words.

## The model

You learned English printed. Every word separate, every letter standing up straight: *going to*,
*want to*, *have got to*, *do you*, *should have*. Then you met English written by hand — joined
up, leaning, one word running into the shoulder of the next — and you could not read it.

That is what happened the last time somebody asked you something and you caught the beginning and
the end and nothing in the middle. It was not a listening failure. You were looking for two words
and there was one shape.

> D'you wanna get something on the way?

Printed, that is *do you want to get something on the way*. Handwritten, it is two beats at the
front and the rest. Nobody is being lazy. This is simply what that sentence looks like written by
hand, and the handwritten version is the one you will meet almost every time.

**The reduced form is the normal one. The full form is the loud one.** This gets taught backwards
and it costs learners years. Say *I am going to speak to him* with every word fully out, and you
have not been careful — you have been emphatic. You sound annoyed, or you sound like you are
reading aloud, or like you are spelling it out for somebody who has already misunderstood twice.
The full forms are not neutral. They carry meaning, and it is usually not the meaning you wanted.

So there are two jobs today, and the first one matters more.

**Hearing them.** You cannot answer a question you did not catch, and the part you keep missing is
the front — because *do you*, *did you*, *are you* all collapse into a single beat and you were
waiting for two words to arrive. Once you know the shape, the beat is enough.

**Saying a few of them.** Not all of them, and not everywhere. Pick the handful in the table and
let the rest come on their own. A learner performing every reduction they have ever heard sounds
stranger than one who reduces nothing.

**And there is one place they will not go.** A weak form cannot hold the end of a phrase. *Yes, I
am* — you cannot say *yes, I'm*. *Where are you from?* — the *from* lands heavy, full, on its own.
The joining happens in the middle of the line; at the end of the line the pen lifts. Reduce a word
that is holding the end of a sentence and the sentence sounds unfinished, and the person listening
will wait for more that never comes.

The other edge is *gonna* and *wanna*. Both are followed by a verb, always. *I'm gonna go.* *I
wanna see it.* Never *I'm gonna the shop*, never *I wanna a coffee* — that is what happens when
the sound gets collected without the shape, and it is instantly audible.

> I'm gonna head off in a bit.
> D'you wanna come, or have you gotta be somewhere?
> It's kinda late, but I dunno.
> I should've said something. I'd 'ave thought they'd ask.

**Today's phrases** — all of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| gonna | Something already decided — and always before a verb |
| wanna | What you or somebody else wants to do — again, before a verb |
| gotta | An obligation you are not making a fuss about |
| kinda | Softening a description you are not fully committed to |
| d'you | The front of almost every spoken question, in one beat |
| should've / would've | Hindsight — the *'ve* is one flick of a v, not a word |
| dunno | The honest no-answer, casual and complete |
| I'd 'ave thought … | Mild disagreement, with the h on *have* gone |

## Say it now

Say each three times, out loud, filling in your own content. Nothing written down.

1. *I'm gonna {{…}} later, and then I've gotta {{…}}.*
2. *D'you wanna {{…}}, or shall we {{…}}?*
3. *It's kinda {{…}} — I dunno, maybe that's just me.*
4. *I should've {{…}}. I'd 'ave thought {{…}}.*

Then the pair drill, twice. Say line 1 fully printed — *I am going to … and then I have got to …*
— and immediately after it the spoken version, back to back, no gap. You are listening for the
difference in weight, not for a difference in how hard you worked.

Then join all four lines into one continuous stretch, twice, without stopping. The seams between
the lines are exactly where the printed forms crawl back in.

Take two is not decoration. *Wanna* and *d'you* barely appear when you talk about yourself; they
live in proposals and objections, and take two is the only place today they get forced out of you.

- **Solo form:** as written. In take two, say the other person's lines as well.
- **With a partner:** in take two they actually resist. They keep re-proposing their plan and you
  may not agree to it.

## The deliberate failure

Record a short stretch in which you reduce **everything**, including the words holding the ends of
your sentences. Say *yes, I'm* instead of *yes, I am*. Answer three short questions about yourself
and let the final word of every answer go soft and thrown-away.

**What breaking sounds like:** the sentence does not land. Every answer either drifts upward or
simply stops, and it sounds like you were interrupted in the middle of a thought — somebody
listening will genuinely wait for you to continue. That is the sound of the pen never lifting at
the end of the line.

Now answer the same three questions again with everything joined up except the last word, which
comes out full and heavy. One word changed. Notice that the answers now close — and notice that
closing is not politeness or confidence, it is a weight you put on a syllable.

## When it breaks in the wild

Somebody asks you something at ordinary speed, you miss it, you ask them to repeat — and they
repeat it printed. Every word separate and slow. You understand it perfectly, you answer, and you
walk away having learned nothing, because the version you understood is not the version they said.
Do that often enough and you conclude your listening is bad. It is not. You have been collecting
the printed version of sentences you will only ever meet handwritten.

**The repair:** you already own *Sorry, I missed that last bit* from Day 3. Use it — and then do
the second half, which is the part everybody skips.

> Sorry, I missed that last bit.

After they repeat it, say the spoken version back to yourself once, under your breath. Not the
careful one they just handed you — the one they said the first time, now that you know what was
inside it. That is how a shape gets into your ear instead of into a notebook.
