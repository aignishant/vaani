## One-line answer

Ask *which way* rather than *where*, name the place you are trying to reach, and add one closing question — because an open question gets you a long answer you cannot hold, and a narrow one gets you a sentence you can walk on.

## The story

You are lost. You stop somebody and say:

> Excuse me, where is the hospital?

And he is helpful. Very helpful. He tells you about the roundabout, and the road that used to be
called something else, and the shortcut through the market that is closed on Sundays, and two
minutes later he walks off and you are standing in exactly the same place with a head full of
information and no idea which way to start walking.

He answered your question perfectly. *Where is* asks for a location, and a location is a
description. What you needed was a direction, and that is a different question.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **direction** — which way to go from where you are standing.
- **landmark** — a thing you can see that marks a place: a bank, a bridge, a petrol station.
- **narrow question** — a question with only a few possible answers.
- **roundabout** — a circle in the road that traffic goes round.

The shape of your question decides the shape of their answer, and most lost people ask the
question that produces the least useful reply.

| You ask | You get |
| --- | --- |
| Where is the hospital? | a description of where it is in the city |
| Is it far? | yes or no |
| Which way is the hospital? | a direction |
| How do I get to the hospital from here? | a route, starting from here |

The last one is the good question. Three things make it work: it asks for a **route**, not a
location; it says **from here**, which stops them telling you about the city; and it names the
place.

Then one more question closes it:

> Is it walking distance?

That tells you whether the answer you are about to get is a walk or a bus, which changes
everything about how you listen.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn role-play, and a direction exchange is the most natural way to
reach twelve turns honestly: the ask, the answer, the repeat-back, the correction, the close. This
part is the first of those turns, and getting it wrong makes the rest of the exchange harder than
it needs to be.

## The rule

**The question, in three pieces.**

```text
   Excuse me —    how do I get to    the station    from here?
   ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾     ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾    ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾    ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
   attention      asks for a route   the place      stops the city tour
```

The attention move is from
[day 21, part 1.1](../../../day-21-at-the-shop/parts/01-speaking/1.1-excuse-me-and-the-ask.md) and
it matters more here than in a shop, because you are interrupting somebody who was going
somewhere.

**The closing question.** One of these, straight after:

> Is it walking distance?
> Is it far?
> Am I going the right way?

That last one is the best question in this whole part, because it can be answered with one word
while you are already walking.

**What their answer will be made of.** Three kinds of thing, and knowing the three is what lets
you catch a fast answer:

| Kind | Words you will hear |
| --- | --- |
| turns | turn left, turn right, take the first left, go straight on |
| movement | keep going, carry on, go past, go through, go up, go down |
| landmarks | past the bank, opposite the church, next to the school, at the lights |

The movement and turn words were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Adverbials of direction](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adverbials-direction),
which lists "across, along, back, back to, down, into, onto, out of, past, through, to, towards"
and gives the model line: "Walk **past the bank** and keep going **to the end of the street**."
The landmark words were checked at
[Adverbials of location](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adverbials-location):
"behind, between, by, in, near, next to, on, opposite, outside, over, under."

**Landmarks, not distances.** This is the thing to ask for, and to give:

> "Is there something I'll see?"

A landmark can be checked while you walk. A distance cannot — you cannot tell while walking
whether you have gone the amount they said. So when their answer has no landmark in it, ask for
one, and do not accept a time estimate as a substitute for something you can see.

**If you do not know the place's name.** Describe it:

> I'm looking for a chemist. Is there one near here?

The shape *is there a … near here?* is the version of the question for when you do not have a
name, and it is often the more useful one.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the open question in the story:

> Where is the hospital?

You get a description. The fix: *how do I get to* and *from here*.

The second is the question with no place name, because you are embarrassed about the
pronunciation:

> Excuse me, this address?

holding up a phone. It works, and it gets you a pointed finger and no words at all — which means
no chance to check, no repeat-back, and no second question. The fix: say the name, badly if
necessary, and show the phone as well.

The third is asking somebody who does not know. Not everybody on a street is local, and a person
who does not know will often still try. The fix: one question first — *sorry, do you know this
area?* — which makes it easy for them to say no.

The fourth is asking while walking past. You will get half an answer, because they are being
overtaken. The fix: stop, and let them stop.

## In real life

Answers come fast and reduced. *Take the first left* is *tekthuh firs lef*, and *at the traffic
lights* is *the lights*. What survives at speed are the **content words** from
[day 13, part 1.1](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/01-speaking/1.1-loud-words-and-quiet-words.md)
— *left*, *past*, *bank*, *end* — and those are enough to walk on. Listening for whole sentences
is what makes a real answer feel impossible.

Fluent speakers giving directions stop at two or three steps, because they know you cannot hold
more. If somebody gives you six, they are not being thorough; they have forgotten what it is like
to be lost. Take the first two and ask again later — which is normal, and is what everybody does.

What a helpful stranger notices is whether you are still with them. They watch your face, and if
you look blank they either start again more slowly or give up and point. Neither is a disaster,
but the second one ends the exchange, and pointing is usually wrong by about one street.
