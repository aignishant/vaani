## One-line answer

After they answer, say the route back to them in your own words, short — because the only moment you can be corrected is while they are still standing there.

## The story

Somebody gives you directions and you understand them. Left at the lights, past the school, second
right. You say thanks and walk.

At the lights you turn left. You pass a school. You take the second right and you are in a lane
with no shops and no station and no way to tell whether you went wrong at the school, at the
lights, or at the counting.

Ten seconds earlier there was a person standing in front of you who knew the answer. The repeat-
back is not politeness and it is not a study technique. It is the last moment at which being wrong
is free.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **repeat-back** — saying what you heard back to the person, in short form, to check it.
- **route** — the way from here to there, as a series of steps.
- **confirm** — to say that something is right.
- **lane** — a narrow road.

A repeat-back is three or four words per step and nothing else:

> So — left at the lights, past the school, second right?

It is a **question**, with the voice going up at the end, and it does three things at once.

It **checks**. If you heard *second right* and they said *second left*, this is where it comes out.

It **finishes the exchange properly**. They say "that's it" and you both know you are done.

And it **holds the route in your head**, because saying something out loud is how you remember it.
This is the same reason the reps in this course are spoken rather than read.

The hard part is not the language. It is that repeating something back feels like admitting you
might not have understood, and most people would rather walk off unsure than do that.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn role-play, and the repeat-back is where the turns come from: the
ask, their answer, your repeat-back, their correction, your close. Without it a direction exchange
is three turns and over. With it, it is a conversation — which is what the gate is actually
asking for.

## The rule

**Step one: take only what you can hold.** Two or three steps. If they give six, stop them:

> Sorry — as far as the bank first?

Cutting somebody off mid-directions feels rude and is completely normal. The alternative is
getting all six and keeping none.

**Step two: the repeat-back, short.** Three or four words a step, ending on a rise:

> So — left at the lights, past the school, second right?
> Straight on to the end, then right at the church?

Not a full sentence each. A full-sentence repeat-back takes so long that they start giving you
more directions while you are doing it.

**Step three: listen for the correction.** This is the whole purpose. You will get one of three
answers:

| They say | It means |
| --- | --- |
| "That's it." / "Yeah, that's right." | you have it |
| "No, second *left*." | the repeat-back just saved you |
| "Well — you can, but it's easier if you…" | there is a better route; take it |

**Step four: the last question.** One of these, and it is worth more than another step of route:

> And I'll see it from there?
> What will I see when I'm close?

A landmark at the end tells you when to stop looking, which is the part of every direction that
gets left out.

**When you did not catch it.** One phrase, once. These were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Checking understanding](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding):

> Sorry, could you say that again, please?
> Can you repeat that more slowly, please?
> I'm sorry?

And the one that is better than all three, because it tells them exactly where you got lost:

> Sorry — left or right at the lights?

**How a real answer is built.** The words to expect were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Adverbials of direction](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adverbials-direction)
— "Walk **past the bank** and keep going **to the end of the street**" — and the way a speaker
chains landmarks at
[Finding the library](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/finding-library):
"See the big building over there? OK, so that's the lecture theatre. Next to that, on the right,
is the registration office. And next to that is the library."

Notice that the second one is almost entirely landmarks and almost no turns. Many real answers
are like that, and the repeat-back for a landmark answer is the landmarks in order:

> So — past the big building, then the registration office, and it's the next one?

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is thanks instead of a repeat-back:

> "…second right." — "Thank you so much!"

Warm, polite, and you are now alone with a route you have not checked. The fix: the same *thank
you*, after the repeat-back rather than instead of it.

The second is the repeat-back that is too long:

> "So if I understand correctly, I should go to the traffic lights and then I turn to my left and
> I continue walking until I see the school…"

By the middle of it they have started talking again. The fix: three or four words a step.

The third is repeating back only the first step, because that is all you held:

> "So — left at the lights?"

That is not a failure. It is the correct move when you only caught one step, and you follow it
with *and then?* The fix is to know that a partial repeat-back is allowed and useful.

The fourth is a flat repeat-back:

> So. Left at the lights. Past the school. Second right.

No rise, so it is not a question, so they do not know they are being asked to confirm — and you
lose the correction. The fix: take the last word up.

## In real life

People giving directions are often wrong, and a repeat-back catches their mistakes as well as
yours. Hearing their own route said back is what makes somebody say "actually, no, it's the one
after the school." That correction exists only because you said it back.

Fluent speakers repeat back constantly, and not only for directions — phone numbers, times,
addresses, instructions at work. It is one of the most portable habits in this whole course, and
it is what separates people who make few mistakes from people who are careful.

Under pressure — a loud street, a person in a hurry — the repeat-back gets shorter, down to the
one piece you are least sure about: *second right?* That is a complete and perfectly normal
utterance, and it gets you a yes or a no.
