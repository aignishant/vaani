## One-line answer

Record a long, fast set of directions, answer it the way you really would — *yes, yes, thank you* — then try to draw the route from your own recording and count how far you get.

## The story

Somebody gives you six steps at speed. Around step three you lose it. You know you have lost it.
And you say:

> Yes. Yes. Okay. Thank you so much.

You are not lying and you are not being lazy. Stopping somebody mid-flow in a language you are
still learning is genuinely hard, and *yes* is the cheapest way to make a difficult moment end.

The cost arrives twenty minutes later, in the wrong part of a city, and by then the person who
had the answer is long gone.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **nod** — to move your head up and down to show you agree or understand.
- **bluff** — to act as though you know something when you do not.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **draw the route** — to put the steps on paper as a line with turns.

Everybody does this. It is not a beginner's mistake in the sense of being ignorant — people do it
in their own language, at work, in meetings, with doctors. Saying *yes* ends a moment of
discomfort now and buys a bigger problem later.

What makes it hard to fix is that you cannot see it from inside. At the moment you say *yes*, you
half believe you will work it out. So this part makes it visible by putting a piece of paper
between you and the belief.

You record a long fast answer, respond the way you actually would, and then — later — try to
**draw the route** from your own recording. The line stops somewhere. That stopping point is the
step where *yes* replaced understanding, and it is always earlier than you expect.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking folder's check that can go **RED**. The nod is the single most expensive
habit a language learner has, because it hides every other gap: as long as you nod, nobody ever
finds out what you did not catch, including you.

## The rule

**Step one: build a hard answer.** Write a set of directions that is genuinely too long — six or
seven steps, with a counted turn (*the second left*), a landmark you would not know, and one step
that depends on the one before. Write it so it is hard, because an easy one tests nothing.

> Go straight on past the bank, then take the second left — not the first, that's one-way — carry
> on to the end, right at the church, and it's just past the place where the market used to be,
> opposite the green gates.

**Step two: record it fast, and answer it honestly.** Say the whole thing at speed in the
stranger's voice, then answer in your own voice **the way you really would when you are tired and
lost**. Not a performance of being bad — your actual reaction. Label the take **deliberate**.

**Step three: wait, then draw.** Later, play only your own side and the stranger's answer once,
and draw the route: a line with turns and labels. Stop drawing at the first place you cannot
decide, and mark that spot **X**.

**Step four: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Your line stops at step 3 or earlier | GREEN. You have found where the nod starts, and it is a real number. |
| Your line completes | RED. Either the answer was not hard enough, or you drew from memory of writing it. Make it harder, or have somebody else say it. |
| You did not nod at all in the recording | RED, and worth checking honestly: was that your real reaction, or the one you knew was being marked? |

**Step five: record it again, with the interruptions in.** Same fast answer. This time stop the
stranger at the first step you lose, using one of these:

> Sorry — second left or first?
> Sorry, can I stop you there? Past the bank, and then?
> Sorry — what was after the church?

Then repeat back what you have, short and rising, and take the rest.

**Step six: the finding.** One line: **at which step did the nod start, and what did it cost?**
Then one more: the exact sentence you will use next time to interrupt. Having the sentence ready
is what makes interrupting possible — the reason people nod is usually that they do not have a
prepared way not to.

The interrupting phrases were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Checking understanding](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding):
"I'm sorry?"; "I don't understand"; "Could you say that again, please?"; "Can you repeat that more
slowly, please?"; and the clarifying shape "Do you mean the hammer?"

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is performing the nod rather than doing it. You put on a exaggerated *yes yes yes* that
nobody would ever say, and the finding becomes "obvious bluffing does not work". The fix: answer
as you actually would — a small *yes*, a *right*, a *thank you so much* — because the real version
is quiet and convincing.

The second is making the directions easy enough to hold. Then you draw the whole route, mark
GREEN, and have learned nothing. The fix: six steps, one counted turn, one unknown landmark, said
fast.

The third is treating the result as a character problem — deciding you are dishonest or timid. It
is neither. It is a missing sentence. Step five exists because the fix for nodding is having a
ready-made interruption, not being braver.

## In real life

The nod is expensive because it removes the evidence. Every other mistake in this course produces
a wrong result somebody notices: a wrong basket, a wrong cupboard, a misheard *can't*. The nod
produces a perfectly smooth conversation and a bad outcome later, with nothing connecting the two.

Fluent speakers interrupt directions all the time, and nobody minds. *Sorry — left or right?* is
so ordinary that the person giving directions expects it, and often pauses to let it happen. The
silence after step two in a real answer is not them finishing; it is them checking whether you
need to stop them.

What a helpful stranger does when you nod through everything is stop giving detail — because
somebody who understands everything does not need the bit about the one-way street. So the nod
does not just fail to get you help. It actively reduces the help you are offered.
