## One-line answer

Written directions are numbered steps with one instruction each, every step ending at something the reader can see, and a last line telling them how they will know they have arrived.

## The story

You send somebody the way to your place:

> Come out of the station and go left, then it's the road after the big junction, you'll see a
> chemist, take the lane next to it and we're the blue gate at the end, if you get to the temple
> you've gone too far.

Every fact in that is correct. It is also one sentence with five instructions in it, sent to
somebody standing outside a station holding a bag, and they will read it three times and still ask
you to call.

The information was all there. It was not in a shape a person can follow while walking.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **step** — one instruction in a set of directions.
- **junction** — where two or more roads meet.
- **landmark** — a thing you can see that marks a place: a chemist, a bridge, a gate.
- **arrive** — to get there.
- **lane** — a narrow road.

Written directions are not a description of a route. They are a set of **steps**, and a step has
exactly two parts:

| Part | Example |
| --- | --- |
| what to do | Turn left out of the station. |
| what you will see when you have done it | You'll see a chemist on the corner. |

The second part is what makes directions written rather than remembered. A reader following your
steps needs to know, at the end of each one, whether they have done it right — and a **landmark**
is the only thing that tells them.

And the last step is not a step at all. It is the arrival line:

> Ours is the blue gate at the end, on the right.

Plus, if there is one, the overshoot line:

> If you reach the temple, you've gone too far.

That line is worth more than any other single sentence you can write, because it is the only one
that helps a reader who has already gone wrong.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate asks for a written note that asks for something, thanks somebody **and gives a
direction**. The direction half is this part exactly. Written directions are also the most common
piece of practical writing anybody does — to a visitor, a delivery driver, a friend coming over —
and they fail in one predictable way, which is the story above.

## The rule

**Numbered steps, one instruction each.**

```text
   1. Come out of the station and turn left.
   2. Walk past the bank and keep going to the end of the street.
   3. Turn right at the chemist, into the lane.
   4. We're the blue gate at the end, on the right.

   If you reach the temple, you've gone too far.
```

**One instruction per step.** Two instructions in one step is the commonest failure and the reader
does the first and forgets the second:

| One step | Two steps |
| --- | --- |
| Turn right at the chemist. | Turn right at the chemist and go past the school. |

**Every step ends at something visible.** Not a distance and not a time. A reader cannot check a
distance while walking, and a time depends on how fast they walk and whether they are carrying
anything. They can check a bank.

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| Walk a long way down the road. | Walk to the end of the road, past the bank. |
| It's not far after that. | It's the second turning after the chemist. |

**Use the movement and turn words.** These were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Adverbials of direction](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adverbials-direction),
which lists "across, along, back, back to, down, into, onto, out of, past, through, to, towards"
and gives the model: "Walk **past the bank** and keep going **to the end of the street**." The
landmark words come from
[Adverbials of location](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adverbials-location):
"behind, between, by, in, near, next to, on, opposite, outside, over, under."

**Say where the reader is starting from.** A direction with no starting point is a description,
the same failure as a room with no anchor. Put it in the first line:

> From the station:

**Steps, not sentences joined by *and*.** This is a list, so it follows day 21's list rules
([day 21, part 2.1](../../../day-21-at-the-shop/parts/02-writing/2.1-a-list-is-not-a-note.md)):
one item per line, scannable, numbered. But unlike a shopping list, each line here **is** a
sentence, with a capital and a full stop, because it is an instruction to be read rather than
scanned.

The model for numbered written instructions was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Instructions for a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/instructions-colleague),
whose model puts each instruction on its own line, gives the reason behind it, and ends by
inviting questions.

**The count of steps.** Four or five. If your route needs nine, either the reader needs a map or
you should start them somewhere nearer — a bus stop, a junction they will recognise — rather than
writing nine steps nobody follows to the end.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the run-on in the story. Five instructions, one sentence, no
numbers. The fix is mechanical: put a number and a full stop wherever there is an *and then*.

The second is the step with no landmark:

> Go down the road and turn right.

Which road, which right, and how will they know? The fix: end the step at something visible —
*turn right at the chemist*.

The third is the missing arrival line:

> 4. Turn into the lane.

And then what? The reader is in a lane with no idea which building. The fix: the last line
describes the door, the gate or the sign.

The fourth is directions written from your own habits:

> Come the usual way from the bridge.

which only works for somebody who has already been. This is day 21's shorthand in a new place. The
fix: write for somebody who has never come.

## In real life

Written directions are read on a phone, outdoors, in one hand, often in bad light, by somebody who
is already slightly late. Anything that requires scrolling back is not read. That is the whole
argument for numbered steps: a reader can find their place again after looking up at the street.

Fluent writers add one thing beginners never do — **what the reader will pass that is wrong**.
*You'll pass a second chemist; it's not that one.* Naming the wrong turning is often more useful
than describing the right one, because that is where people actually go astray.

What a reader does with directions they cannot follow is phone you, and then you give them the
directions again, by voice, badly, while they stand in traffic. Ten minutes of that is what four
numbered lines were meant to prevent.
