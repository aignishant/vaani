## One-line answer

Write the route in steps, then test it by walking it in your head as somebody who has never been — and the test that finds the most is doing it backwards, step by step, asking at each one what you can actually see.

## The story

You write four clean numbered steps to your door and send them. They are correct. The person still
calls from the corner, because step three says *turn right at the chemist* and the chemist closed
last year and the sign has gone.

You walk past that corner every day. Your eye stopped seeing the shop and kept using it as a
landmark, because it is a landmark in your head rather than on the street.

Directions go out of date, and the writer is the last person to notice.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **test** — to check something by trying it, not by looking at it.
- **backwards** — from the end to the beginning.
- **out of date** — no longer true, because something changed.
- **visible** — able to be seen from where the reader is standing.

Written directions cannot be checked by reading them, because you know the route. They have to be
**tested**, and there are two tests that actually find things.

**Test one: the visibility test.** Take each step one at a time and ask a single question: *what
can a stranger standing here actually see?* Not what is there — what is **visible**, from the
pavement, by somebody who does not know it is there. A chemist with no sign is not visible. A
bank is. A blue gate is.

**Test two: walking it backwards.** Start at step four and go to step one. This breaks your
memory of the route, which is what makes it work. Reading forwards, your head fills in every gap
before you notice it; reading backwards, the gaps stay open.

Both tests are done in your head, or on the pavement if you happen to be passing. Neither takes
long, and between them they find the two things that break real directions: landmarks that are not
visible, and steps that only make sense if you already did the one before.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate note has to give a direction to somebody who is not standing next to you. It will
be read once, acted on immediately, and either work or generate a phone call. A direction you
tested is the only kind that has a chance.

## The rule

**Step one: write the route.** Four or five numbered steps in the shape from
[part 2.1](2.1-one-instruction-per-step.md), with a starting line, an arrival line and an
overshoot line.

```text
   From the station:
   1. Come out of the main entrance and turn left.
   2. Walk past the bank and keep going to the end of the street.
   3. Turn right into the lane, opposite the green postbox.
   4. We're the blue gate at the end, on the right.

   If you reach the temple, you've gone too far.
```

**Step two: the visibility test, step by step.**

| For each step, ask | If the answer is no |
| --- | --- |
| Can a stranger see the landmark from where this step starts? | Replace it with one they can. |
| Is there more than one of it nearby? | Add which one — *the second bank*, *the green postbox*. |
| Was it there last month? | Check, or use something that does not move. |

The best landmarks are the ones that cannot close, move or repaint: a bridge, a junction, a
postbox, a level crossing, a big tree, the end of a road. Shops are the worst, and they are what
everybody reaches for first.

**Step three: walk it backwards.** Read step 4, then 3, then 2, then 1. At each one ask: *does
this step tell me what to do without needing the step before it?* A step that only works in
sequence is fine; a step that only works if you **remembered** the one before is not.

**Step four: the two lines beginners leave out.**

> You'll pass a second chemist — it's not that one.
> If you reach the temple, you've gone too far.

The first names the wrong turning. The second rescues somebody who has already overshot. Between
them they cover the two ways people actually go astray.

**Step five: say where you are.** The starting line at the top. *From the station.* *From the bus
stand.* A route with no start is a description, not directions.

The model for written instructions was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Instructions for a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/instructions-colleague),
which puts each instruction on its own line, gives the reason behind it, and closes by inviting
questions — and the direction words at
[Adverbials of direction](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adverbials-direction):
"Walk **past the bank** and keep going **to the end of the street**."

**Add one line at the end.** From the same page's tips: invite the question.

> Call me when you're at the bank if you're not sure.

That single line turns a direction that might fail into one that fails safely.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the shop as a landmark:

> Turn right at the chemist.

Shops close, change name and take their signs down. The fix: a postbox, a junction, a bridge — or
the shop **plus** something permanent: *turn right at the lane, just after the chemist*.

The second is the step that depends on remembering:

> 3. Take the second one.

Second what? The reader has forgotten what step 2 was counting. The fix: repeat the noun — *take
the second lane*.

The third is directions with no starting line, sent to somebody who might arrive from either
direction. Half your readers will do everything backwards. The fix: name the start.

The fourth is testing by reading it forwards and finding nothing wrong. You will find nothing
wrong; you know the way. The fix: backwards, and the visibility question asked out loud.

## In real life

Directions are used by delivery drivers, visitors and people arriving in the dark, and the dark is
worth thinking about: half your landmarks are invisible at night. A written route that works only
in daylight is a route that fails exactly when somebody most needs it.

Fluent writers give a route **and** a fallback: the steps, plus *or just tell the driver it's
opposite the school*. The second version is for somebody who gave up on the first, and giving it
to them costs one line.

What a reader does with directions that failed once is stop using them and phone. They will not
tell you which step was wrong, so the direction stays broken and you resend it to the next person.
That is why the tests happen before sending, not after the phone call.
