## One-line answer

Rewrite your route with two instructions crammed into one step, the landmarks taken out and the arrival line deleted, then follow it literally on paper and mark the exact point where the reader is lost.

## The story

Four steps become three:

> 1. Come out of the station, turn left and walk to the end.
> 2. Turn right into the lane and go to the end.
> 3. It's the gate.

Everything in the original is still there. The words *past the bank*, *opposite the green postbox*
and *the blue gate on the right* have gone, and two steps now carry two instructions each.

A reader gets to the end of a street, turns right into one of three lanes, walks to the end of it
and stands in front of four gates. Nothing in the directions was false. There was simply nothing in
them that could be checked.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **cram** — to push too much into a small space.
- **literally** — doing exactly what the words say and nothing more.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **lost point** — the step at which a reader can no longer tell where to go.

You have a tested route ([part 2.2](2.2-write-the-way-to-your-door.md)). This part finds out
which of the things you put in it were actually carrying the reader, and it does that by taking
three of them away at once:

1. **Cram two instructions into a step.**
2. **Delete every landmark.**
3. **Delete the arrival line and the overshoot line.**

Then you follow the broken route **literally** — doing exactly what each step says, and nothing
you happen to know — and mark the **lost point**: the first step where a reader could be standing
somewhere wrong and not know it.

Being literal is the whole skill. Your instinct will be to read *turn right into the lane* and
picture your lane. A literal reading asks: *how many lanes are there, and does anything here tell
me which?*

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing folder's check that can go **RED**. Directions are the piece of writing in
phase 2 that most obviously either works or does not, and this is the only way to find the lost
point before a real person finds it standing in the street.

## The rule

**Step one: break the route, three ways.** Copy your part 2.2 route with all three changes made.
Do not change any turn and do not make anything false. Label it **deliberate**.

> From the station:
> 1. Come out, turn left and walk to the end.
> 2. Turn right into the lane and go to the end.
> 3. It's the gate.

**Step two: follow it literally, step by step.** For each step, write two things:

| For each step | Write |
| --- | --- |
| What exactly does this tell me to do? | |
| Standing here, could I be in the wrong place and not know? | |

Stop at the first step where the second answer is *yes*. Mark that step **X**. That is the lost
point.

**Step three: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| The lost point is at step 1 or 2 | GREEN. Landmarks and single instructions were carrying the whole route. |
| The lost point is at the last step only | RED. Your original route may have been thin — check whether it had landmarks at every step, or whether you are reading it with local knowledge. |
| There is no lost point | RED. You are following it as somebody who knows the way. Give it to another person, or read it as though you had arrived from the opposite direction. |

**Step four: count what each change cost.** Restore the three things one at a time and note where
the lost point moves to:

| Restore | Lost point moves to |
| --- | --- |
| the landmarks | |
| one instruction per step | |
| the arrival and overshoot lines | |

This table is the useful part. It tells you which of the three you can least afford to leave out —
and for most routes it is the landmarks, by a long way.

**Step five: restore fully and mark GREEN.** Write the complete route out again with all three
back in, plus the wrong-turning line and the call-me line. Keep the broken version underneath.

**Step six: the finding.** One line: where the lost point was, and which single restoration moved
it furthest.

— one instruction per line, the reason given, questions invited — and the direction words at

"Walk **past the bank** and keep going **to the end of the street**." The chained-landmark style
of a real spoken answer was checked at
[Finding the library](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/finding-library):
"Next to that, on the right, is the registration office. And next to that is the library."

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is breaking the route by making it wrong — changing a left to a right. That is a
different failure and it teaches nothing about shape: a reader finds a wrong turn quickly, because
the world stops matching. The fix: keep every instruction true. The point is that true is not
enough.

The second is following it with local knowledge. You read *turn right into the lane* and your feet
know which lane. The fix: the literal question — *how many of these are there, and what tells me
which?* — written down, not thought.

The third is skipping step four because the route already failed. Step four is where the actual
information is: knowing that landmarks matter three times as much as step-splitting changes how
you write every direction afterwards. The fix: restore one at a time and write down where the
lost point moves.

## In real life

Real directions are followed literally by people who have no choice — a driver at night, a
visitor with a bag, somebody who has never been to your city. They cannot fill in a gap, because
they have nothing to fill it with. Everything you left implicit is simply missing for them.

Fluent writers overspecify exactly the steps where the world offers a choice — three lanes, two
chemists, a fork — and leave the obvious ones alone. *Come out and turn left* needs nothing.
*Turn right into the lane* needs two more words. Knowing which steps need protecting is the
judgement this exercise builds.

What a lost reader does is call, and what they say is "I'm at the end of a road" — which is a
sentence that tells you nothing, because they do not know which road they are on. That is the real
cost of a missing landmark: not just that they are lost, but that neither of you can work out
where they are.
