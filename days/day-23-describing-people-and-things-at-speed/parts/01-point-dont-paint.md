# Point, don't paint

`FN-06` · Day 23

## The one-line answer

When you describe a person or a thing out loud you are pointing at it across a room, not painting
it — and the exact adjective you keep hunting for is almost never the thing the other person
needed.

## The model

Watch what happens when somebody asks you *what's your manager like?* or *what is that, exactly?*
Somewhere in the second sentence you stop. Not because you have run out of things to say. Because
you have gone looking for a word. You almost have it. You can nearly feel the shape of it. And
while you look, everything else stops with you.

That stall has a cause and the cause is not vocabulary. You went painting.

A painter has to reproduce the thing. Somebody pointing only has to pick it out from everything
else in the room. Nearly every description you are ever asked for is a pointing job, and pointing
is cheap — it is done with words you have owned for years.

**Point by what they do, or what they have.** *He's the one who …* and *She's the one with the …*
are whole clauses with no adjective anywhere inside them. That is the trick: a relative clause is
a description made of verbs and nouns, and verbs and nouns are the words that arrive on time.

**Point by what it is like.** *It's a bit like a …, but …* hands the listener something they
already have and then changes one part of it. The sharper version is *Think a …, but …* — you
build the new thing out of an old one in a single breath.

**Point by what it is for.** *It's the kind of thing you …* describes an object by the action it
belongs to. Everything in your kitchen can be described this way in one sentence, and nothing in
your kitchen needs two.

**People get a fourth: what they care about.** *She's really into …* does more work than five
adjectives, because it does not tell the listener what the person is like — it tells them what a
conversation with that person would be like, which is what they were actually asking.

**And then the permission to stop.** *Nothing special, just …* is a complete, fluent, adult
answer. Not every noun deserves a description. Over-describing a dull object is one of the
clearest signs of a speaker who thinks they are being tested, and it sounds odder than saying
almost nothing.

One frame does need an adjective, and you should keep it: *He can be a bit …*. This is the
softened negative about a person. The frame carries all the softening, which means you can put an
ordinary blunt word in the slot and it still comes out fair.

> He's the one who always ends up organising things.
> It's a bit like a kettle, but you leave it on the table.
> It's the kind of thing you use once and then forget you own.
> She's really into old films. Nothing special, just a lot of them.

**Today's phrases** — all of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| He's the one who … | Picking one person out of several, by what they do |
| She's the one with the … | Same job, by something a stranger could see |
| It's a bit like a … | The word will not come, so you compare instead |
| Think a …, but … | Building the thing out of one the listener already knows |
| It's the kind of thing you … | Describing an object by what it is for |
| She's really into … | A person in one stroke, by what they care about |
| He can be a bit … | The softened negative — the frame does the softening |
| Nothing special, just … | Refusing to over-describe something ordinary |

## Say it now

Say each three times, out loud, with your own content. Nothing written down.

1. *He's the one who {{…}}. She's the one with the {{…}}.*
2. *It's a bit like a {{…}}, but {{…}}.*
3. *Think a {{…}}, but {{…}}. It's the kind of thing you {{…}}.*
4. *She's really into {{…}}. He can be a bit {{…}}, though.*
5. *Nothing special, just {{…}}.*

Then pick one person you know and one object in front of you, and describe both in a single
continuous stretch with no pause where you change from the person to the object. That join is
exactly where you would normally go hunting, and holding it is the whole exercise.

## The deliberate failure

Take one more object and describe it with **no frames at all**. Single adjectives only, and you
are not allowed to move on until you have found the right one.

**What breaking sounds like:** the stall has a shape, and it is the same shape every time. *It's…
it's quite… it's really…* — the sentence stops in one place, one word short of the end, over and
over. Count them. They all land immediately before the adjective.

Now describe the same object with the frames restored. Notice that the word you were hunting never
arrives and is never missed — which tells you that spot was never a hole in your vocabulary. It is
the place a frame goes.

## When it breaks in the wild

Somebody asks what your closest colleague is like, and out comes a string that could be anybody:
*she's nice, she's very good, she's kind.* All true. The listener has nothing to hold on to and
nothing to ask about, so they say "oh, nice", and the topic closes.

You did not fail to describe her. You described her so generally that you described everyone, and
a description that fits everyone points at nobody.

**The repair:**

> She's the one who {{…}} — that's her, really.

One clause, one specific thing she actually does, and she is suddenly in the room. It works
several sentences late, because you are not correcting anything — you are just adding the detail
you skipped.
