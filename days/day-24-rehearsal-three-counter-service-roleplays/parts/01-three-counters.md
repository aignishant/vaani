# Three counters, and what each one is testing

`CF-07` · Day 24

## The one-line answer

A counter does not stop for you, so today is not a test of ordering — it is a test of changing
your mind, missing what was said, and being told no, without stepping out of the exchange.

## The model

You already know how to ask. Day 19 gave you *Could I get …*, *Have you got any …*, *I'll take …*
and *Is it alright if I …*, and between them they cover the asking half of almost any counter you
will ever stand at. None of that is what today tests. Today tests what happens after the ask,
when something goes wrong.

Think of a counter as a belt that is already moving. Once you are at the front it does not stop
while you sort yourself out — the person behind the counter is already reaching for something,
somebody is waiting behind you, and the whole thing keeps running whether you are ready or not.
You cannot pause it. You can only steer it while it runs. Every phrase in this part is a way of
steering.

Three roleplays. Each one drops a different thing onto the belt.

**One — the order you change halfway through.** You have said what you want and then you change
your mind. The instinct is to abandon the sentence and start it again from the top, and that is
the single most common way a simple order turns into a scene. A fluent speaker never restarts.
They patch the sentence while it is still moving: *Actually, make that …* — three words, and the
order continues from where it was.

**Two — the answer you did not catch.** They say a total, or ask you something you half-hear.
Almost everyone says yes here and hopes, and that is how you end up paying for something you did
not want. Two moves, and the second is the one people skip. Ask for it again — you already own
*Sorry, I missed that last bit* from Day 3, and today's version buys you the same thing more
slowly. Then, if the repeat is no clearer, say the number or the word back to them to check.

**Three — the thing they haven't got.** You ask; they do not have it. Now you have to choose
again, out loud, with everyone waiting. In real life this produces the longest silence of the
three, and the fix is one small phrase whose only job is to cover the moment you are choosing in:
*Ah right — {{…}} then.* It is not clever. It fills the gap, and it keeps the belt moving.

There is a fourth thing all three test, and it is not trouble at all. **Ending it.** Learners
finish the business of an exchange and then stand there, waiting to be released, because nobody
ever taught them the line that closes it. Part 02 carries that line, and the ones for when you
have been handed the wrong thing.

> Could I get the one on the left — actually, make that two.
> Sorry, could you say that a bit slower?
> Ah right — the other one then. And sorry, one more thing —
> Whatever's easiest.

**Today's phrases** — these and part 02's go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Actually, make that … | Changing your order without restarting the sentence |
| Sorry, could you say that a bit slower? | You caught that they said something, not what |
| Ah right — {{…}} then. | They have not got it and you are choosing again out loud |
| Sorry, one more thing — | Adding something after you thought you had finished |
| Whatever's easiest. | A choice you genuinely do not care about |
| Sorry, my mistake. | You gave the wrong answer and you are fixing it on the spot |

## Say it now

Say each three times, out loud, with your own content. Nothing written down.

1. *Could I get {{…}} — actually, make that {{…}}.*
2. *Sorry, could you say that a bit slower?*
3. *Ah right — {{…}} then. And sorry, one more thing — {{…}}.*
4. *{{…}}, whatever's easiest.*
5. *Sorry, my mistake — I meant {{…}}.*

Then run one whole exchange, both voices, from your first ask to the moment you would walk away,
without stopping — and get all five lines into it. The exchange will be ridiculous. Somebody who
changes their mind, mishears, adds something and then corrects themselves does not exist. Run it
anyway: the point is the joins between the lines, and the joins only exist when the lines are
crowded together.

> person's lines into your shoulder, half under your breath, so that you genuinely miss them.
> Then get the exchange to a finish anyway.

Do not decide in advance what the counter person says. If you know it, you cannot miss it, and
missing it is the whole rep.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** they say their lines facing away from you, and they are allowed to be
  unhelpful — repeating the same words at the same volume as before.

## The deliberate failure

Run roleplay one, but change your order by **starting the whole sentence again** every time. You
are not allowed to patch it. Change your mind twice.

**What breaking sounds like:** *Could I get a — sorry, could I get — no, actually, sorry, could I
get …* The apologies stack up, the sentence never gets past its own opening, and by the third
attempt you sound like somebody in trouble rather than somebody choosing a drink. Count the
sorries.

Now run it again with *actually, make that …*. Notice that the sentence never stops — one word of
trouble goes in, and the order carries on out the other side. Nothing was apologised for, because
nothing broke.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are at the front of a queue and you change your mind. You can feel the people behind you.
Rather than say anything, you take the thing you asked for first — the wrong thing, paid for,
because correcting it felt more expensive than being wrong.

**The repair:**

> Actually, make that {{…}} — sorry.

Notice where the *sorry* sits. At the front it turns into a request for permission and it invites
a pause. At the end it is politeness attached to a decision that has already been made, and the
belt never stopped.
