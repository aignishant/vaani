## One-line answer

In English your voice falling at the end means *I have finished*, and your voice rising means *your turn* — so the tune, not the words, is what hands the conversation over.

## The story

You are on the phone with somebody you do not know well. You say:

> I'll be there tomorrow.

and your voice stays level, the way it would in your own language. There is a pause. Then she
says, "Sorry — is that a question?"

Nothing was wrong with the words. She could not tell whether you had finished, whether you were
asking her to agree, or whether you were unsure. All three are different tunes in English and you
had used none of them, so she had to ask.

The sentence carried your meaning. The tune carried nothing, and the tune is what tells the other
person what to do next.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **intonation** — the tune of a sentence: where your voice goes up and where it goes down.
- **pitch** — how high or low your voice is at a moment.
- **falling** — the voice going down at the end.
- **rising** — the voice going up at the end.
- **level** — the voice staying flat.
- **hand over** — to give the other person their turn to speak.

**Intonation** is the tune your voice makes across a sentence. It is not loudness — that is stress,
from [day 13, part 1.1](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/01-speaking/1.1-loud-words-and-quiet-words.md).
It is **pitch**: high and low.

English uses it to do a job that many languages do with a word: it says what kind of thing you
just said.

| Your voice at the end | What the listener understands |
| --- | --- |
| falls | I have finished. This is a fact. |
| rises | Your turn. Answer me. |
| stays level | *nothing* — and the listener has to guess |

That last row is the important one. A **level** ending is not neutral in English. It is a gap
where information should be, and the listener fills it with a question or with a feeling that
something is wrong.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn role-play. Turns are handed over by the tune: a falling ending
means the other person speaks, a rising one asks them to answer. A role-play said on a flat voice
does not have twelve turns; it has one long stretch with pauses in it.

## The rule

**Falling: statements, and questions with a question word.** This is the surprising half. A
question starting with *what*, *where*, *who*, *how* or *when* goes **down** at the end, not up:

> I live in Pune. ↘
> Where do you live? ↘
> What time does it open? ↘

**Rising: yes/no questions.** A question with no question word — one that could be answered with
yes or no — goes **up**:

> Do you live in Pune? ↗
> Is it open today? ↗
> You're coming tomorrow? ↗

This was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Intonation (linguistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_(linguistics)), which states
that "in English a falling pitch movement is associated with statements, but a rising pitch turns
a statement into a yes–no question", that "low pitch occurs at the end of utterances other than
yes–no questions, while high pitch occurs at the end of yes–no questions", and that wh-questions
"work the same way" as statements, with the example "Who will **help**?" falling at the end.

**Where the movement happens.** Not on the last word — on the **last loud word**, and it carries
on to the end of the sentence from there:

```text
   I live in PU-ne.              Do you live in PU-ne?
                ‾‾╲                             ╱‾‾
           falls from here              rises from here
```

So the tune and the stress work together: stress picks which syllable, intonation decides which
way it moves.

**The third tune: fall then rise.** The voice goes down and comes back up a little. It means *not
quite* — I am not sure, or there is more coming, or I am being polite about disagreeing:

> It's all right. ↘↗    (meaning: but not really)
> I could come tomorrow. ↘↗    (meaning: but I would rather not)

You do not have to produce this one yet. You do have to know it exists, because you will hear it
and mistake it for agreement.

**Listen before you say.** These entries were opened on 2026-09-22 and each carries an audio file:
[sorry](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sorry) /ˈsɒɹi/,
[tomorrow](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tomorrow) /təˈmɒɹəʊ/. A dictionary gives you one word at
a time, and intonation is a tune over a whole sentence — so use these for the word, and for the
tune use your own recording: say the sentence both ways and play them back one after the other.
That comparison is the model, and it is the one you can actually make at home.

**The trap.** Many languages mark a question with a word or an ending, and keep the tune level.
If yours does, a level English question will simply not be heard as a question — the listener will
hear a statement and wait for you to continue.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the level statement in the story:

> I'll be there tomorrow. →

The listener cannot tell whether you have finished. What they do is wait, which produces the
silence that makes both of you uncomfortable. The fix: let the voice drop on *tomorrow*.

The second is the rising statement, used everywhere:

> I live in Pune? ↗
> I work in a shop? ↗

Each one now sounds like a question, or like somebody who is not sure of their own life. Very
common in learners who have been told English "goes up". The fix: only yes/no questions go up.

The third is the rising wh-question:

> Where do you live? ↗

This is the one almost nobody is taught. It is understood, and it sounds anxious or over-polite —
like somebody checking whether they are allowed to ask. The fix: down at the end.

The fourth is the flat yes/no question:

> Do you live here. →

Understood as a demand for information rather than a question. The fix: up on the last loud word.

## In real life

Intonation is the part of English that survives a bad phone line when everything else is lost.
When you cannot hear the words at all, you can still hear whether somebody asked you something.
That is how much work the tune is doing, and it is why a flat voice is harder to deal with than a
strong accent.

Fluent speakers use tune to be polite without using polite words. A falling *no* is final; a
fall-rise *no* leaves the door open. A rising *right* invites you to continue; a falling *right*
ends the topic. None of this is in the dictionary and all of it is heard.

What a native listener does with a flat speaker is take control of the conversation — asking short
questions, checking constantly, filling every pause. It reads as helpfulness and it is really
repair work, and the effect is that you end up saying much less than you could.
