## One-line answer

Write out a real conversation of your own, draw an arrow at the end of every line, and record it — because the tune is a decision you make per sentence, and marking it on paper is how you find out you had not been making it.

## The story

You are on the phone arranging to collect something. Six lines, back and forth, and it goes badly
— not wrongly, just slowly. Every time you finish a sentence there is a small pause before she
speaks, and twice she says "sorry, go on" when you had finished.

Afterwards you write the six lines out. They are all correct. Then you draw an arrow at the end of
each and you notice that you have no idea which way four of them went, because you never decided.

The tune is not something that happens to a sentence. It is a choice, and if you do not make it,
the sentence goes out flat.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **arrow** — here, a small mark ↘ or ↗ showing which way the voice goes.
- **turn** — one person's stretch of speaking before the other speaks.
- **arrange** — to agree a plan with somebody: when, where, who.
- **mark up** — to write small signs on a page to remind you how to say it.

[Part 1.1](1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md) gave you two tunes and the rule for which goes
where. This part is about doing it on your own sentences, and the method is the same one that
worked for the joins on
[day 17, part 1.2](../../../day-17-linking-words/parts/01-speaking/1.2-link-your-own-lines.md):
write it, **mark it up**, then say it.

The marking is what does the work. Deciding *falling* while looking at a written line is easy.
Deciding it in the middle of a real phone call is not — unless you have done it on paper enough
times that the hand knows.

And a conversation is the right unit here, not a sentence, because the tune's job is handing over
**turns**. You cannot practise handing over a turn in a sentence that has nobody on the other end
of it.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn role-play with both voices yours. Twelve turns need eleven
hand-overs, and each hand-over is an arrow. This part is the gate's mechanics, practised on a
conversation you would actually have.

## The rule

**Step one: write a real six-turn conversation.** Both sides. Something you might genuinely have
this week — arranging to collect something, checking an opening day, asking about a delivery.

```text
   you:   Hi — is the shop open on Sunday?
   them:  Yes, till four.
   you:   And do you have the small size?
   them:  I think so. Which colour?
   you:   The grey one.
   them:  Let me check.
```

**Step two: mark every line with an arrow.** One per line, at the end.

| Line | Kind | Arrow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the shop open on Sunday? | yes/no question | ↗ |
| Yes, till four. | statement | ↘ |
| And do you have the small size? | yes/no question | ↗ |
| Which colour? | wh-question | ↘ |
| The grey one. | statement | ↘ |
| Let me check. | statement | ↘ |

Notice how few go up. Most speech falls. Rising is the marked one, and that is why using it
everywhere is so noticeable.

**Step three: say it with the arrows, recorder on.** Both voices. Exaggerate at first — much more
than feels natural. A tune that feels too big usually sounds about right on playback, because you
hear your own voice differently from inside.

**Step four: check on playback, one question per line.** *Could I tell, without the words, whether
this was a statement or a question?* Mark any line where the answer is no.

**The three lines worth drilling separately.** These come up in almost every conversation and each
has a fixed tune:

| Line | Arrow | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sorry? | ↗ | asking somebody to repeat — must rise |
| Sorry. | ↘ | apologising — must fall |
| Really? | ↗ | interested, asking |
| Really. | ↘ | flat interest, or disbelief |

The word was checked on 2026-09-22 at [sorry](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sorry) /ˈsɒɹi/, which
lists an audio file; the same two syllables carry two opposite meanings depending only on which
way the second one moves.

**The one to be careful with.** A rising statement that was meant as a statement makes you sound
unsure of your own facts:

> I've been there for two years. ↗

In an interview that costs you more than any grammar mistake on this page.

The rule behind all of it was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Intonation (linguistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_(linguistics)): "in English a
falling pitch movement is associated with statements, but a rising pitch turns a statement into a
yes–no question."

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is marking the arrows and then not using them. You write ↗ and say
the line level, because the tune is a habit and habits do not change because a page says so. The
fix: exaggerate. Go further than feels reasonable, then listen.

The second is marking by the punctuation instead of by the kind of question:

> Where do you live? ↗

The question mark says nothing about the tune — wh-questions fall. The fix: ask which kind it is,
not what mark is on the end.

The third is one tune for the whole conversation. You get the first line right and then the rest
comes out on the same shape, because you are concentrating on the words. The fix: six lines is
already enough; do not write twelve until six are right.

The fourth is judging the tune while speaking. You cannot hear your own pitch properly from
inside; everybody sounds more expressive to themselves than they do on a recording. The fix: the
playback question, marked line by line.

## In real life

Real conversations are mostly falling, with rises used sparingly — and that is what makes a rise
do so much work when it comes. A speaker who rises constantly sounds unsure; one who never rises
sounds like they are reading a notice.

Fluent speakers use a small rise mid-sentence to say *I have not finished* — a list, a reason, a
condition — and that is what stops people interrupting them. Listen for it: it is the tune that
holds the floor. You do not need to produce it yet, but hearing it explains why some people never
get cut off.

What a native listener does with a flat speaker is exactly what happened in the story: they pause
after every sentence, checking whether you are done. Neither of you ever names it. The
conversation is simply slower and a little uncomfortable, and both of you assume it is about
vocabulary.
