## One-line answer

Record your conversation with every line flat, then play only your own lines and try to sort them into statements and questions by ear — the ones you cannot sort are the ones a listener could not have answered.

## The story

Six lines, recorded flat, played back to a friend who has not seen the page. After each one he is
asked a single question: *did that person ask you something?*

He gets two right out of six, and the two he gets right are right because of the words — *do you*
and *which colour* give it away. On the other four he shrugs.

Those four are the lines where, in a real conversation, he would have had to guess. He would have
guessed wrong about half the time, and neither of you would ever have known that the tune was the
reason.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **flat** — no rise and no fall; the voice on one level.
- **sort** — to put things into two piles.
- **by ear** — using only what you hear, not what you know.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.

You cannot hear your own intonation while speaking, and you cannot hear it on playback either if
you can still see the words — because the words tell you which kind each line is, and your ear
simply agrees.

So this exercise removes the words. You record the conversation flat, and then you sort the lines
**by ear** alone: play each one and say, before thinking, whether it was a statement or a
question. Doing it a day later, or having somebody else do it, is what makes it honest.

The count is the finding. Six lines, and the number you can sort is a measure of how much of your
meaning is currently in your tune. For most people recording this for the first time the number is
small, and that is not a failure — it is the thing you did not know an hour ago.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking folder's check that can go **RED**. A flat question is not a small
pronunciation issue; it is a sentence the listener cannot act on. And unlike a wrong vowel, it
never gets corrected, because the listener repairs it by asking and nobody notices that a repair
happened.

## The rule

**Step one: record the flat version.** Take your six-turn conversation from
[part 1.2](1.2-put-the-tune-on-your-own-lines.md). Say all six lines with the voice on one level —
no rise, no fall, ordinary volume, ordinary speed. Do not sound robotic or bored; just remove the
movement. Label the take **deliberate**.

**Step two: record the marked version straight after**, with every arrow used.

**Step three: sort by ear.** Later — the next day, or with somebody else doing the listening —
play the flat take one line at a time. After each, write down immediately: **statement or
question?** No pausing, no replaying, no looking at the page.

| Line | Heard as | Actually was | Right? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | | | |
| 2 | | | |
| 3 | | | |
| 4 | | | |
| 5 | | | |
| 6 | | | |

**Step four: sort the marked take the same way, and compare.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Flat take: three or more wrong. Marked take: five or six right. | GREEN. The tune is carrying real information, and you have the numbers. |
| Both takes score about the same | RED. Your marked take is still fairly flat — the arrows are on the page but not in your voice. Exaggerate much more and record again. |
| The flat take scores well | RED, usually because the words are giving it away. Check: if every question starts with *do* or *which*, the tune was never being tested. Rewrite two lines as statement-shaped questions — *You're open on Sunday?* — and redo it. |

**Step five: the repair.** For every line you got wrong on the flat take, say it twice on the
recorder — flat, then with its arrow — and name out loud which way the arrow goes. That pairing is
what puts the tune in the ear.

**Step six: the finding.** One line: how many of six you could sort flat, how many marked, and
which kind of line was hardest — statements or questions. For most learners it is the
statement-shaped questions, because those are the lines where the tune is doing **all** of the
work.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is performing flatness — a dull, slow, robot voice. That is not the failure being
tested; real flat speech is at normal speed with normal energy and simply no pitch movement at the
end. The fix: say the lines the way you would if you were concentrating hard on the words, which
is when flatness actually happens.

The second is sorting with the page in front of you. Your eye will answer before your ear does.
The fix: cover it, or better, hand the recording to somebody who has never seen it.

The third is a conversation where every question begins with an obvious question word. Then the
words carry everything and the tune is never tested. The fix: at least two of your six lines
should be statement-shaped questions — *You're open on Sunday?*, *That's the last one?* — because
those are the ones that exist only as tune.

## In real life

Flat speech does not get corrected, ever. A listener who cannot tell whether you asked something
does not say so; they answer as though you had asked, or they wait, and the conversation absorbs
it. So the only correction available is a recording, which is why this part exists and why the
sorting has to be done by ear.

Fluent speakers rely on tune so heavily that they use it instead of words. *Right?* with a rise is
a question; *right* with a fall closes the topic. *You're coming* with a rise is an invitation;
with a fall it is an instruction. Same words, opposite meanings, and nothing on the page
distinguishes them.

Under pressure — a bad line, a noisy street, somebody half-listening — the tune is often all that
gets through. What survives a lost phone signal is whether the voice went up. If your voice never
does, then on a bad line you have said nothing at all.
