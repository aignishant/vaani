## One-line answer

A message about amounts has two halves — what you have and what you need — and the reader can only act if the amount words are right and at least one real number is in there somewhere.

## The story

You are organising something small — a few people coming round, a job that needs doing — and you
send:

> There's a lot of people coming and not much chairs. Can you bring some?

They bring two chairs. Eleven people arrive.

*A lot of* is true for eleven and it is also true for five, and *some chairs* is anything from one
to ten. Every word in the message was in the right family, and the reader still had no way to know
what to do.

Amount words say **which side of enough** you are on. They do not say how much. For that you need
a number, and a message that has to be acted on needs at least one.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **amount word** — *many*, *much*, *a few*, *a little*, *a lot of*: how much, roughly.
- **number** — an exact figure: six, eleven, two hundred.
- **act on** — to do what a message says without asking any more questions.
- **short of** — not having enough of something.

Amount words are **comparisons in disguise**. *A lot of* means *more than I expected*. *Not many*
means *fewer than we need*. They tell the reader your judgement, not the quantity, and two people
can mean very different things by the same word.

That is fine for most messages. It is not fine when somebody has to buy, carry or bring something.

So a message about amounts does two things:

| For | Use |
| --- | --- |
| the picture | amount words — *a lot of people*, *not much time* |
| the action | a number — *eleven people*, *bring six chairs* |

The shape that works is: **the amount word for the situation, the number for the ask.** One
sentence of each.

> There are a lot of people coming — eleven, I think. Could you bring six chairs?

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate note asks for something specific enough to be acted on. Every version of that
note — a shopping list, directions, a request for help — fails in the same way when the amount is
left as a judgement. The number is what turns a description into an instruction.

## The rule

**The four-sentence shape.**

```text
   1. the situation, with an amount word   "There are a lot of people coming."
   2. the number                           "Eleven, I think."
   3. what is short, with an amount word   "We haven't got many chairs."
   4. the ask, with a number               "Could you bring six?"
```

**Choosing the amount word.** From
[part 2.1](2.1-much-many-and-a-lot-of.md), and checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Nouns: countable and uncountable](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/nouns-countable-uncountable)
— "There aren't many potatoes"; "There isn't much water" — and at
[Quantifiers](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/quantifiers),
which puts *many* and *a few* with count nouns, *much* and *a little* with uncount nouns, and *a
lot of* with both:

| Sentence | Countable | Uncountable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| positive | a lot of chairs | a lot of time |
| negative | not many chairs | not much time |
| question | how many chairs? | how much time? |
| small amount | a few chairs | a little time |

**Ask with the right question word.** If you want the reader to tell you the amount, the question
word does the work:

> How many are coming?
> How much is it going to cost?

**Say which side of enough you are on.** The single most useful sentence in a message about
amounts, and beginners leave it out:

> We're about six chairs short.
> We've got enough food, but not enough plates.

*Enough* and *short of* are the words that turn a count into a decision.

**Rounding is allowed, and should be marked.** *Eleven, I think.* *About ten.* *Six or seven.*
A reader can act on an approximate number; they cannot act on no number. Marking it as approximate
is honest and costs two words.

**One number per ask.** If you need chairs, plates and a table, that is three asks with three
numbers, in three sentences — the list rule from
[day 21, part 2.1](../../../day-21-at-the-shop/parts/02-writing/2.1-a-list-is-not-a-note.md).

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is all judgement and no number:

> There's a lot of people coming and not much chairs. Can you bring some?

Two chairs arrive. The fix: one number in the situation sentence, one in the ask. (And *not many
chairs* — *chairs* is countable.)

The second is the number with no situation:

> Bring six chairs.

The reader has no idea why, so if they can only find four they do not know whether that is a
disaster or fine. The fix: the amount sentence above it. A reader who knows the situation can
solve problems you did not anticipate.

The third is the wrong amount word with a common noun:

> There's a lot of peoples.

*People* is already plural and countable: *a lot of people*, *many people*, *not many people*.

The fourth is *some* doing the work of a number:

> Can you bring some chairs?

*Some* means "more than one", which covers two and ten. The fix: a number, even an approximate
one.

## In real life

Messages about amounts are acted on immediately and literally, usually by somebody already out of
the house. They will bring what the message said, and they will not call to check, because the
message did not look unclear — it looked complete.

Fluent writers give the number **and** the flexibility: *six if you can, four is fine*. That is
one clause and it survives the reader only having four, which is what usually happens. It is the
same failure line that protected the note on
[day 17, part 2.3](../../../day-17-linking-words/parts/02-writing/2.3-the-place-word-that-lies.md).

What a reader does after bringing two chairs for eleven people is not blame themselves. They
remember that your messages need checking, and from then on they phone — which costs you both more
than the number would have.
