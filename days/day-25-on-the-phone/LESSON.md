## §1 Where we are

You call a shop and say "hello, the thing I ordered?" and the call never recovers, because on a
phone there is no face, no counter and nothing to point at. The speaking folder is about the
three parts of a call — the opening that says who you are, the middle you can pause with *hold
on*, and the closing both people do together — and about the piece of paper beside the phone
that decides whether you come away with anything.

You write "I will be there at six." and your friend asks if everything is all right. The writing
folder is about the rules that change on a phone: no complete sentences, no final full stops, and
a full stop on a short message that reads as annoyance.

Choose either folder first. Each stands on its own. Follow the parts in that folder's order.

## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** Answer each broken version's question in writing in
[part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-call-with-no-ending.md). If in version B you can write the
number down confidently, the check is RED — the number was too short or too familiar; use six
mixed characters said once at speed. If version C does not sound abrupt, listen again at the exact
moment the line goes dead, or play it to somebody else.

**Writing:** Fill in the feeling table for the punctuated versions in
[part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-the-message-that-reads-cold.md) and then for your originals. No
difference at all is RED — show both to another person and ask which sender is in a better mood.

finding to write down.

## §7 Traps

- An opening with no name, so the whole middle is spent working out who is calling.
- Silence while you look something up, which sounds like the line has dropped.
- Hanging up the moment you have your answer, over the top of the other person.
- Apologising for your English at the start, which spends the opening and slows them down.
- A number heard and not said back. Long numbers go back digit by digit.
- Not asking how a name is spelled, and then being unable to use it in the next call.
- No four-line note, so the call goes well and produces nothing written down.
- A letter sent as a text message: greeting, full sentences, sign-off.
- A full stop on a one-word reply, which reads as displeasure.
- Abbreviations and emojis in a message to a landlord or a manager.
- An open question — *are you free this week?* — which is homework, and homework waits.
- A reply that gives the reason before the answer.
- Reading your own messages as the writer who knows what was meant.
