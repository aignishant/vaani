## One-line answer

A phone call has a fixed opening — who you are and why you are calling — a middle you can pause with *hold on*, and a closing that both people do together, and skipping any of the three is what makes calls feel hard.

## The story

You call a shop about something you ordered. They pick up and say a word you do not catch, and
there is a silence, and you say:

> Hello. The thing I ordered?

And it goes downhill from there — not because your English failed, but because you had no opening.
They do not know who you are, what you ordered or which of the forty things they sell you mean,
and they cannot see your face to work any of it out.

On a phone there is no face, no counter, no thing to point at. The opening is the only thing that
tells the other person how to help you, and it is three seconds long.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **opening** — the first few lines of a call.
- **hold on** — wait a moment; do not hang up.
- **closing** — the last few lines, where both people agree the call is over.
- **hang up** — to end a call.
- **voicemail** — a recorded message you leave when nobody answers.

A call is three parts, and each has a job.

**The opening** answers two questions before anything else: **who is this**, and **what is this
about**. In that order. Until the other person has both, everything you say afterwards is being
held rather than understood.

**The middle** is the actual business. The useful thing here is not more language — it is knowing
how to **pause** it. A call has silences that would be fine face to face and are alarming on a
phone, so English has small words whose only job is to fill them: *hold on*, *just a second*,
*let me check*.

**The closing** is done by both people, in turns. A call does not end when you have finished
talking; it ends when you have both said a version of *right — thanks — bye*. Hanging up before
that feels, to the other person, like being cut off.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn role-play in a shop or a café — a face-to-face scene. A phone
call is the harder version of the same skill, with the face taken away, and it is the one you will
actually need for a landlord, a delivery, an appointment. Learning the three parts here makes the
gate's role-play easier, not harder.

## The rule

**The opening, in two lines.**

```text
   Hello — is that the bike shop?          ← check you have the right place
   Hi, my name's Ravi. I ordered a light    ← who you are
   last week and I wanted to check on it.   ← why you are calling
```

Both lines are short. The mistake is not being too brief; it is starting the business before the
other person knows who is talking.

**When somebody else answers.** The person who picks up may not be the person you want:

> Hello, could I speak to somebody about deliveries?
> Is Sam there, please?

**The lines you will hear.** These were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Booking a table](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/booking-table)
and at
[A voicemail message](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/voicemail-message).
A business answers with its name and nothing else — "Hello, Gino's." — so the first thing you hear
is often a single word, said fast, and not catching it is completely normal.

**Holding the middle.** Three phrases, and they are worth saying aloud until they are automatic:

| You say | When |
| --- | --- |
| Hold on a second. | you need to look at something |
| Let me check. | you are finding an answer |
| Sorry, can you hold on? | somebody is at the door |

And what you will hear back: *hold on*, *hang on*, *just a moment*, *bear with me*, *I'll put you
through*. The last one means they are passing you to somebody else, and there will be a silence.

**When you cannot hear.** This matters more on a phone than anywhere, and the phrases were checked
on 2026-09-22 at
[Checking understanding](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding):

> I can't hear you very well.
> Could you say that again, please?
> Can you repeat that more slowly, please?
> I'm sorry?

*I can't hear you very well* is the most useful of the four on a phone, because it names the line
rather than your English, and the other person will move, speak up, or call you back.

**The closing, in turns.** It takes two or three exchanges and that is correct, not padding:

> Right — thanks very much.
> No problem.
> Thanks. Bye.
> Bye.

The transcript checked above ends exactly this way: "Great. Thanks! Bye." — "Bye."

**Leaving a voicemail.** If nobody answers, the same three parts, compressed, and slower than feels
natural:

> Hi, this is Ravi. I'm calling about the light I ordered last week. Could you call me back on
> this number? Thanks a lot.

The model checked above opens with the name, states the purpose — "I need some information on your
new products" — asks for a callback and closes with "Thanks a lot. I look forward to hearing from
you."

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the opening with no name:

> Hello. The thing I ordered?

The other person now has to find out who you are before they can do anything, and doing that by
phone takes four questions. The fix: name first, reason second.

The second is silence in the middle. You are looking for the order number and saying nothing, and
after a few seconds they say "hello? hello?" The fix: *hold on a second* before you go quiet.

The third is hanging up as soon as you have your answer:

> "It'll be here on Thursday." — "Ok bye." *click*

It reads as rude even when it is only nerves. The fix: the two-turn closing.

The fourth is apologising for your English at the start:

> Sorry, my English is not very good.

It makes the other person slow down and simplify more than you need, and it spends your opening on
something other than who you are. The fix: use *I can't hear you very well* when you need it, and
otherwise just start.

## In real life

Phone English is the hardest listening there is, and this is not about you: the line removes the
top and bottom of the sound, and there is no face to read. Native speakers mishear each other on
the phone constantly. What they do about it is ask immediately, which is why *sorry?* exists.

Fluent callers front-load. Everything important is in the first two lines, because a call can be
cut off and because the other person is deciding, in those two lines, how much attention to give
you. The business goes in the middle, where it has the most room.

What a person on the other end does with a caller who never says who they are is guess, and then
answer the question they think you asked. That is how calls end with both people satisfied and the
wrong thing happening, which is the phone's version of day 23's nod.
