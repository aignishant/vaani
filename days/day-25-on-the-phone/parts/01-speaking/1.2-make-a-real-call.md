## One-line answer

Before a call, write four things on a piece of paper — your opening line, your question, what you need to come away with, and space for the answer — and say every number and name back before you hang up.

## The story

You call about an appointment. It goes well. You understand everything, you are pleased with
yourself, and you put the phone down.

Then somebody asks what day it is and you say Tuesday, and you are only about seventy per cent
sure, and the reference number she gave you is gone completely — you heard it, you did not write
it, and it was six characters long.

The call was a success right up to the moment it ended. What was missing was not English. It was a
piece of paper.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **reference number** — the number a company gives you so they can find your case again.
- **say it back** — repeating a number or name to check you heard it right.
- **spell it out** — saying a word letter by letter.
- **note** — here, the paper you keep beside you during the call.

Two things make phone calls survivable, and neither is vocabulary.

**The first is the note before.** Four lines on a piece of paper, written before you dial. It costs
almost nothing and it removes the thing that actually makes calls hard: having to invent language
while also listening to a bad line.

**The second is saying things back.** This is the repeat-back from
[day 23, part 1.2](../../../day-23-asking-directions/parts/01-speaking/1.2-take-the-answer-and-say-it-back.md),
and on the phone it matters more, because there is nothing to look at and nothing to point to.
Numbers, dates, times, names and prices all get said back, out loud, before the call ends.

Nobody minds. Everybody does it. A person giving you a reference number **expects** to hear it
come back.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate wants twelve turns of role-play, and the turns in a real call come from exactly
this: the ask, the answer, the say-back, the correction, the close. It is also simply the thing
that makes a call produce a result instead of a feeling that it went well.

## The rule

**The note before, four lines.**

```text
   1. opening      "Hi, my name's Ravi. I'm calling about an appointment."
   2. my question  "Can I move it to a Saturday?"
   3. must get     the new day · the reference number
   4. answer       ________________________
```

Line three is the one people leave out and it is the reason for the whole page: **what must I have
when this call ends?** If you do not decide that before, you will not notice it missing until
after.

**Saying numbers back.** The shape is *so that's …, yes?*, with the voice rising — the question
tune from
[day 24, part 1.1](../../../day-24-voice-up-and-down/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md):

> So that's Saturday the fourteenth, yes?
> Sorry — was that fifty or fifteen?
> Let me just read that back to you: four, seven, two, nine.

Note the last one. Long numbers are said back **digit by digit**, not as a whole number, because
that is how mistakes are caught. And *fifty* against *fifteen* is worth asking about every time —
it is the pair that goes wrong most in any language, and it was day 10's trap.

**Spelling things out.** Names and street names are the other half. When you give yours, offer to
spell it before they ask:

> It's Ravi — R, A, V, I.

And when you need theirs, ask straight:

> Could you spell that for me, please?

**Filling the silence while you write.** You need a second or two per number, and silence on a
phone is uncomfortable. Say what you are doing:

> Hold on, let me write that down.
> Right — go on.

**Getting the name of the person you spoke to.** One question, at the end, and it saves whole
calls later:

> Sorry, what was your name?

If anything goes wrong afterwards, the next call starts with *I spoke to somebody called Sam on
Tuesday*, and that sentence is worth a great deal.

**The closing, with the summary in it.** Before the goodbye turns, one line:

> So — Saturday the fourteenth, reference four seven two nine, and I'll get an email. Thanks a
> lot.

The voicemail and booking transcripts checked on 2026-09-22 —
[A voicemail message](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/voicemail-message)
and [Booking a table](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/booking-table)
— show both halves: the voicemail gives a callback number and an email address spelled out, and
the booking call ends "Great. Thanks! Bye." — "Bye."

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the call with no note, which is the story. Everything is
understood and nothing is kept. The fix: four lines of paper, and line three especially.

The second is the number heard and not said back:

> "Your reference is four seven two nine." — "Ok, thank you."

You have a number you are not sure of and no way to check. The fix: read it back digit by digit.

The third is silence while writing, which makes the other person think the line has dropped, so
they start saying "hello? hello?" over the number you are writing. The fix: *hold on, let me write
that down.*

The fourth is not asking for a spelling because it feels like too much trouble. It is not, and the
alternative is a name you cannot use later. The fix: *could you spell that, please?*

## In real life

Companies expect you to have nothing written down and are often surprised when you do. A caller
who says "I spoke to Sam on Tuesday, reference four seven two nine" gets a different and much
faster call, because they have handed over everything needed to find the record.

Fluent callers write during the call, not after, and they say so out loud while doing it. The
narration — *hold on … right … go on* — is not politeness; it is what keeps the other person from
repeating themselves over your writing.

Under pressure, on a bad line, in a queue, the one thing that always survives is the say-back.
Even when you have caught almost nothing, *sorry — Saturday the fourteenth?* gets you a yes or a
no, and a yes or a no is enough to act on.
