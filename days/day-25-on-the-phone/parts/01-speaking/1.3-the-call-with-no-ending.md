## One-line answer

Record the same call three times — no opening, no say-back, no closing — and after each one write down what the other person still does not know and what you still do not have.

## The story

Three versions of the same call, all understood, all polite.

In the first, nobody says who is calling, so the whole middle is spent working it out.

In the second, the numbers go past unrepeated, and the caller hangs up holding a date they are not
sure of.

In the third, the caller says "ok thanks" and hangs up while the other person is still speaking,
and the other person sits looking at the phone.

No sentence in any of them is wrong. Each is missing one of the three parts, and each one fails in
a different direction — the first wastes the other person's time, the second wastes your own, and
the third costs you something you will never be told about.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below rest on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **hang up** — to end a call.
- **abrupt** — ending or starting suddenly, in a way that feels hard.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **what you came away with** — the facts you actually have written down when the call ends.

A phone call is the one place in this course where you cannot see the damage. There is no wrong
basket, no wrong cupboard, no drawing that stops. Everything sounds fine and the cost arrives
later, as a date you are unsure of or a person who found you rude.

So this part makes three separate broken versions, each missing exactly one part, and checks each
with a question that has a written answer:

| Broken version | The question |
| --- | --- |
| no opening | What does the other person still not know about me? |
| no say-back | What am I not sure of, now the call has ended? |
| no closing | What would the other person be feeling as I hung up? |

Three versions rather than one, because the three parts fail differently and mixing them tells
you nothing about which one you personally skip.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking folder's check that can go **RED**. Of the three, the missing say-back is the
one with a checkable answer — you either have the number written down or you do not — and it is
usually the one learners skip, because it feels like extra talking in a conversation they already
found hard.

## The rule

**Step one: record version A — no opening.** Take a real call from
[part 1.2](1.2-make-a-real-call.md). Start straight into the business: no name, no reason, no
checking you have the right place. Play the other side honestly — they will have to ask who you
are and what this is about. Label it **deliberate**.

> "Hello, Gino's." — "Yeah, about the booking?"

**Step two: record version B — no say-back.** Full opening, full closing, but every number, date
and name goes past without being repeated. Have the other side give you two numbers and a name,
said once and fairly fast.

**Step three: record version C — no closing.** Full opening, full say-back, and then you hang up
the moment you have your answer, over the top of them.

**Step four: after each, answer its question in writing.** Not in your head.

| Version | Its question | Your answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A — no opening | What do they still not know about me, and how many turns did it take to find out? | |
| B — no say-back | Which facts am I now unsure of? Write down what you think the number was. | |
| C — no closing | What would the other person be feeling? | |

**Step five: record the full version**, all three parts, and answer all three questions again.

**Step six: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Version A needs three or more extra turns; in B you cannot write the number down with confidence; C feels abrupt on playback | GREEN. All three parts are load-bearing and you have the evidence. |
| Version B: you wrote the number confidently | RED. The number was too short or too familiar. Use a six-character reference and a date, said once at speed. |
| Nothing about C sounded abrupt | RED — listen again as the other person, at the exact moment the line goes dead mid-word. If it still sounds fine, play it to somebody else. |

**Step seven: the finding.** One line: **which of the three do I actually skip in real calls?**
Most people know the answer immediately, and writing it down is what makes the next call different.

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is playing the other side too helpfully in version A. A real receptionist with a queue
of calls does not patiently reconstruct who you are; they ask short questions and get slightly
flatter. The fix: play them realistically, including the flatness.

The second is using a number you would remember anyway in version B — your own birthday, a round
figure. Then you write it down correctly and conclude the say-back is optional. The fix: six
characters, mixed, said once.

The third is deciding that version C is fine because the business was finished. The business was
finished; the call was not. Those are different, and the difference is the thing that decides
whether the person remembers you as easy to deal with. The fix: listen to the moment the line goes
dead, and ask whether you would want to be on the other end of it.

## In real life

Nobody tells you a call was abrupt, and nobody tells you they had to work out who you were. What
happens instead is that the next call to the same place is slightly harder, or an email does not
get sent, and neither of you connects it to anything.

Fluent callers treat the closing as part of the business, not as an afterthought — the summary
line, then the goodbye turns. The summary is where mistakes are caught, which means the closing is
doing real work and not just being polite.

And the honest note about version B: skipping the say-back is not laziness. It is that repeating a
number in a language you are still learning feels like drawing attention to yourself. It is worth
naming that, because the fix is not effort — it is knowing that everybody does it and nobody
notices.
