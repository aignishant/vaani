## One-line answer

In a text message you do not need whole sentences, you do not need a full stop at the end, and a full stop at the end of a short message often reads as annoyance — which is the opposite of everything a letter taught you.

## The story

You send a friend a message:

> I will be there at six.

And she reads it, and something in her shoulders drops slightly, and she writes back "ok is
everything alright?"

Nothing is wrong. You wrote a correct, complete English sentence with a capital letter and a full
stop, exactly as you were taught, and in a text message that is not neutral. The full form and the
final stop together read as cold — as somebody being careful with you.

The rules did not change because texting is lazy. They changed because on a phone the **shape** of
a message carries feeling, and there is nothing else to carry it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **text message** — a short written message sent from a phone.
- **abbreviation** — a short form of a word or phrase, like *LOL* for *laugh out loud*.
- **emoji** — a small picture used in a message to show a feeling.
- **tone** — how a piece of writing feels: warm, cold, angry, joking.
- **register** — how formal or friendly a piece of writing is. From
  [day 19, part 2.2](../../../day-19-can-and-cant/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-a-request-and-a-rule.md).

A text message is a third kind of writing, beside the list and the note from
[day 21, part 2.1](../../../day-21-at-the-shop/parts/02-writing/2.1-a-list-is-not-a-note.md), and
it has its own rules.

These rules are not carelessness. They exist because a message is a **turn in a conversation**,
not a document — closer to speech than to a letter. And speech has tone, so a message needs
something to do tone's job.

The four rules were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[A text message invitation](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/text-message-invitation),
whose tips are, word for word:

1. "You don't need to write complete sentences."
2. "You can use abbreviations like *LOL* (= laugh out loud) or *IDK* (= I don't know) to save
   time."
3. "Use emojis to add feelings."
4. "You don't need to use full stops (.) at the end of messages."

Rules one and four are the ones that feel wrong to somebody who has been taught to write properly.
They are correct, and rule four has a second half nobody writes down, which is in the next
section.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 2 is messages, notes and short emails, and the message is the one you will send most — many
times a day, to people whose opinion of you matters. Knowing which rules are suspended is what
stops every message you send sounding slightly stiff.

## The rule

**What a message keeps and what it drops.**

| A letter or email | A text message |
| --- | --- |
| complete sentences | fragments are normal |
| full stop at the end | usually none |
| *I will*, *cannot* | *I'll*, *can't* |
| a greeting and a sign-off | often neither |
| careful spelling | careful spelling, still |

Spelling does not relax. Abbreviations are a choice; misspellings are still misspellings.

**The model conversation**, from the checked page:

> Hey! Want to meet for a drink after work?
> Cool, what time do you finish?
> Me too. Msg me when you leave
> OK. Where do you want to go?
> IDK. I might be hungry tho
> Don't worry. We can meet at the bus stop and decide then.
> See you later

Look at what is there: no full stops on most lines, fragments — *Cool, what time do you finish?* —
a shortened word, *Msg*, an abbreviation, *IDK*, and one complete sentence used where the meaning
needed it.

**The full stop, and the thing nobody writes down.** Rule four says you do not need one. The
second half is that **putting one on a short message adds weight**, and in a friendly conversation
weight reads as coldness or annoyance:

| Message | How it reads |
| --- | --- |
| ok | fine, normal |
| Ok. | slightly cold |
| Fine. | annoyed |
| I'll be there at six | normal |
| I will be there at six. | formal, or distant |

This is real and it is not about punctuation being wrong. It is that in a place where nobody uses
final stops, using one is a choice, and readers read choices as meaning something.

**Where full stops still belong.** Inside a longer message with several sentences, and in anything
work-related. A message to a manager, a landlord or a customer is written as a short note, not as
a text: complete sentences, a greeting, a sign-off.

**Emojis do tone's job.** They are not decoration; they are the thing that replaces your face. One
is usually enough, and they belong in friendly messages and almost never in work ones.

> Sorry, running late 😅
> No problem 🙂

**Abbreviations, carefully.** Use the ones you have actually seen: *LOL*, *IDK*, *tho*, *msg*,
*pls*, *thx*. Do not invent them, and do not use them with somebody older than you or senior to
you until you have seen them use one first.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the letter sent as a message:

> Dear Sam, I am writing to inform you that I will arrive at six o'clock. Yours sincerely, Ravi.

To a friend this is funny; to somebody you are trying to be normal with it is slightly sad. The
fix: one line, no greeting, no sign-off.

The second is the full stop on a one-word reply:

> Ok.

which reads as displeasure to almost every reader under forty. The fix: drop it, or write *ok
sure* or *ok 👍*.

The third is the opposite — abbreviations in a work message:

> Hi, IDK if the flat is still free, pls lmk thx

To a landlord this reads as somebody who did not think the message was worth writing. The fix:
work messages are short notes, not texts.

The fourth is a message with no context, sent hours after the last one:

> Yes

Yes to what? The other person has had forty messages since. The fix: one or two words of the
thing you are answering — *yes, Saturday's fine*.

## In real life

Message tone is read much more carefully than the writer intends, because there is nothing else
to read. A delay, a full stop, a missing emoji, a one-word reply — people take all of it as
signal, fairly or not, and the person you are writing to is usually reading yours in about a
second while doing something else.

Fluent writers match the other person. If they send fragments and emojis, you send fragments and
emojis; if they write full sentences, so do you. Matching is the safest tone rule there is, and it
removes almost all of the judgement from this page.

And the rules move. What reads as normal changes between groups and generations, and it will not
be the same in five years. That is another argument for matching: the person you are writing to is
your reference page, and they are always current.
