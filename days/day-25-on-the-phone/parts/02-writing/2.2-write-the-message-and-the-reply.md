## One-line answer

Write every message with its reply beside it, because a message is only finished when you know what the other person can send back in one line.

## The story

You send:

> Are you free this week?

Which is a friendly, open question, and it sits unanswered for two days. Not because she does not
want to see you — because answering it properly means working out her whole week and then
proposing something, and that is a job, and jobs get done later.

Then you send:

> Free Thursday evening? Could do that place near the station

and she replies in eleven seconds: *yes, 7?*

The second message is not better English. It is a message whose reply is one word, and that is
the only difference.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **reply** — what the other person sends back.
- **open question** — one with many possible answers, which takes work to answer.
- **closed question** — one answerable with yes, no or one word.
- **propose** — to suggest a particular thing, so the other person only has to agree or not.

A message is half a conversation, and the half you do not write decides whether the half you do
write works.

So the test for any message is not *is this clear?* It is: **what is the shortest reply that
finishes this?** If the shortest honest reply is longer than a line, your message has given the
other person homework, and homework waits.

The fix is almost always the same: **propose** something instead of asking openly. Turn the work
of deciding into the work of agreeing.

| Gives homework | Easy to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Are you free this week? | Free Thursday evening? |
| What do you want to do? | Fancy the place near the station? |
| When shall we meet? | Seven? |
| Can you help me with something? | Can you give me a hand moving a table on Saturday? |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate note asks for something and has to actually get it. Every message in this phase
has the same job. And the habit transfers straight to email, which is the same skill with a
greeting on top.

## The rule

**Write the reply first, or at least beside it.** On paper, two columns. Write your message, then
write the shortest honest reply the other person could send.

```text
   MY MESSAGE                          THEIR SHORTEST REPLY
   Are you free this week?             "um, I think Thursday? or maybe
                                        Friday, what were you thinking?"
   Free Thursday evening?              "yes"
```

If the right-hand column is long, rewrite the left.

**The four pieces of a message that gets answered.**

| Piece | Example |
| --- | --- |
| what it is about | Thursday — |
| the proposal | that place near the station? |
| the detail they need | they do food till late |
| the easy out | no worries if you're busy |

The last one matters more than it looks. *No worries if you're busy* makes **no** a one-word reply
too, and a message that is easy to refuse is easier to accept.

**Replying to somebody else's message.** Three rules:

1. **Answer the question first.** Your reason, if any, goes second.
2. **Repeat the thing you are answering**, if any time has passed — *yes, Saturday's fine*, not
   *yes*.
3. **Say what happens next**, if it is your turn to.

> yes Thursday's good. 7 at the station?

**The shape, from the checked page.**
[A text message invitation](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/text-message-invitation),
checked on 2026-09-22, models exactly this back-and-forth: "Hey! Want to meet for a drink after
work?" — "Cool, what time do you finish?" — "OK. Where do you want to go?" — "Don't worry. We can
meet at the bus stop and decide then." Each line is short and each hands over something easy to
answer. Its tips: "You don't need to write complete sentences"; "You don't need to use full stops
(.) at the end of messages"; "Use emojis to add feelings."

**Saying you will be late.** Its own small shape, and the most-sent message in the world:

> Sorry, running a bit late — be there as soon as I can

An apology, the fact, and what happens next. The model at
[A message to say you're late](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/message-say-youre-late),
checked the same day, advises saying how late you think you will be, and adds: "It's good to say
sorry and sorry again, but you don't need to say sorry in every message."

**The work version.** Same thinking, different clothes: greeting, the ask, the reason, the easy
out, sign-off. Sentences and full stops throughout.

> Hi Sam — could you let me know if the room is still free? I'm hoping to move in next month. If
> it's gone, no problem, I'll keep looking. Thanks.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the open question in the story:

> Are you free this week?

It is friendly and it creates work, so it waits. The fix: propose a day.

The second is the message with no subject, sent into a gap:

> Yes

The other person has to scroll. The fix: one or two words of context.

The third is the reason before the answer:

> So the thing is, I was going to come but then my brother said he might need the car, and I'm not
> sure what time I'd finish work anyway, so…

By the time the answer arrives the reader has stopped. The fix: answer, then reason.

The fourth is the message with no easy out, which forces the other person to either agree or write
a paragraph explaining themselves. The fix: six words at the end.

## In real life

Messages are read in a second, while doing something else, and answered immediately or never.
"Never" is not rudeness; it is that the message was moved to the pile of things needing thought,
and that pile is not read again. Almost every unanswered message you have ever sent was a message
that needed thought.

Fluent writers propose and offer an out, in almost everything — *Thursday work? if not, no
stress.* It reads as relaxed. What it actually is, is a message engineered so that both possible
replies are one word long.

And when something matters, fluent writers stop messaging and call, because a message thread
cannot hold a difficult conversation. Knowing when to stop writing is part of writing well, and
the signal is usually the third message in a row that needs a paragraph.
