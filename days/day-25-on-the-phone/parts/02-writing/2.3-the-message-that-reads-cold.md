## One-line answer

Rewrite your friendly messages with full stops added, contractions expanded and the emoji removed — no words changed — then read them as the person receiving them and write down what you would think the sender was feeling.

## The story

The same six words, twice.

> ok that's fine 🙂

> OK. That is fine.

Same meaning, same information, same politeness by any rule in a grammar book. One of them is a
friend agreeing and the other is somebody who has decided not to say what they actually think.

If the second version looks perfectly normal to you, that is worth knowing now rather than after
you have sent forty of them. It is the version a careful learner produces by default, because it
is the version school teaches.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **tone** — how a piece of writing feels: warm, cold, angry, joking.
- **contraction** — a short form: *I'll*, *can't*, *that's*.
- **expand** — to write a contraction out in full: *I will*, *cannot*, *that is*.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.

Everything else in this course breaks in a way you can point at: a wrong basket, a lost reader, a
number you did not write down. **Tone** does not. A cold message is correct, clear, polite and
answered — and what it costs is something nobody will ever mention.

So this exercise changes only three things, none of them words:

1. **Add a full stop** to the end of every message.
2. **Expand every contraction**: *that's* becomes *that is*, *I'll* becomes *I will*.
3. **Remove every emoji.**

No word is changed, added or deleted. Then you read the result as the person receiving it and
write down one thing: **what do I think this person is feeling?**

If the answer changes, punctuation was carrying feeling, and you have proof of something you can
otherwise only be told.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing folder's check that can go **RED**. It is also the only place in this course
where the correct-looking version is the wrong one, which makes it the hardest thing here to
believe without doing it yourself.

## The rule

**Step one: break them.** Take the four messages from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-the-message-and-the-reply.md) — not the work one; that one is supposed to be
formal. Copy them with the three changes made and **no words touched**. Label the page
**deliberate**.

| Yours | The broken version |
| --- | --- |
| Free Thursday evening? | Free Thursday evening? |
| yes Thursday's good. 7 at the station? | Yes. Thursday is good. 7 at the station. |
| Sorry, running a bit late 😅 | Sorry. I am running a bit late. |
| no worries if you're busy | No worries if you are busy. |

**Step two: wait, then read them as the receiver.** For each one, write down two things, quickly,
without reasoning about it:

| Message | What is this person feeling? | Would I reply differently? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| | | |

Do this for the broken versions first, then for your originals, and compare.

**Step three: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| At least two broken versions read as colder, annoyed or distant | GREEN. Punctuation was carrying tone, and you have it in your own handwriting. |
| No difference at all | RED. Either the messages were already formal, or you are reading as the writer who knows what was meant. Show both versions to somebody else and ask which sender is in a better mood. |
| The broken ones read as *better* — more correct, more respectful | RED in the most useful way. That reaction is the habit this part exists to find. Write it down, and ask the same person which one they would rather receive. |

**Step four: the one that should be formal.** Now do the reverse with your work message. Write it
the friendly way — no stops, contractions, an emoji — and read it as a landlord or a manager.
Write down what they would think. This is the other half of the rule: the register has to match
the reader, and the friendly version is just as wrong in the wrong place.

**Step five: restore, and write the finding.** Keep both versions of everything on the page.
Write one line: **which single change did the most damage** — the full stops, the expansions, or
the missing emoji? For most people it is the full stops, by a distance.

The rules being tested were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[A text message invitation](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/text-message-invitation):
"You don't need to write complete sentences"; "You don't need to use full stops (.) at the end of
messages"; "Use emojis to add feelings"; and at
[A message to say you're late](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/message-say-youre-late),
which advises saying how late you will be and notes: "It's good to say sorry and sorry again, but
you don't need to say sorry in every message."

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is changing a word while rewriting — swapping *yeah* for *yes*, or dropping *no
worries*. Then you have tested vocabulary, not punctuation. The fix: the three changes only, and
check the words afterwards.

The second is reading as yourself-the-writer. You know you were not annoyed, so nothing sounds
annoyed. This is the same trap as every read-back and it is worst here, because tone is the thing
you are least able to hear in your own voice. The fix: show both versions to another person and
ask one question — *which of these two people is in a better mood?*

The third is concluding that the rules are silly and correctness should win. It is a fair thought.
The answer is that both versions are correct English; the difference is register, and register is
not optional — it is the part of writing that tells the reader what kind of relationship this is.
A formal message to a friend is exactly as wrong as a casual one to a landlord, and in both cases
the words are fine.

## In real life

Nobody will ever tell you a message read coldly. They will reply more briefly, or a little later,
and the conversation will be slightly flatter than it would have been. Over months that is a real
cost paid entirely in things that did not happen.

Fluent writers match. They read the other person's last message and write in the same shape, and
that single habit handles almost everything on this page without any thinking. When in doubt about
a new person — a colleague, a landlord, somebody's parent — write slightly more formally than they
did, and then follow them down.

And the rules are not stable. Final stops read differently to different generations, and emoji use
changes every few years. Matching is the rule that survives all of that, because the person in
front of you is always the current reference.
