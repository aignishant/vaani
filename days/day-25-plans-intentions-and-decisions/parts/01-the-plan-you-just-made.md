# The plan you already had, and the one you just made

`FN-07` · Day 25

## The one-line answer

English keeps two separate futures — one for plans you packed before the conversation started,
one for decisions you make while your mouth is already open — and reaching for the wrong one
makes perfectly correct English sound strange.

## The model

Think of everything you are going to do as either **already in the bag** or **grabbed at the
door on your way out.**

In the bag are the things that were decided before anyone spoke to you. The appointment. The
trip. The thing you told someone you'd do. You did not decide those just now; you are only
reporting them. English marks that:

> I'm meeting {{someone}} on Thursday.
> I'm thinking of moving, actually.
> We're meant to be going out on Saturday.
> I was going to cook, but I'm too tired now.

Notice that none of those are the word *will*. That is the part most learners never get told.

Grabbed at the door is the other kind entirely. Someone says something, and the decision
happens *in response*, out loud, in front of them. That is *I'll*:

> You know what, I'll take the smaller one.
> Right, I'll ask her.
> Actually — I'll walk. It's nicer.

**Here is why the wrong one sounds odd even when the grammar is fine.** Someone offers you a
choice. If you answer with a packed form — *I'm going to have the second one* — you have told
them you decided before they offered. Their offer did nothing. It lands as if you weren't
listening, and they cannot say why it felt cold.

Now flip it. Someone asks what you're doing at the weekend and you say *I'll visit my sister.*
Now you sound like you are inventing your own life on the spot, deciding your weekend while
they watch. Fine grammar. Wrong bag.

So the question is never "which future tense is correct". It's: **did I have this before they
spoke, or did I make it just now?** Answer that honestly and the form follows.

One more move worth having. The plan you had, and then dropped, is enormously common in real
speech and almost never taught: *I was going to …, but …*. It is how people explain a change of
mind without apologising for it. You already have *I've been meaning to …* from Day 4 — that is
the plan you never packed at all. This is the one you packed and then put down.

**Today's phrases** — check `docs/CHUNKS.md` first, then append these tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I'm thinking of … | A plan that's real but not fixed — you can still be talked out of it |
| I was going to …, but … | A plan you had and dropped, and you're saying why |
| We're meant to be … | An arrangement someone else is inside too, so it's not yours to change |
| I'm supposed to … | A plan with an obligation attached, and you're a bit heavy about it |
| Actually, you know what, I'll … | Changing your mind out loud, mid-conversation |
| I'll just … | Deciding on the spot, small, no fuss |
| Right, I'll … | Committing on the spot after a pause to think |
| I might end up …ing | A plan you half expect to change on you |

## Say it now

Say each three times, filling your own content into the slots. Nothing written down.

1. *I'm thinking of {{…}}, but I might end up {{…}} instead.*
2. *We're meant to be {{…}} — I'm supposed to {{…}} before that.*
3. *I was going to {{…}}, but {{…}}.*
4. *Actually, you know what, I'll {{…}}. I'll just {{…}} first.*

Then join all four into one continuous stretch, twice, no stopping. Lines 1 to 3 are the bag;
line 4 is the door. The switch between them is the whole drill — you should be able to hear
where the packed plans stop and the live decision starts.

## The deliberate failure

Record a short stretch in which you answer six on-the-spot offers using **only** packed forms.
Every single answer starts *I'm going to …* or *I'm …ing*. Offer yourself the choices out loud
— tea or coffee, walk or bus, now or later — and answer each one from the bag.

**What breaking sounds like:** you sound like you already knew. Every answer arrives slightly
too fast and slightly too settled, as if the question were a formality. Listen for the flatness
— there's no moment of *choosing* anywhere in the take, and choosing out loud is most of what
makes a conversation feel live.

Now run the same six offers again with *I'll* and *you know what, I'll*. The content is
identical. What comes back is a tiny hesitation before each answer and then a small commitment
— and that is the sound of someone actually responding to you.

## When it breaks in the wild

You're standing at a counter, or in someone's kitchen, and you're offered a choice. You have
rehearsed the sentence in your head while they were still speaking, so out it comes fully
packed: *I'm going to have the tea.* Nobody corrects you. But you've just answered a question
that hadn't finished being asked, and the exchange goes slightly stiff.

The real damage is that this pattern repeats all day. Every offer you meet with a pre-packed
answer removes one small moment of live conversation.

**The repair:**

> Oh — you know what, I'll have the tea, thanks.

The *oh* buys you the beat. The *I'll* puts the decision back where they can hear it happen.
