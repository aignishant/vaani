## One-line answer

*Let's* decides, *shall we* asks, and *how about* offers — and choosing the wrong one is how a friendly suggestion comes out as an instruction.

## The story

Four of you are standing outside deciding where to eat. Somebody says "I don't mind", somebody
else says "anywhere", and after a while you say:

> Let's go to the place by the station.

And you all go. And on the way, one of them says quietly that she cannot eat anything there.

*Let's* is a decision. It does not leave a space for anyone to say no, and in a group of people
being polite, the first person to use it wins — which means you have just decided dinner for
somebody who was still waiting to be asked.

You meant to offer an idea. You made a plan.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **suggest** — to offer an idea for the other person to agree with or not.
- **decide** — to settle what will happen.
- **accept** — to say yes to a suggestion.
- **refuse** — to say no to it.
- **the out** — the space a suggestion leaves for somebody to say no easily.

English has three ways to suggest and they are not the same strength. They differ in how much
room they leave the other person.

| You say | What it does | How much room it leaves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Let's go. | decides | very little |
| Shall we go? | asks | some |
| How about going? | offers | most |

*How about* is the softest because it does not even mention the two of you — it puts an idea on
the table and steps back. *Shall we* is a genuine question. *Let's* is a decision said out loud,
and it is useful exactly when a decision is what the group needs.

None of the three is more polite than the others in the abstract. The question is always: **is
the other person free to say no?** If they are, and you want them to be, use the softer ones.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn role-play, and a plan being made is one of the few ordinary
scenes that naturally runs to twelve turns — suggest, refuse, suggest again, agree, settle the
details. Making plans is also simply what friendships are made of, and a person who can only
decide or only agree does not get to shape them.

## The rule

**The three forms, and what comes after each.**

```text
   Let's        +  plain verb       →  Let's meet at six.
   Shall we     +  plain verb  + ?  →  Shall we meet at six?
   How about    +  -ing form        →  How about meeting at six?
   How about    +  a noun           →  How about the place by the station?
```

The one to watch is *how about*, which takes the **-ing** form or a noun, never the plain verb.
*How about meet at six* is the commonest mistake on this page.

**The model lines**, checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Making suggestions](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/making-suggestions):

> "Let's focus."
> "How about starting with social media?"
> "Shall we present it at the next team meeting together?"
> "Why don't you ask Vanya to help you create a new social media plan?"

That last one is a fourth form worth having: ***why don't you…?*** or ***why don't we…?***, which
sounds like a question and works as a suggestion.

**Accepting.** Short, warm and immediate. From the same page:

> "Sure." · "Yeah, sure." · "Agreed. Good idea." · "OK, no problem." · "Good plan."

A long acceptance sounds like doubt. *Sure* is enough.

**Refusing.** This is the half that gets left out of lessons, and it has a shape: **soften, reason,
counter-suggestion.**

> I can't do Thursday, sorry — I'm working. How about Friday?

All three pieces matter. Without the reason it sounds like you do not want to; without the
counter-suggestion the plan dies and somebody else has to start again.

**Leaving an out.** One clause, added to any suggestion, and it changes the whole thing:

> Shall we meet at six? Only if that's easy for you.
> How about Friday — or is that bad for you?

A suggestion that can be refused is a suggestion people accept honestly, and the out is what makes
the acceptance worth having.

**The tune.** *Shall we* and *how about* are questions and go **up** at the end
([day 24, part 1.1](../../../day-24-voice-up-and-down/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md)).
*Let's* is a statement and goes **down**. A *shall we* said flat becomes a *let's*, whatever the
words are.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is *how about* with the plain verb:

> How about meet at six?

A listener understands and it is clearly a learner's sentence. The fix: *meeting*, or turn it into
a noun — *how about six?*

The second is *let's* used as a suggestion in a group, which is the story. Nothing is wrong and
you have decided for four people. The fix: *shall we…?* or *how about…?* when you want a real
answer.

The third is the bare refusal:

> No, I can't.

Correct, and it ends the planning. The other person now has to start again from nothing, and after
two of those they stop suggesting things. The fix: the three pieces — soften, reason, counter.

The fourth is the suggestion with no detail:

> Shall we meet sometime?

*Sometime* never happens. This is the open-question problem again: it gives the other person the
work of choosing. The fix: name a day.

## In real life

Real plans are made by proposing something specific and being wrong. *Friday? no? Saturday then?*
Two or three quick wrong suggestions settle a plan faster than one careful right one, because each
one is easy to answer.

Fluent speakers refuse with a counter almost automatically — *can't do Thursday, Friday any good?*
— and that habit is what keeps them from being the person nobody invites. Refusing well is more
socially useful than suggesting well.

And a real difference to know: *let's* between two friends who are already agreed is warm and
normal — *right, let's go*. It is only in a group still deciding that it takes the decision away.
The word is not the problem; the moment is.
