## One-line answer

A plan is not made until four things are said out loud — what, when, where and who is doing what — and the conversation is finished by one person saying all four back.

## The story

You and a friend agree to meet. It is a warm, friendly conversation. *We should definitely do
something this week. Yes, let's. It's been ages.*

Nothing happens. Nobody was lying and nobody forgot. There was simply never a day, a time or a
place in the conversation, so there was nothing for either of you to remember, and the week went
past.

Two weeks later you have the same conversation again.

A plan is not an agreement to want the same thing. It is four specific facts, and until they are
said, the plan does not exist.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **plan** — an arrangement that will actually happen: what, when, where, who.
- **settle** — to fix something so nobody has to think about it again.
- **loose end** — a piece of the plan nobody has decided.
- **confirm** — to say the whole thing back so both people agree.

Making a plan is a small piece of work with a clear finishing line, and most conversations stop
before it.

Four things have to be settled:

| Piece | The question | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **what** | What are we doing? | getting something to eat |
| **when** | Which day, and roughly when? | Friday, after work |
| **where** | Where exactly do we meet? | outside the station |
| **who does what** | Is anybody booking, bringing, telling? | I'll book a table |

The last one is the one nobody asks about and it is the one that decides whether the plan survives
contact with a busy restaurant.

And then the plan is **confirmed**: one person says all four back in a single sentence. That
sentence is the plan. Without it you have had a nice conversation.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn role-play. Settling four pieces with one refusal and one change
in the middle is a twelve-turn conversation by itself, and it is the most natural one in ordinary
life. This part is the gate's shape, practised on something you would really do.

## The rule

**The order that works: what, when, where, who.** Not because it is a rule of English, but because
each answer narrows the next. You cannot pick a place before you know what you are doing.

**Opening it.** Any of the three forms from
[part 1.1](1.1-three-ways-to-suggest.md), with something specific in it:

> How about getting something to eat on Friday?

**Narrowing, one piece at a time.** Each turn settles one thing and hands over:

> Shall we say after work? — Sure, seven?
> Where do you want to meet? — How about outside the station?

Notice the questions being handed back. A plan is made of small closed questions, each one easy to
answer — and *how about* and *shall we* are exactly the forms that do that.

**Handling the refusal.** Something will not work; something never does. Soften, reason, counter:

> I can't do Friday, sorry — I'm working late. How about Saturday?

This is normal and it is not a setback. A plan that survives one refusal is a plan that is
actually being made.

**Sorting out who does what.** One question, and most people never ask it:

> Do you want me to book it?
> Shall I let Sam know?

**Confirming — the sentence that makes it real.**

> So — Saturday, seven, outside the station, and I'll book a table.

Say it back, in one line, at the end. Then the plan exists for both of you in the same words. This
is the same say-back that caught the wrong turn on
[day 23, part 1.2](../../../day-23-asking-directions/parts/01-speaking/1.2-take-the-answer-and-say-it-back.md).

**The accepting and suggesting lines**, checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Making suggestions](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/making-suggestions):
"Let's focus."; "How about starting with social media?"; "Shall we present it at the next team
meeting together?"; "Why don't you ask Vanya to help you…?" — and, for agreeing: "Sure."; "Yeah,
sure."; "Agreed. Good idea."; "OK, no problem."; "Good plan."

**Leaving one loose end on purpose is allowed** — but say so:

> We can decide where when we get there.

That is a decision, not a gap. The difference is that both of you know it is open.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the plan with no *when*:

> We should do something this week.
> Yes, definitely.

Warm, agreed, and it will not happen. The fix: a day, in the same conversation.

The second is *sometime* and *soon*, which are the words a plan goes to die in. The fix: name
something specific, even if it is wrong — being wrong is easy to correct, and vague is not.

The third is three pieces settled and no confirmation. You know the day, the time and the place
and so do they, in slightly different words, and one of you is outside the wrong entrance. The
fix: the one-line say-back.

The fourth is nobody taking the job:

> "Do we need to book?" — "Probably." — "Yeah."

Nobody books. The fix: *I'll do it*, out loud, by name.

## In real life

Plans are killed by politeness. Everybody says *whatever suits you* and nobody proposes anything,
and the whole thing dissolves. The person who says a specific day is not being pushy; they are
doing the work, and everyone else is quietly relieved.

Fluent speakers propose and abandon quickly — *Friday? no? Saturday? no? next week then* — three
wrong suggestions in ten seconds. Each is cheap because each is easy to refuse, and that speed is
what gets to a settled plan.

And the confirmation sentence travels into everything else: work, appointments, deliveries.
Anywhere two people agree something, the person who says it back in one line at the end is the
person it does not go wrong for.
