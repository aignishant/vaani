## One-line answer

Record your planning conversation twice — once with every suggestion as *let's* and no outs, once with the softer forms — then play the other person's part and write down, each time, what it would cost you to say no.

## The story

Two people are arranging the same evening.

The first says: *Let's go to the place by the station. Let's say seven. I'll book it.*

The second says: *How about the place by the station — or somewhere else if you'd rather? Seven
any good? Shall I book it?*

Both plans end up identical: that place, seven, one table booked. The difference is that in the
second conversation the other person was asked three times whether it suited them, and in the
first they were told three times what was happening.

Neither speaker was rude. One of them will find out a week later that their friend cannot eat
there.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below rests on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **out** — the space a suggestion leaves for somebody to say no easily.
- **cost of saying no** — how much work or awkwardness a refusal takes.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **go along with** — to agree to something you do not really want.

A suggestion can be measured, and the measure is not politeness. It is: **what does it cost the
other person to refuse this?**

| Form | The cost of no |
| --- | --- |
| How about Friday — or is that bad for you? | nothing; the no is already offered |
| Shall we say Friday? | small; it is a question |
| Let's meet on Friday. | real; they have to undo a decision |
| I've booked Friday. | large; they have to cancel something |

When the cost is high enough, people **go along with** things. Not because they are weak — because
refusing costs more than one evening in the wrong restaurant.

So this part records the same plan twice and, for each suggestion, asks the one question that can
be answered honestly: *if I were them, and this did not suit me, what would I have to say?* Long
answers mean high cost.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking folder's check that can go **RED**. The gate's role-play is two people
arranging something, and a conversation where one voice decides everything is not twelve turns —
it is one person talking and another agreeing. Hearing your own decided version is how you find
out which one you have been having.

## The rule

**Step one: record the decided version.** Take a planning conversation from
[part 1.2](1.2-make-a-plan-with-somebody.md). Say **every** suggestion as *let's* or a flat
statement, with no outs, no *or is that bad for you*, no questions handed back. Keep it friendly —
warm voice, ordinary speed. Label the take **deliberate**.

> Let's get something to eat on Friday. Let's say seven. We'll meet outside the station. I'll
> book it.

**Step two: record the open version straight after.** Same plan, same four pieces settled, but
every suggestion in *shall we* or *how about*, with an out on at least two of them, and the
confirmation at the end.

**Step three: play the other person, and price every no.** Listen to each take one suggestion at a
time. After each, write the sentence you would have to say to refuse it.

| Suggestion | What I'd have to say to refuse | How hard is that? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| | | |

**Step four: count.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| The decided version needs longer refusals; the open one needs a word or two | GREEN. The outs are doing real work and you have the sentences to prove it. |
| Both versions are equally easy to refuse | RED. Your decided version still has questions in it. Take them all out — every single suggestion is a statement — and record again. |
| The decided version sounds friendlier or more helpful to you | RED, and it is the finding worth having: a decided plan does feel helpful from the inside. That is exactly why it is hard to notice. |

**Step five: the repair.** For each *let's* in the decided take, say the softer version immediately
after it on the recorder — decided, then open, back to back — and name out loud the words that
made the difference. Usually it is three: *or*, *shall*, *how*.

**Step six: the finding.** One line: **in a real group, do I decide or do I wait?** Both have a
cost. Somebody who always waits never gets a plan they wanted; somebody who always decides stops
being told when something does not suit.

The forms were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Making suggestions](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/making-suggestions):
"Let's focus."; "How about starting with social media?"; "Shall we present it at the next team
meeting together?"; "Why don't you ask Vanya to help you…?" — and the agreeing lines "Sure.";
"Yeah, sure."; "Agreed. Good idea."; "OK, no problem."; "Good plan."

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is performing the decided version as bossy — loud, fast, impatient. Then the finding is
"bossy people are hard to refuse", which needed no recording. The fix: say it warmly. The
discovery is that warmth does not lower the cost of saying no.

The second is pricing the refusals as yourself, who would not mind that restaurant anyway. The fix:
imagine one specific real reason — you cannot eat there, you have no money this week, you are
tired — and price the refusal against that.

The third is concluding that *let's* is a bad word and should be avoided. It is not. A group of
four people all offering soft suggestions and nobody deciding is its own failure, and it is the
more common one. The fix is in step six: know which of the two you do, and use the other form
deliberately when the situation needs it.

## In real life

Nobody tells you that your plan was hard to refuse. They come, and they are slightly quiet, and
you never learn why. The whole cost of this is paid in information you do not receive — which is
the same shape as day 23's nod, seen from the other side.

Fluent speakers switch. They open soft — *how about Friday?* — and once everybody has had a chance
to object, they close hard — *right, Friday then, I'll book it.* Soft to gather, firm to settle.
A plan needs both, in that order, and using them the wrong way round is what produces either a
decided evening or no evening at all.

And the group version is worth knowing: in a group of people being polite, the first firm
suggestion wins by default. If that is always you, you are not leading — you are just first. If it
is never you, you are not being easy-going; you are letting somebody else choose.
