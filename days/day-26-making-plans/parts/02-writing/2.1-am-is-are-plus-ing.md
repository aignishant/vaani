## One-line answer

Put *am*, *is* or *are* in front of a verb and *-ing* on the end of it, and the sentence stops being about what you usually do and becomes about what is happening right now.

## The story

Your sister has moved abroad and asks what everyone is doing. You write back:

> My father watches television. My mother cooks. I study.

She reads it and pictures nothing, because you have not told her what is happening this evening —
you have told her what those three people do in general, which she already knew.

You meant: *right now, as I write this, the television is on and the kitchen smells of something
and my books are open.* English has a different tense for that, and it is the difference between a
description of a family and a picture of a room.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **tense** — the form of a verb that says when something happens.
- **present simple** — the form for things you do regularly: *I work*, *she works*. From
  [day 15, part 2.1](../../../day-15-daily-routine/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-s-on-he-she-it.md).
- **present continuous** — the form for what is happening now: *I am working*.
- **-ing form** — the verb with *-ing* on the end: *working*, *eating*, *going*.

English has two present tenses and they mean different things.

**The present simple** is for what is generally true or regularly done. The page checked on
2026-09-22,
[Present simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple),
says: "We can use the present simple to talk about things we do regularly" — "I **go** to the gym
three times a week" — and "We can also use it for things which are generally true" — "She
**loves** her job."

**The present continuous** is for what is happening at this moment. The page
[Present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-continuous),
checked the same day, says it "is made from the **present tense of the verb** be and the **–ing
form** of a verb", and gives the use: "Please be quiet. The children are sleeping."

| Present simple | Present continuous |
| --- | --- |
| My father watches television. | My father is watching television. |
| *he does this often* | *he is doing it now* |
| I work in a shop. | I'm working today. |
| *that is my job* | *that is what I am doing at the moment* |

Both are correct English. Which one you choose tells the reader whether you are describing a life
or a moment.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 2 is messages about ordinary life, and half of what you write to somebody far away is what
is happening now. It is also how you say you are busy — *I'm working* — which is the most useful
sentence in this tense and the one that gets written wrong most.

## The rule

**The shape: be + -ing.**

```text
   She    is    working.
   ‾‾‾    ‾‾    ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
   who   am/is/are   verb + ing
```

The *be* part changes with the person, exactly as it did on
[day 6, part 2.1](../../../day-06-final-consonants/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are.md). The *-ing*
part never changes.

| | Full | Short |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | I am working | I'm working |
| you / we / they | you are working | you're working |
| he / she / it | he is working | he's working |

The page checked above lists exactly this: "I am working · You are playing · He is talking · She
is living · It is eating · We are staying · They are sleeping."

**No *-s*, ever.** This is the trap, and it comes from day 15. The *-s* rule belongs to the
present simple and nothing here takes one:

| Write | Not |
| --- | --- |
| She is working. | She is works. |
| He is eating. | He is eatings. |

**Spelling the -ing form.** Three small rules:

| Verb ends in | Do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| most verbs | add -ing | work → working |
| a silent *e* | drop the *e*, add -ing | live → living, come → coming |
| one vowel + one consonant, stressed | double the consonant | sit → sitting, run → running |

**Negatives and questions.** From the same page: "We make negatives by putting *not* (or *n't*)
after *am*, *is* or *are*" — "I'm not doing that"; "You aren't listening." And: "We make questions
by putting *am*, *is* or *are* in front of the subject" — "Are you listening?"; "When is she going
home?"

```text
   statement   You  are  listening.
   negative    You  aren't  listening.
   question    Are  you  listening?
```

**The other jobs this tense does.** Two are worth knowing now, because you will use them:

| Use | Example, from the checked page |
| --- | --- |
| a plan already arranged | "Mary is going to a new school next term"; "What are you doing next week?" |
| something temporary | "Michael is at university. He's studying history." |

The second one matters: *I'm living with my cousin* means for now. *I live with my cousin* means
that is where I live. One word, and the reader knows whether to ask when you are moving.

**The verbs that do not take it.** A small group describing states rather than actions — *know*,
*like*, *want*, *need*, *understand*, *belong* — stay in the simple:

| Write | Not |
| --- | --- |
| I know him. | I am knowing him. |
| I want tea. | I am wanting tea. |

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the missing *be*:

> She working in the shop today.

A reader understands and it is the most visible beginner error in English. The fix: *is*.

The second is the *-s* carried over from day 15:

> He is works today.

The fix: after *is*, the verb takes *-ing* and nothing else.

The third is the state verb in the continuous:

> I am wanting to ask you something.

The fix: *I want to ask you something.*

The fourth is the simple where the continuous belongs, which is the story:

> My father watches television.

sent to somebody asking what is happening tonight. Nothing is wrong and nothing is answered. The
fix: *is watching*.

## In real life

The two tenses are how English says *this is my life* against *this is my week*. Getting them the
wrong way round does not confuse anybody, but it does quietly say something untrue: *I'm working
in a shop* suggests a temporary job, and *I work in a shop* suggests a career. On an application
that difference is read.

Fluent speakers use the continuous to soften and to excuse: *I'm just finishing something*, *he's
not answering*, *I'm not saying that*. Each one frames the thing as a passing state rather than a
fact about somebody, and that is what makes them gentler than the simple version.

And the spelling doubling — *sitting*, *running*, *getting* — is worth learning as whole words
rather than as a rule. Native writers do not compute it; they have seen the word.
