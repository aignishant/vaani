## One-line answer

Write what is happening around you in the continuous, one thing at a time, and anchor it with one present-simple sentence — because a reader needs to know what is usual before a moment means anything.

## The story

You write to your sister abroad:

> It's raining. My mother is making tea. My father is watching the news. The neighbours are
> arguing again. I'm sitting by the window with my books open and not reading them.

She reads it twice. It is five short sentences, it contains no news and nothing has happened, and
it is the first message from home in a month that she has actually been able to picture.

Then a week later you send:

> It's raining. My mother is making tea. My father is watching the news.

and it lands flat — because now it is just a list of ordinary things, and the reader has no way to
know whether any of it is worth noticing.

The difference is one sentence: whether anything told her what is **usually** true.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **anchor** — the one sentence that says what is normally true, so the moment can be measured
  against it.
- **moment** — a short piece of time: now, this evening.
- **picture** — what the reader sees in their head.
- **flat** — with nothing in it to hold the reader's attention.

A paragraph about now needs two tenses, not one.

**The continuous sentences** carry the moment: *she is making tea*, *it is raining*, *they are
arguing*. Each one is a small picture.

**The one simple sentence** is the **anchor**. It says what is usual, and it is what tells the
reader whether the moment is ordinary or not:

> They argue most evenings.  ← usual
> They never argue.  ← so tonight matters

Without the anchor, a reader far away cannot tell the difference between a quiet Tuesday and
something worth worrying about. With it, five ordinary sentences become a scene.

One anchor is enough. Two or three and you are back to describing a life instead of a moment.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 2 is messages to real people, and the most common one you will send is some version of
*here is what is going on*. It is also exactly the shape of describing a picture, which is day 27's
writing, and of any report of a situation — at work, to a landlord, to somebody who asked how it
is going.

## The rule

**The shape: five or six continuous sentences, one simple anchor, one feeling line.**

```text
   1–2  the setting, continuous    "It's raining. The street's already flooding."
   3–5  the people, continuous     "My mother's making tea. My father's watching the news."
   6    the anchor, simple         "It rains like this most days in July."
   7    the feeling line           "It's quiet, and I don't mind it."
```

The feeling line is the one from
[day 16, part 2.2](../../../day-16-likes-and-dislikes/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-your-day-as-a-paragraph.md),
doing the same job: without it a reader has a picture and no reason to answer.

**One thing per sentence.** The same rule as the room on
[day 18, part 2.1](../../../day-18-where-things-are/parts/02-writing/2.1-big-things-first.md). Two
people in one sentence makes the reader hold one while placing the other:

| Write | Not |
| --- | --- |
| My mother's making tea. My father's watching the news. | My mother's making tea and my father's watching the news and it's raining. |

**Short forms.** In a message, use them: *she's making*, *they're arguing*, *I'm sitting*. The full
forms read formal in a friendly message, which is day 25's rule about tone.

**Where the anchor goes.** Near the end, not the start. A reader builds the moment first and then
gets told how to read it. Putting the anchor first turns the whole thing into a general
description.

**Negatives, for what is not happening.** Often the most interesting sentence in the paragraph:

> Nobody's talking. The shop isn't open yet. He isn't answering his phone.

From the checked page
[Present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-continuous),
opened on 2026-09-22: "We make negatives by putting *not* (or *n't*) after *am*, *is* or *are*" —
"I'm not doing that"; "You aren't listening."

**Saying what is temporary.** One of the two extra jobs from
[part 2.1](2.1-am-is-are-plus-ing.md), and it belongs in this kind of message constantly:

> I'm staying at my cousin's this month.
> He's working nights at the moment.

The same page gives "Michael is at university. He's studying history" for a temporary situation,
and "Mary is going to a new school next term" for something already arranged.

**The contrast sentence.** When something has changed, the continuous says so, and the checked
page has the model: "These days most people are using email instead of writing letters." That
shape — *these days … are …ing* — is how you report a change without a long explanation.

## When it breaks

The most common wrong version is the paragraph in the simple tense, which is the story:

> My father watches television. My mother cooks.

The reader learns nothing they did not know. The fix: *is watching*, *is cooking*.

The second is no anchor, so nothing can be measured:

> It's raining. They're arguing. Nobody's talking.

Is this a bad evening or a normal one? The reader cannot tell, and if they are far away they will
assume the worse one. The fix: one simple sentence.

The third is three or four anchors, which turns it back into a description:

> It rains a lot here. They argue most evenings. My father always watches the news.

Now nothing is happening at all. The fix: one anchor, near the end.

The fourth is everything joined with *and*, so the reader gets one long sentence instead of six
pictures. The fix: full stops.

## In real life

Messages like this are how distance is survived. People far from home do not want news; they want
to know what the kitchen smells like on a Tuesday. Five ordinary continuous sentences do that, and
a paragraph of news does not.

Fluent writers use the negative deliberately — *nobody's saying anything*, *the fan isn't working*
— because absence is more specific than presence. Anyone can say the room is quiet; saying what
is not happening in it puts the reader in the room.

And the anchor is the piece that transfers everywhere. A report to a manager, a message to a
landlord, a note to a doctor: *this is what is happening* plus *this is what usually happens* is
the whole shape, and the second sentence is what turns a complaint into information.
