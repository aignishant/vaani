## One-line answer

Rewrite your paragraph with every continuous turned simple and the anchor turned continuous, then write down, sentence by sentence, what the new version actually claims about your life — the sentences whose meaning changed are the ones the tense was carrying.

## The story

Three sentences, each written by somebody who knew the rule.

> I am going to the office every day.
> I work with my cousin this month.
> I am knowing the answer.

The first says a habit in the tense for right now. The second says a temporary arrangement in the
tense for permanent facts — so it reads as *that is my job*, and the person who wrote it will be
asked about it in six months. The third uses a verb that does not take the continuous at all.

None of the three is unclear. Each one quietly says something slightly untrue about the writer's
life, and nobody will ever point it out.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below rest on what beginners commonly do, not
on a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **swap** — to put one thing where the other belongs.
- **claim** — what a sentence says about the world, whether or not you meant it.
- **RED** — a check that found a real problem. **GREEN** — a check that found none.
- **state verb** — a verb about knowing, liking or wanting, which does not take *-ing*.

The two tenses do not differ in correctness. They differ in **what they claim**, and that is why
this failure is invisible: a swapped tense produces a correct English sentence about a slightly
different life.

So the check is not *is this right?* It is: **what does this sentence now say about me?**

You swap every tense in your own paragraph and then write, in plain words, the claim each new
sentence makes. Where the claim changes, the tense was doing real work. Where it does not, the
sentence was never carrying much — and that is worth knowing too.

There are three errors, all on this one page:

1. **Continuous for a habit** — *I am going to the office every day*.
2. **Simple for something temporary** — *I work with my cousin this month*.
3. **Continuous on a state verb** — *I am knowing*, *I am wanting*, *I am liking*.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing folder's check that can go **RED**. Nothing in a conversation ever corrects a
swapped tense, because the sentence is correct. And the cost is real in exactly the places that
matter: on an application, *I'm working in a shop* and *I work in a shop* are read as two
different people.

## The rule

**Step one: break it.** Take your paragraph from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-what-is-happening-now.md). Copy it with **every continuous turned simple**
and **the anchor turned continuous**. Then add one sentence using a state verb in the continuous.
Change no other word. Label the page **deliberate**, and hide the original.

> It rains. The street already floods. My mother makes tea. My father watches the news. Nobody
> talks. It's raining like this most days in July. I'm knowing it won't stop.

**Step two: write the claim.** For each sentence, in plain words, what does this now say?

| Sentence | What it claims now | What I meant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| It rains. | it rains here in general | it is raining at this moment |
| My mother makes tea. | she is the one who makes the tea | she is making it now |
| It's raining most days in July. | | |

**Step three: mark it.**

| Result | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| At least four claims changed | GREEN. The tense was carrying the meaning, and you have it written out. |
| One or two changed | RED. Your paragraph may have been mostly general to begin with — check whether it really described a moment. |
| None changed | RED. Read the broken version as somebody who does not know your life, and ask what they would picture. If you still see no difference, show both to another person. |

**Step four: the state-verb line.** Separately, look at the sentence you added with *knowing*,
*wanting* or *liking*. Write down why it is wrong — not the rule, but what is odd about saying a
state is in progress. That sentence is the one that will still feel arbitrary a year from now
unless you can say it in your own words.

**Step five: restore, and name your own swap.** Write the correct paragraph out again, marked
**GREEN**, beneath the broken one. Then write one line: **which direction do I swap in?** Almost
everybody has one — either putting habits in the continuous, or putting temporary things in the
simple. Naming yours is the useful output of the whole part.

The two tenses were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-continuous)
— "made from the **present tense of the verb** be and the **–ing form** of a verb"; "Please be
quiet. The children are sleeping"; "Michael is at university. He's studying history"; "Mary is
going to a new school next term" — and at
[Present simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple):
"We can use the present simple to talk about things we do regularly" — "I **go** to the gym three
times a week" — and "We can also use it for things which are generally true" — "She **loves** her
job."

## When it breaks

The exercise itself goes wrong in three ways.

The first is changing words while swapping tenses. You turn *is raining* into *rains* and then
tidy the sentence, and now you are checking your editing. The fix: swap the verb form only.

The second is writing the claims as grammar notes — *this is the present simple so it is a habit*.
That is repeating the rule, not testing it. The fix: write what the sentence says about **your
life**, in ordinary words, as a stranger would take it.

The third is skipping step four because the state verbs feel obvious. They are obvious in
*knowing*; they are not obvious in *I'm loving this* or *I'm liking the new place*, which you will
hear people say. The rule has soft edges, and having your own explanation is what lets you handle
the edges later.

## In real life

Nothing corrects this, ever. A swapped tense is a correct sentence, so no listener repairs it and
no reader mentions it. It simply sits in your English, saying slightly wrong things about your
work, your home and your plans, for as long as you keep making it.

Fluent speakers use the difference deliberately to be kind or careful. *He's being difficult* is
about today; *he is difficult* is about him. *I'm not understanding this* leaves the door open;
*I don't understand* closes it. The same swap you are trying to avoid by accident is a tool when
it is on purpose.

And the state verbs genuinely move. *I'm loving it* and *I'm liking the new job* are common now
and would once have been wrong. The safe position for you is: keep *know*, *want*, *need* and
*understand* in the simple, and notice what people around you do with the others.
