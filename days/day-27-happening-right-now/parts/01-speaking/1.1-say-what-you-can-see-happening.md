## One-line answer

To say what is happening in front of you, you need a *be* word and an *-ing* word in the same
breath — and the *be* word is the one that falls out when you speak fast.

## The story

You are standing at your window. Downstairs, two men are lifting a fridge off a small truck, and
one of them is shouting at the other. Your neighbour comes and stands next to you and asks what is
going on.

You want to tell her. You open your mouth and you say:

> Two men lift a fridge.

She looks at you, then back down at the street, and waits — because what you said is the kind of
sentence that goes in a book about men and fridges. It is a fact about the world. It is not a
thing that is happening while the two of you stand there.

What she was waiting to hear was:

> Two men **are** lifting a fridge, and one of them **is** shouting.

Same men. Same fridge. One small word each time, and now she is watching it with you.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **live** — happening now, while you watch.
- **commentary** — saying out loud what is happening as it happens.
- **verb** — the doing word in a sentence: *lift*, *shout*, *wait*.
- **-ing word** — the verb with *-ing* on the end: *lifting*, *shouting*, *waiting*.
- **be word** — *am*, *is* or *are*.

English has one shape for *what is true in general* and a different shape for *what is happening
right now*. Speakers of many other languages have one shape for both, so the *be* word feels like
a spare part and gets dropped. It is not spare. It is the part that puts the sentence in front of
the listener instead of in a book.

| What you mean | What you say |
| --- | --- |
| It is true about him, usually | He sells fruit. |
| It is happening, now, as you look | He is selling fruit. |

Both are correct English. They say different things, and today the second one is the job.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a recorded role-play in a shop or a café, both voices yours, and at least
twelve turns. Half of a role-play is describing what is going on in the room — *somebody is
waiting behind me*, *she is making it now*, *they are closing*. A speaker who only has the general
shape has to stop and think before every one of those turns, and a twelve-turn conversation with a
pause before every turn is not twelve turns.

## The rule

**The shape is three pieces, spoken as one.**

```text
   somebody   +   am / is / are   +   verb + ing
   Two men          are               lifting
   He               is                shouting
   I                am                watching
```

**The model lines**, checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-continuous),
which builds the tense from "the present tense of the verb be and the –ing form of a verb":

> "Please be quiet. The children are sleeping."
> "I'm just leaving work. I'll be home in an hour."

**Which be word.** You already say these every day: *I am*, *you are*, *he is*, *she is*, *it is*,
*we are*, *they are*. The same list, unchanged, goes in front of the *-ing* word.

**The sound, which is the real lesson here.** Nobody says the *be* word loudly. It shrinks:

> He is waiting.  →  **He's** waiting.
> They are coming.  →  **They're** coming.
> I am looking.  →  **I'm** looking.

It shrinks, but it never disappears. Listen for the little *z* sound at the end of *he's*, and the
little *r* in *they're*. If your mouth makes nothing at all between *he* and *waiting*, the
listener hears a missing word, not a fast one.

**Saying it before you see it.** Two words buy you thinking room while your eyes are still working:

> Look — somebody's moving the chairs.
> Right now they're opening the shutters.

*Look* and *right now* are what a real speaker says while the sentence assembles itself.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the missing *be* word:

> She waiting outside.

A listener understands you, and also hears a learner. The fix is one syllable, and it goes in the
middle: *she's waiting outside*.

The second is the *-s* from day 15 arriving where it does not belong:

> He is waits.

This one comes from working hard. You learned that *he* takes an *-s*, so you put it in. The *-s*
belongs to the general shape only. Here the *-ing* is already doing the job: *he is waiting*.

The third is the general shape used for live action:

> A man walks to the door.

Correct English, wrong moment. The listener hears a habit — this is a man who walks to that door
every day. If you meant *now*, say *a man is walking to the door*.

The fourth is the one your mouth does under pressure: you say the *be* word so weakly that it is
gone. *They're coming* becomes *they coming*. The fix is not to say it louder. The fix is to make
sure your tongue actually touches the *r* or the *z* on the way past.

## In real life

Real people describe live scenes in short bursts, not full sentences. Half of what comes out has
no subject at all:

> Look at this. Two of them. Carrying it up the stairs. No idea how.

That is normal, fluent English, and it is built on top of the full shape, not instead of it. You
can drop pieces when both of you can see the scene. You cannot drop them on the phone, or when the
other person has just walked in, because then nobody knows who *they* are.

Under pressure — a fast talker, a loud street — the first thing that goes is the *be* word, for
everybody. What a native listener notices is not one dropped *is*. It is a whole run of sentences
with no *be* word anywhere, which stops sounding like speed and starts sounding like a different
grammar.

And a difference worth having: a native speaker will add *just* when it started a second ago —
*they're just closing* — and *still* when it has gone on too long — *he's still shouting*. Those
two small words carry most of the feeling in a live description.
