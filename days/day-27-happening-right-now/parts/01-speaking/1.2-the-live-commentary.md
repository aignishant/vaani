## One-line answer

A live description is not a list of sentences about now; it is one thing, then the next thing, in
the order your eyes actually found them.

## The story

Your aunt is sitting in the back room and cannot get to the window. Outside, a wedding procession
is going past. She asks you what she is missing, and you tell her:

> There are lights. And music. Many people. A horse.

She has heard four true things and can picture none of it, because she does not know what is
happening — only what exists. Then you try again, and this time you follow the street with your
eyes and say what each thing is doing:

> The band is coming first. They're playing something loud. Behind them a horse is walking very
> slowly, and a boy is sitting on it, and he's holding an umbrella. Now the family is coming past
> our gate.

She smiles, because she is watching it.

The difference is not vocabulary. It is that the second one moves.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **commentary** — saying out loud what is happening as it happens.
- **order** — which thing you say first, second, third.
- **anchor** — the one sentence that tells the listener where everything else is.
- **filler** — a small word that buys you thinking room: *so*, *right*, *now*.

A live commentary has three jobs, and only the second one is about grammar.

1. **Put the listener somewhere.** One sentence that says where you are and what you can see.
2. **Say what each thing is doing**, with the shape from
   [part 1.1](1.1-say-what-you-can-see-happening.md).
3. **Keep moving.** When one thing stops being interesting, go to the next thing.

Most beginners stop after job two, and the result is a set of correct sentences that do not add
up to a scene.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the day you stop needing a prepared sentence. Everything up to here you could think about
first — your family, your routine, your plan for Friday. A live commentary gives you no thinking
room at all, because the thing you are describing is changing while you describe it. That is
exactly the pressure of a real conversation, and it is the pressure the phase 2 gate role-play
puts you under for twelve turns.

## The rule

**Open with the anchor.** One sentence, before anything moves:

> I'm standing at my gate, and I can see the whole street.

**Then follow one thing at a time.** Do not jump. Each sentence picks up something from the last
one:

> A woman is unlocking the shop.
> She's pulling the shutter up with both hands.
> Now she's carrying the boxes out onto the step.

**Use the three small words that hold a commentary together.**

| Word | What it does | Said like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Now | starts the next thing | Now the bus is stopping. |
| Still | the thing has not finished | He's still waiting. |
| Just | it started a moment ago | They've just arrived. |

**Say what you cannot see, too.** This is the mark of a real commentary and it costs you nothing:

> I can't see what he's carrying. Something heavy.
> I don't know who she's talking to.

**When nothing is happening, say that.** Silence is the thing beginners fear and it is not a
problem:

> Nothing much is happening now. Two dogs are lying in the sun and that's it.

The model for "activities at the moment of speaking" was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-continuous):

> "Please be quiet. The children are sleeping."

## When it breaks

The commonest break is the **noun list**, which is the story:

> Lights. Music. Many people. A horse.

Nothing is wrong. Nothing is happening either. The fix is to give every noun a verb: *the band is
playing*, *a horse is walking*.

The second is **jumping**:

> A woman is opening the shop. A bus is coming. She's carrying boxes. A boy is running.

Every sentence is correct and the listener is being thrown around the street. The fix is to finish
one thing before starting the next.

The third is the **frozen present**, which is what happens when you get tired in the middle of a
long commentary and slip back into the general shape:

> Now she carries the boxes.

The listener hears that this is what she does every morning, which may be true and is not what you
were telling them.

The fourth is **stopping when you run out of words**. A real commentary survives a missing word:

> He's carrying a … I don't know the word. A big bag thing. It's heavy.

That is a better recording than silence, and it is what fluent speakers do in a language they are
still learning.

## In real life

People describe live scenes to each other constantly — at a window, in a queue, watching a match,
standing where the other person cannot see. In all of those the listener is doing something else,
so a real commentary is short bursts with gaps, not a paragraph.

Under pressure, two things go. You lose the anchor, so nobody knows where you are; and you start
every sentence the same way — *there is a man, there is a woman, there is a boy* — which a native
listener hears immediately as somebody reading out a photograph rather than watching a street.

What a native listener forgives: a wrong word, a missing word, a restarted sentence. What they
cannot follow: a scene with no order. Order is the thing to protect when your English is running
out.
