## One-line answer

Say a live scene in the general shape on purpose, play it back, and hear the street turn into a
timetable.

## The story

A friend of yours went to a busy junction and recorded what he could see, and he was proud of it,
because every sentence was correct:

> The traffic stops at the light. A man crosses the road. A woman sells flowers on the corner. The
> bus turns left.

Then somebody asked him a simple question: was the man crossing the road when you recorded this,
or does he cross it every day?

He could not answer with his own recording. Neither sentence was in it.

Nothing he said was wrong. All of it was about a junction in general, and he had been standing at
that junction watching one particular afternoon.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. It is one of the most common
things a beginner does in a live description, which is why it is the failure worth building today.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **RED** — the check failed; something really went wrong and you heard it.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.
- **claim** — what a sentence says is true, even when you did not mean to say it.

Today you break your own commentary in the one way that leaves every sentence correct. You will
say a live scene twice: once with the *be* word and the *-ing* word, and once in the general shape
from day 15. Then you play both back and write down what each version *claims*.

The failure is worth doing because it is invisible on paper. Nobody can mark it wrong. It only
shows up when a listener asks *now, or usually?* and the recording cannot answer.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every later speaking day leans on a listener being able to place your sentence in time. When you
get to asking for help, explaining a problem at a counter, or the gate role-play, the difference
between *the machine isn't working* and *the machine doesn't work* is the difference between a
person who will help you now and a person who thinks your machine has always been broken.

## The rule

**Take one — the live version.** Record a real scene the way
[part 1.2](1.2-the-live-commentary.md) says: anchor, then one thing at a time, every sentence with
*am*, *is* or *are* plus an *-ing* word.

**Take two — the frozen version, deliberately.** Same scene, same order, same words, one change:
every sentence goes into the general shape.

> Live: A woman is pulling the shutter up.
> Frozen: A woman pulls the shutter up.

Say "this take is deliberately wrong" at the start of take two, out loud, on the recording. A
deliberate exercise that is not labelled becomes a mistake somebody later believes.

**Then fill this in from the recordings, not from memory.**

| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

Write the claims in plain words — *this says she opens that shop every morning* — not as grammar
notes.

**The check.** Fewer than four claims changed means you did not really swap the tense; you changed
the words instead. That is RED. Listen again and swap only the verb shape.

And the harder RED, the one worth catching: if the frozen take sounds **better** to you — smoother,
more confident, more like English — that is the real finding. It means the general shape is what
your mouth reaches for under pressure, and the live shape is still being assembled by hand.

**Then repair.** Record three pairs, one after the other, frozen then live:

> He sells fruit. — He's selling fruit.
> The bus turns. — The bus is turning.
> She waits outside. — She's waiting outside.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version, the one beginners actually produce, is not a whole frozen commentary. It
is a live commentary with frozen sentences scattered through it:

> The band is coming first. They play something loud. A horse is walking behind them. A boy sits
> on it.

Half now, half always. A listener does not hear a grammar mistake here — they hear the speaker
losing the thread, and they stop being sure which parts they are supposed to be watching.

The second wrong version is the over-correction that comes on the day after this one, when
everything becomes *-ing*:

> I am wanting a shop like that. I am knowing this street.

*Want*, *know*, *like*, *need* stay in the general shape. This is the trap that waits on the other
side of today's lesson, and it is worth hearing yourself say it once, wrongly, so you recognise it.

## In real life

Fluent speakers switch between the two shapes inside one breath, and the switch is meaningful:

> He works at the bank, but he's working from home this week.

Both halves are about the same man. The first is his life; the second is this week. That sentence
is the whole lesson, and it is the sentence to aim at.

Under pressure the frozen version wins, for everybody, in every language they are learning. The
repair is not more grammar. It is that you have heard yourself do it, once, on a recording, and
now the sound of it is familiar enough to catch in the moment.

What a native listener does when you freeze a live sentence: nothing, usually. They work it out
from the situation. What they do when a whole conversation is frozen: they start asking *do you
mean now?*, and every one of those questions costs you a turn.
