## One-line answer

A reader cannot see your picture, so a described picture is built in four moves — the whole, the
place, the people, the one detail — and a paragraph that starts with the detail is a paragraph
nobody can build.

## The story

You are looking at a flat to share. You take three photographs and send them to the person who
might move in with you, and because the signal is bad you also write a few lines underneath:

> There is a green cupboard. The window is big. A chair. The kitchen is on the left of the second
> photo and there is a small table near it, and the floor is new.

She writes back with one question: *is there a second room, or is that the same room?*

You had described real things, all of them true, and she could not assemble them. She did not know
what she was looking at before you started pointing at parts of it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **describe** — to write what something looks like, so a reader can see it.
- **paragraph** — a block of sentences about one thing, with no blank lines inside it.
- **whole** — the thing all at once, before its parts.
- **detail** — one small thing inside the picture.
- **general to particular** — big first, small after.

A reader builds a picture the way a builder builds a wall: bottom first. Every sentence you write
has to stand on the one before it. So the order is not decoration — it is the whole job.

The four moves, in order:

| Move | What the sentence does | What the reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | the whole | what kind of thing this is |
| 2 | the place | where it is, or where things are in it |
| 3 | the people or the action | what is going on in it |
| 4 | one detail | the thing that makes it this one, not any other |

Move four is one detail, not seven. A paragraph with seven details has no detail at all.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 2 ends with a written note that does three jobs in a hundred words. Every one of those jobs
needs a reader to see something they were not present for — a place, a problem, an arrangement.
The four moves are the order that makes that possible, and they are the same four moves that a
form at an office, a message about a lost bag and a description of a fault all need.

## The rule

**Move 1 — the whole, in one sentence.** Never a part:

> This is the front room of the flat.

**Move 2 — the place.** Where the thing is, or where the main things sit inside it:

> It has one big window, and the kitchen is through a door on the left.

**Move 3 — what is happening.** If people are in the picture, say what they are doing, using *am*,
*is* or *are* with an *-ing* word. The model was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/present-continuous):

> "Please be quiet. The children are sleeping."

So, in your paragraph:

> Two men are painting the wall behind the door.

If nothing is happening, say what is there instead — *there is a green cupboard against the back
wall* — and keep moving.

**Move 4 — one detail, at the end.** The one thing you would remember:

> The cupboard has a broken handle.

**The shape on the page.** The four moves are four sentences or five, in one block, no blank lines
inside it. Capital letter at the start of each sentence, full stop at the end. A described picture
is a paragraph, not a list, because a list gives the reader no order to build in.

**The tips for writing to somebody informally** were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Social media posts](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/social-media-posts),
which says: "You don't need to write full sentences." and "Use emojis to show how you're feeling."
That is true for a caption under a photo somebody can already see. It is not true here, and the
difference is the point of today: **when the reader cannot see the picture, full sentences and
order are the only thing they have.**

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **detail-first paragraph**, which is the story:

> There is a green cupboard. The window is big. A chair.

The reader has three objects floating in nothing. The fix is one sentence at the top: *this is the
front room of the flat.*

The second is the **list**:

> - green cupboard
> - big window
> - new floor

Easy to write, impossible to picture. A list has no relationships in it — nothing is behind,
beside or under anything else.

The third is the **everything paragraph**, where each sentence adds another object until the
reader gives up:

> There is a cupboard and a chair and a table and a light and a small shelf and two boxes and a
> fan.

The fix is brutal and simple: keep the three things that matter and delete the rest.

The fourth is **no verbs of action**, so the picture is true and dead:

> Two men. Paint. A wall.

The fix is move 3: *two men are painting the wall.*

## In real life

Real descriptions are written to somebody who has to *do* something with them — rent the flat,
find the bag, send the right part. So a real description is shaped by the decision the reader has
to make. If your reader is choosing a room, the number of windows matters and the colour of the
cupboard does not.

Under pressure people write the detail that is bothering them and forget the whole. Somebody
writing about a leak writes about the water and never says which room.

What a careful reader does when a description arrives with no first sentence: they ask one
question and wait. That costs a day. What a formal reader — an office, a landlord, an insurer —
does: they ask you to send it again in their own form, because a description without an order
cannot be filed.
