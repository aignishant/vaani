## One-line answer

Turn the four moves into one real paragraph by giving the reader a place to stand and then moving
in one direction — and stop while the reader can still hold it.

## The story

Two people wrote about the same photograph of a courtyard.

The first one wrote:

> A courtyard. Plants. Washing on a line. Someone's bicycle against the wall on the right and the
> door is open and you can see a corridor and there are steps up to the first floor and the
> plants are in old paint tins.

The second one wrote:

> This is the courtyard in the middle of the building. It is open to the sky, and there are steps
> on the far side going up to the first floor. Somebody is hanging washing on a line across the
> middle. The plants along the wall are growing in old paint tins.

Same courtyard. Same facts, more or less. The first one is a person pointing at things as they
notice them. The second one walks the reader in through the entrance and around, once.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph** — a block of sentences about one thing.
- **direction** — the way your description travels: left to right, near to far, top to bottom.
- **flow** — sentences that follow each other without a jump.
- **trim** — to cut something out.

A picture has no order in it. Everything in a photograph is there at once. The order is something
*you* add, and the reader receives it as the only order there ever was.

So a written picture needs two decisions before the first word:

1. **Where does the reader stand?** At the door. At the window. Behind the camera.
2. **Which way do they move?** Left to right, or near to far, or big to small. Pick one and keep
   it to the end.

A paragraph that changes direction halfway is the one that gets read twice.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the first piece of writing in the course that is longer than a message and shorter than a
letter, and it is the shape most real writing takes: one block, one job, one order. Later in this
phase you will write a paragraph about your work, a note that asks for something and the hundred
word note of the phase gate. All of them are this, with a different subject.

## The rule

**Start with the whole, and put the reader somewhere.**

> This is the courtyard in the middle of the building.

**Then travel in one direction**, and say the direction out loud in the sentence so the reader can
follow:

> There are steps on the far side going up to the first floor.
> Along the wall on the left there are plants in old paint tins.

**Say what is happening once**, near the middle, with *am*, *is* or *are* plus an *-ing* word:

> Somebody is hanging washing on a line across the middle.

**End on the detail, and then stop.** The last sentence is the one the reader keeps:

> One of the tins still has the old paint label on it.

**Length.** Five sentences to seven. Not because longer is forbidden, but because a reader
building a picture from words can hold about that much before the early sentences start falling
out. If you have more than seven, you have two pictures — write the second one as its own
paragraph.

**Short forms are fine here** when you are writing to somebody you know, and the informal register
was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Social media posts](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/social-media-posts),
whose tips include "You don't need to write full sentences." and "Use emojis to show how you're
feeling." Take the warmth from that page; leave the fragments, because your reader cannot see the
picture.

**Punctuation you will need today, and nowhere to hide from it:** a capital letter at the start of
every sentence, a full stop at the end of every one, and a comma before *and* only when what
follows *and* could stand as its own sentence.

> It is open to the sky, and there are steps on the far side.
> There are plants and old tins along the wall.

The first has a comma because *there are steps on the far side* is a sentence. The second does not,
because *old tins along the wall* is not.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **one-sentence paragraph** held together with *and*:

> There is a bicycle and the door is open and there are steps and the plants are in tins and
> somebody is hanging washing.

Everything is in it. Nothing is ordered. The fix is full stops: cut it into four sentences and
then decide which one goes first.

The second is the **direction change**:

> On the left there are plants. Above the door there is a light. On the right there is a bicycle.
> At the back there are steps. Near the plants there is a tap.

The reader is being turned around four times, and the last sentence sends them back where they
started. The fix is to finish the left side before moving to the right.

The third is the **missing whole**, again — it is the mistake that comes back every time the
paragraph gets hard, because the writer is already inside the picture and forgets the reader is
not.

The fourth is **stopping with no detail**, which leaves a correct paragraph that nobody remembers:
four sentences of furniture and no paint tin.

## In real life

Most real descriptions are read by somebody who is half paying attention — on a phone, between
other messages. They read the first sentence carefully, skim the middle and read the last one. So
real writers put the whole in sentence one and the thing that matters in the last sentence, and
accept that the middle may be skimmed.

Under pressure — a complaint, a lost bag, an insurance form — people write the middle first,
because the middle is what upset them. Then they never write the first sentence at all, and the
reader has a page of detail about a thing they cannot identify.

A formal reader will not guess. An office reading a description without its first sentence will
write back asking what you are describing, and your answer arrives days after your problem.
