## One-line answer

Scramble your own description on purpose, hand it to somebody with a pencil, and watch which
sentence they stop at — that sentence is where your order was doing the work.

## The story

Somebody wrote a description of their kitchen and thought it was clear. Their cousin tried to draw
it from the words alone, and stopped after the second line with the pencil in the air.

> The shelf is above it. There is a window on the right. The stove is under the shelf and the
> table is in the middle of the room and the door is behind you.

*Above what?* was the question. The first sentence pointed at something that had not been
mentioned yet, so the whole drawing had nowhere to start.

The writer had the kitchen in front of them the whole time they wrote. That is exactly why they
could not see the hole.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Writing a description that
only works for somebody who already knows the place is the most common failure in beginner
description writing, which is why it is today's deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **scramble** — to mix the order up.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.
- **referent** — the thing a word like *it*, *above it* or *there* points back to.

A description can fail in three ways, and only one of them is visible to the writer:

1. A wrong word — visible.
2. A missing sentence — sometimes visible.
3. **A wrong order** — invisible to the writer, fatal to the reader.

Today you build number three on purpose, then make somebody try to draw it.

## Why Vaani needs it

Everything you will write for the rest of this phase is read by somebody who was not there — a
note, an email asking for something, the hundred-word gate note. You cannot test those by reading
them yourself, because you already know what you meant. A drawing test is the only cheap way to
find out whether a piece of writing works without you standing next to it.

## The rule

**Step 1 — write the GREEN version.** Use
[part 2.2](2.2-write-the-picture.md): whole first, one direction, one action sentence, one detail
last. Five to seven sentences.

**Step 2 — write the RED version, deliberately.** Same sentences, same words, three changes and
no others:

- Move the last sentence to the front.
- Delete the sentence that says what the whole thing is.
- Replace two nouns with *it* or *there*.

> GREEN: This is the front room. There is a shelf along the left wall. A radio is sitting on the
> shelf.
> RED: A radio is sitting on it. There is a shelf along the left wall.

Write **deliberate** at the top of the RED version. An unlabelled broken version is a broken
version somebody will later believe.

**Step 3 — the drawing test.** Give the RED version to one person with a pencil and paper. Do not
explain anything. Do not answer questions. Ask them to draw what they read, and to stop and put a
cross on the page wherever they cannot continue. Then give them the GREEN version on a fresh sheet.

If nobody is available, leave both versions and come back to them after a day away, and draw them
yourself without looking at the photograph.

**The check.** The drawing from the RED version must be visibly wrong or visibly unfinished. If it
comes out right, that is RED for *you*: your scramble was too gentle, or your reader was guessing
from the room they are sitting in. Scramble harder — take out the first sentence entirely — and
run it again.

**Step 5 — repair.** Write the GREEN version again from nothing, without looking at either
earlier version, and put one extra sentence in it that answers the question your reader actually
asked.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version, the one beginners write without trying, is not a scramble. It is this:

> It is very nice and there is a lot of light in the morning. My mother sits there. The colour is
> green now, we changed it last year.

Three sentences, every one true, and the reader still does not know it is a room. *It*, *there* and
*the colour* all point backwards at something that was never put on the page. The writer was
looking at it while they wrote.

The second real wrong version is the description that is all feeling and no object:

> It is my favourite place. It is so peaceful. I love sitting there in the evening.

A reader gets a mood and no picture. The smallest fix is one sentence at the top naming the thing,
and one in the middle saying what is in it.

## In real life

Nobody in real life will tell you your description failed. They will ask a question, or send a
photograph of their own, or simply do the wrong thing — bring the wrong part, go to the wrong
door, rent the other room. The failure is silent, and it costs somebody a trip.

Under pressure it gets worse, not better, because the pressure is usually that you are in the
place and they are not: a fault, an accident, a lost bag, a leak. That is exactly when the writer
knows most and the reader knows least.

What a careful reader does with a description that has a hole: they guess. They fill it with the
most ordinary possibility, which is right about half the time. What a formal reader — an office,
a landlord, an insurer — does: they file it as incomplete and write back for details, and the
half-day you saved by not writing the first sentence becomes a week.
