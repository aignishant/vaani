# How hard it lands

`FN-08` · Day 27

## The one-line answer

Your opinion and the force of your opinion are two separate things, and English gives you a dial
for the second one — so you can say exactly what you think at whatever volume the room needs.

## The model

Picture a dial on the side of every opinion you have. The opinion itself doesn't change as you
turn it. Only how hard it hits the other person.

Most learners have the dial stuck. Either it is jammed at the top, so every view arrives as a
verdict, or — far more often — it is jammed near zero, so nothing ever quite arrives at all.

Here is the same opinion at three settings. Say all three out loud and hear the difference:

> Honestly? I've got a bit of a problem with that.
> If you ask me, that's not the right way round.
> I could be wrong, but I'm not massively keen on it.

Same position. Three volumes. Nobody is confused about what you think in any of them — and that
is the test. **Softening is not the same as hiding.** A softened opinion is still an opinion; a
hidden one is a shrug with more words.

**Turning it up.** *Honestly?* at the front, said as its own little unit with a pause after it,
is the cheapest way to raise the volume. So is *if you ask me* — it marks the view as yours,
deliberately, which paradoxically makes it land harder, not softer. *Personally, I think …*
sits between them: clearly yours, clearly not a claim about the world.

**Turning it down.** Two moves, and they sit in different places.

Before the opinion: *I could be wrong, but …*. This one is doing something clever. It absorbs
the doubt at the front so the opinion behind it can stay firm. You have already admitted you
might be wrong, so you don't have to hedge the actual claim.

> I could be wrong, but I'd say it's the wrong tool for that.

After the opinion: *…, but that's just me.* This lets the view land at full weight and then
softens the room afterwards. The listener has already received the opinion. You are only
signalling that you are not going to fight about it.

> That's the wrong tool for that. But that's just me.

**And one specific piece of English worth having on its own:** the way negative opinions get
turned down with a *not*. Not *I hate it* — *I'm not massively keen on it.* Not *it's bad* —
*I'm not sure it works.* English speakers say almost every dislike this way, and a learner who
says the direct version is heard as much angrier than they are.

You already have *I'm not entirely sure* and *it depends on the situation* from Day 8, and
*it's not that I don't …, it's just …* from Day 4. Those are all near-neighbours of today's
work. Use them, don't relearn them.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| If you ask me, … | Marking a view as plainly yours — turns the dial up, not down |
| Personally, I think … | Yours, but not a claim about the world |
| Honestly? … | Raising the volume before you've said anything |
| I'd say … | A claim with a little give left in it |
| I could be wrong, but … | Absorbing the doubt up front so the opinion can stay firm |
| I'm not massively keen on … | A dislike, turned right down — the standard English shape |
| I've got a bit of a problem with … | A real objection, still not aggressive |
| …, but that's just me. | Landing it at full weight, then softening the room after |

## Say it now

Say each three times, filling the slot with something you actually think. Nothing written down.

1. *If you ask me, {{opinion}}.*
2. *I could be wrong, but I'd say {{opinion}}.*
3. *I'm not massively keen on {{something}} — I've got a bit of a problem with {{the part of it}}.*
4. *Personally, I think {{opinion}}. But that's just me.*

Then take **one** opinion — the same one — and run it through all four lines in a continuous
stretch, twice. Four settings of the dial, one view underneath. If the opinion changes as you
move down the lines, you are softening the content instead of the volume, and that is the fault
this whole day exists to catch.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d27-free.m4a`.**

Two takes in one file, no stopping between them.

> **Take one — something you have a real view about**, from your work, your city, or something
> you use every day. Say what you think and why. Dial at the top: no softeners at all.
> **Take two — the same view, to someone you've just met.** Same position, same reasons, dial
> turned down. Exactly one softener per opinion. Not two.

The rule for take two is one, and it is a hard rule. One softener is a courtesy. Two is a
retreat.

- **Solo form:** as written. Before take two, say your position out loud in one short sentence
  so you can check afterwards that it survived.
- **With a partner:** after take two they say your opinion back to you in their own words. If
  what comes back is vaguer than what you meant, the dial went past soft into gone.

## The deliberate failure

Record three opinions with the softeners stacked at both ends and in the middle. Something like:
*I could be wrong, but I'd say maybe it's sort of not really ideal, possibly, but that's just me,
I don't know.* Pile them on. Make it as padded as you can.

**What breaking sounds like:** the opinion never arrives. Play it back and try to write down, in
one plain sentence, what the speaker thinks — you won't be able to, and you are the speaker.
Listen also for what happens to your voice: it goes quieter and faster through the middle, as
though hurrying past the part that matters.

Then say the same three opinions with exactly one softener each. The view appears immediately.
Notice that it does not sound rude — that is the discovery. The politeness you thought was
coming from the pile of hedges was coming from one of them all along, and the rest were just
noise you were paying for with your credibility.

## When it breaks in the wild

Someone in a group turns to you and asks directly what you think. You know exactly what you
think. But it's a room where you don't want to be the one causing trouble, so out comes the
padded version — and the conversation moves on without your view in it. Then somebody else says
roughly what you were saying, plainly, and the room hears it as theirs.

That's the real cost, and it isn't about English. It is that a view expressed at zero volume was
not expressed.

**The repair:**

> Sorry, let me put that properly — if you ask me, {{your view}}. I could be wrong, but that's
> where I'd land.

Say it once, at one setting, and then stop talking. The stopping is part of the repair.
