# The dial, not the switch

`FN-09, IN-02` · Day 28

## The one-line answer

Agreement is a dial with several notches, not a switch with two positions, and nearly every real
conversation runs on the notches in the middle.

## The model

Watch what happens when someone says something you half-believe. You have two things ready: *yes*
and *no*. Neither of them is true. So you take *yes*, because it is cheaper, and now two things
have happened. You are on record agreeing with something you do not think. And the topic is
closed, because full agreement closes topics — there is nothing left for either of you to say.

That is the switch. Up or down, and both positions cost you something.

What fluent speakers use instead is a dial. Between *no* and *yes* there are three or four
notches, and those notches are where the conversation actually lives. You take the shape of what
they said and refuse the size of it. You take the first half and turn on the second. You give up
one point in order to keep a different one. Each of those is a separate click, and each click has
words attached to it.

Here is the scale, from the top down.

**Full agreement** — and the odd thing about English here is that it often opens with *no*.

> No, you're right.
> That's exactly it.

Both of those mean yes. The *no* is not a mistake and it is not rude; it is aimed at the doubt in
their voice, not at their point. Say it out loud several times before you trust it, because it
will feel wrong in your mouth for a while.

Full agreement is fine. Just know what it does. Use it on everything and you will have a run of
short dead conversations and no idea why they died.

**Conceding** — you were on the other side, and you are stepping off it.

> Fair enough.

Two words, and they do something that no amount of explaining does: they let the other person
stop defending. A conversation where nobody ever concedes anything is exhausting, and everybody
in it can hear that.

**Half agreement** — the notch you will use most.

> Yeah, up to a point.
> Well, yes and no.
> That's true, although …

These are the ones missing from most learners' English, and their absence is exactly what forces
you back onto the switch. *Up to a point* agrees with the shape and refuses the size. *Yes and
no* obliges you to say which is which, and that obligation is a gift — it hands you your own next
sentence. *That's true, although…* agrees first and turns after, and the order is the whole
thing: everything before the *although* has to sound like real agreement, because it is.

**Disagreement with the listening shown.**

> I see what you mean, but …

The first half is not padding. It is evidence. It tells them you processed the thing before you
rejected it, and it is the difference between a conversation and two speeches happening near each
other.

**Flat disagreement** — named, on purpose.

> That's where I'd disagree.

You need this notch. Softening everything is its own failure: if you never disagree audibly, your
agreement stops carrying information and people lose the ability to read you. Say it level, and
keep going. Do not apologise into it and do not rush the sentence after it.

And here is the interaction half of today, which matters more than the phrases. **Where you stop
on the dial decides whether there is a next turn.** The top of the scale ends the topic. The
middle continues it, every time, because half agreement is unfinished by design — the other
person can hear the unfinished part and step into it. Half-agreeing is a turn-taking move before
it is an opinion.

## Say it now

You need something to agree with. Before you start, say three claims out loud — real ones, things
you have actually heard somebody say recently. Do not write them down.

Then run each line three times, once per claim.

1. *No, you're right — {{the part they got right}}.*
2. *Yeah, up to a point. {{where your agreement stops}}.*
3. *Well, yes and no. {{the yes}}, but {{the no}}.*
4. *That's true, although {{the thing it leaves out}}.*
5. *I see what you mean, but {{where you land instead}}.*
6. *That's where I'd disagree. {{what you think instead}}.*

Then take one claim and answer it with lines 2, 4 and 5 joined into a single continuous stretch,
no stopping. It will feel like far too much agreement machinery for one answer. Run it anyway —
you are building the joins, and the joins are the part that fails when somebody is actually
waiting for you.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d28-free.m4a`.**

> Three claims you genuinely half-believe — one about your work, one about where you live, one
> about something you enjoy. Answer each of them out loud, and **stop on a different notch every
> time.** One full agreement, one half agreement, one flat disagreement. Say which notch you mean
> to use before each answer, then use it.

The phrases are not the hard part. Choosing the notch honestly is, and you will feel a pull
towards the polite end of the dial. Let the recorder catch that pull.

- **Solo form:** as written.
- **With a partner:** they supply the claims and they do not tell you in advance. This time you
  do not announce the notch — decide it silently, and afterwards they say which one they heard.
  Where their answer and yours differ, that gap is the finding.

## The deliberate failure

Answer five claims using only *yes* and *no*. Nothing else. No *but*, no *although*, no second
sentence. Make sure at least two of them are things you only half-believe.

**What breaking sounds like:** every turn ends dead. Listen for the silence after each one, the
place where the next thing should have started and did not. Then listen to the two you
half-believe — they come out flat and slightly fast, because your mouth is trying to get off
them. That flatness is audible to other people. It is why *yes* to something you do not fully
accept never quite works: they can hear the switch.

Then answer those same two again on the middle notch. Same opinion, same facts, nothing added.
What changed is that your answer now stops on an unfinished note instead of a closed one, and you
can hear the difference in the last three words.

## When it breaks in the wild

A group decides something while you are in the room. Somebody proposes it, everyone looks around,
and you half-agree — you can see the point and you also think it is going to cause a problem. You
have no words on the dial, so you nod and say *yeah*. It gets decided. Later you are one of the
people who agreed, and the objection you actually had never got said by anyone at all.

That is the real cost of a missing middle. Not rudeness, not politeness. Decisions with your name
on them that you did not make.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — can I come back to that? I said yeah, but it's more yes and no than that.

Reopening late is ordinary and nobody minds it. Standing behind something you do not believe is
far more expensive than a small interruption.

- [ ] No answer of yours ends on a bare *yeah* or a bare *no*. Count them. The count is zero.

The second one is the condition that will fail, and it fails on the answer you cared about least.
Boredom sends you straight back to the switch — which is worth knowing, because it means the dial
is not really about opinions. It is about whether you are still in the conversation.
