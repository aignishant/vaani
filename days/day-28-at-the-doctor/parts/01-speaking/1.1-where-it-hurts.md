## One-line answer

A doctor needs three things before they can help you — what is wrong, where it is, and since when
— and English gives you two ways to say the first one: *I've got* for the thing, and *it hurts*
for the feeling.

## The story

You sit down in front of the doctor. She looks up. You have been thinking about this all morning
and what comes out is:

> I am not well. I am very bad since some days.

She writes nothing, because there is nothing yet to write. She asks where. You point at your
stomach. She asks how long. You say *some days*. She asks whether it is worse after food, and by
now four of her questions have been spent getting the three facts you walked in with.

You knew all of it. You just did not have the three sentences.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **symptom** — the thing you feel that tells you something is wrong: a pain, a cough, a fever.
- **hurt** — to be painful. *My knee hurts.*
- **sore** — painful when touched or used. *A sore throat.*
- **ache** — a dull pain that does not stop.
- **since** — from a point in the past until now.

A doctor is doing one job while you talk: narrowing down. Every sentence you say either narrows
it or does not. Three sentences narrow it more than ten sentences of how you feel about it.

| The doctor's question | The sentence that answers it |
| --- | --- |
| What is wrong? | I've got a bad cough. |
| Where? | It hurts here, on the left side. |
| Since when? | Since Tuesday. |

Everything else — what you ate, what your neighbour said, what you already took — comes after
those three and is welcome. Before them, it is noise.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the first day where being understood has a real cost attached. A shop assistant who
misunderstands you sells you the wrong size. A doctor who misunderstands you treats the wrong
thing. The habit this day builds — the three facts first, then the detail — is also the shape of
every counter, office and helpline conversation in the rest of this phase.

## The rule

**Sentence one — the thing you have.** English uses *have got* for illnesses, and this was checked
on 2026-09-22 at
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got),
which says "We use I/you/we/they + have got or he/she/it + has got to talk about things that we
have" and gives the illness example:

> "He's got a terrible cold."

So, for yourself:

> I've got a headache.
> I've got a sore throat.
> I've got a temperature.

**Sentence two — where it hurts.** Here English uses a verb, not a noun. The verb *hurt* means "to
be painful", checked on 2026-09-22 at [hurt](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/hurt), which gives
exactly the sentence a doctor says back to you:

> "Does your leg still hurt?"

And *sore* means "causing pain or discomfort; painfully sensitive", checked on 2026-09-22 at
[sore](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sore):

> "Her feet were sore from walking so far."

So:

> My knee hurts.
> It hurts here. (and you point)
> My throat is sore when I swallow.

**Sentence three — since when.** Two shapes, and they are not the same:

```text
   since  +  a point in time      →  since Tuesday, since this morning, since last week
   for    +  a length of time     →  for three days, for a week
```

> I've had it since Tuesday.
> I've had it for three days.

Both are correct. *Since* is easier to get right, because a point in time is a thing you can name.

**The four extra facts a doctor asks for next**, worth having ready:

| They ask | You say |
| --- | --- |
| Is it worse at any time? | It's worse at night. |
| Did you take anything? | I took something for the pain yesterday. |
| Is it the first time? | No, it happened last year too. |
| How bad is it? | I can't sleep with it. |

That last one is the most useful sentence in the whole part: describe what it stops you doing.
*I can't sleep*, *I can't walk on it*, *I can't eat properly*. It turns a feeling into a fact.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **noun with no owner**:

> Stomach pain. Three days.

A doctor understands and it tells them nothing about whether it comes and goes, or where exactly.
The fix is one verb: *my stomach hurts, and it started three days ago*.

The second is **since with a length**:

> I have this since three days.

This is the single most common English mistake made by learners whose own language has one word
for both. The listener understands. It is also the sentence that marks a learner instantly. The
fix: *for three days*, or *since Monday*.

The third is **the feeling instead of the symptom**:

> I am very bad. I am not well. I am tired all the time.

All true, all unusable. A doctor cannot act on *bad*. The fix: name the part of the body.

The fourth is **a whole story before a single fact**:

> On Sunday we went to my cousin's house and there was food that I don't usually eat, and my
> brother also had a problem last year with his stomach, and …

The doctor is still waiting for sentence one. Tell the story second.

## In real life

A real consultation is short and the doctor is steering. What fluent speakers do is answer the
question that was asked and stop, then add one fact. *Since when? — Tuesday. It was worse
yesterday.*

Under pressure — pain, fear, a queue outside the door — people either say too little, one word at
a time, or too much, in one unbroken run. Both are normal and both cost time you may not have.

What a native listener notices and forgives: *since three days*, a wrong word for a body part,
pointing instead of naming. What no listener can work around: not knowing where the pain is. If
one English sentence goes in today, make it the one with a body part and the word *hurts* in it.

And a real-life difference: at a pharmacy counter you get one sentence, not three, because there
is a queue behind you. Practise the one-sentence version too: *I've got a sore throat since
Tuesday and I can't swallow properly.*
