## One-line answer

Say the three facts, then the story that explains them, then the one thing you want — and say the
thing you want out loud, because nobody can guess it.

## The story

Two people sat down in the same waiting room with the same problem.

The first one said everything as it came to her: the pain, then her sister's opinion, then the
pain again, then the tablets she took, then when it started, then how worried she is. It was all
true. The doctor asked her six questions to put it in order.

The second one said:

> I've got a pain in my lower back. It started on Sunday. It's worse when I stand up.
> I lifted a water can on Saturday evening, and it started the next morning. I took something for
> it on Monday and it helped for a few hours.
> I'd like to know if I can go to work tomorrow.

The doctor asked two questions.

The difference is not English. The second one had an order and an ask.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **account** — the whole telling of what happened.
- **in order** — one thing after another, in the order it happened.
- **the ask** — the thing you actually want from this conversation.
- **worse** — more bad than before.
- **better** — less bad than before.

An account has three blocks, and they always go in this order:

1. **The facts** — what, where, since when. Three sentences.
2. **The story** — what you were doing when it started, what you have already tried, what makes it
   worse or better.
3. **The ask** — what you want to happen now.

Beginners usually have block 2, sometimes block 1, and almost never block 3. Block 3 is the one
that changes what the other person does.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a twelve-turn role-play, and the reason a beginner's role-play dies at turn
five is that they have said everything they know in the first two turns. An account with three
blocks gives you a natural shape that keeps going, and the ask gives the other person something to
answer.

## The rule

**Block 1 — the facts, in the shape from [part 1.1](1.1-where-it-hurts.md).**

> I've got a pain in my lower back. It hurts here. It started on Sunday.

**Block 2 — the story, and only what is connected.** Three sentences is plenty:

> I lifted something heavy on Saturday evening.
> It's worse when I stand up, and better when I lie down.
> I took something for it on Monday and it helped for a few hours.

The words to build block 2 with:

| Word | What it adds | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| when | the moment it changes | It hurts when I bend. |
| after | the thing that comes before it | It's worse after food. |
| since | the starting point | I haven't slept properly since Sunday. |
| but | the part that does not fit | I took something, but it came back. |

**Block 3 — the ask, said plainly.** Pick one:

> I'd like to know if it's serious.
> I'd like something for the pain.
> I need a note for my work.
> Should I come back if it doesn't stop?

**Answer the question that was asked, then stop.** This is the habit that makes the rest work. A
doctor's questions are short and so are the answers:

> Does it hurt now? — A little. Not like yesterday.

**And the sentence that carries the most weight:** say what the problem stops you doing.

> I can't sleep with it.
> I can't carry anything with this arm.

## When it breaks

The commonest break is the **loop**. The speaker says the pain, then the story, then the pain
again, then the same story with one more detail. It comes from being nervous, not from bad
English. The fix is the three blocks, said in order, once.

The second is the **missing ask**. The account ends, the doctor says something general, and you
walk out without the note you came for. Nobody was being unkind: you never said it.

The third is the **answer that will not stop**:

> Does it hurt now? — Yes, and yesterday it was very bad, and my mother had the same thing, and
> I think it is from the cold water, and …

The question was *now*. Say *yes*, add one fact, stop.

The fourth is **agreeing to something you did not understand**, which is what happens at the end
of a tiring conversation:

> Take these twice a day before food, and come back if it continues. — Yes, OK. Thank you.

If any of that was unclear, this is the moment to say so, and the repair is one sentence: *sorry,
before food or after food?*

## In real life

Real consultations are interrupted. The doctor will cut into block 2 with a question, and that is
not rudeness — it is them narrowing. Your job is to answer it and then come back to where you
were, which is easier when your account has blocks you can find your place in.

Under pressure people drop block 1 and start at block 2, because the story is the part they have
been rehearsing in their head. A listener who gets the story first has to hold it all until they
learn what it is a story about.

What a native listener forgives: hesitation, a missing word for a body part, starting again. What
costs you something real: leaving without saying what you wanted, and finding out in the corridor.

And one real difference to know: at a counter — a pharmacy, a clinic reception — block 2 is not
wanted at all. It is block 1 plus block 3, in one breath, and then you move aside.
