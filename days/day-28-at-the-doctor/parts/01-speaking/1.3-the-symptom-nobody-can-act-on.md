## One-line answer

Describe your illness on purpose without a body part and without a starting point, play it back,
and count the questions a stranger would have to ask before they could do anything.

## The story

Somebody recorded himself explaining an illness, listened to it, and thought it was fine. Then he
counted.

> I'm not feeling well. I'm very weak. It's not good. I feel bad since some time. I think it's
> because of the weather. I'm really not okay.

Six sentences. Every one of them true. Not one of them contains a part of the body, a moment in
time, or a thing that changed.

A doctor hearing that has to start from nothing and ask everything: where, when, how long, worse
or better, what did you take. Six questions to reach the point where the conversation could have
started.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Speaking only about how you
feel, with no body part and no starting point, is one of the most common things beginners do when
they are worried, which is why it is today's deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **actionable** — something the listener can actually do something about.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you heard it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

A sentence about illness is **actionable** when it contains at least one of these three:

1. a part of the body,
2. a point in time,
3. a thing you cannot do because of it.

A sentence with none of the three is not a symptom. It is a mood, and nobody can treat a mood.

Today you record a version with none of the three, on purpose, and then count what it costs.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the last day in the phase where you can practise being unclear cheaply. In the phase 2
gate role-play, and at every real counter after it, the other person has a queue behind you and
will give you a fixed number of questions before moving you on. Unclear costs turns, and turns are
the thing you do not have.

## The rule

**Take one — the RED version, deliberately.** Record a real illness of yours using only feeling
words. No body part. No day. No *I can't*. Say "this take is deliberately unclear" first, out
loud, on the recording. Aim for six sentences, and make them sound genuinely worried, because that
is what makes this version happen in real life.

**Take two — the GREEN version.** Same illness, using
[part 1.2](1.2-the-whole-account-in-order.md): three facts, three story sentences, one ask.

**Now the count, and it is the check.** Play the RED take to one other person and ask them one
question: *what would you have to ask me before you could help?* Write every question down.

person is worth finding: the questions they ask are never quite the ones you predict.

**Then repair.** Record three pairs, RED then GREEN, one after the other:

> I feel bad. — My throat hurts when I swallow, since Tuesday.
> I'm weak. — I can't climb the stairs without stopping, since last week.
> It's not good. — There's a pain here, on my right side, and it started this morning.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version in a doctor's room is not usually the pure mood version. It is a mixed one,
where one real fact is buried in five feelings:

> I'm not well at all, I'm very tired, my whole body is heavy, and my stomach also, and I'm
> worried because it's not normal for me.

*My stomach* is in there. It arrives fourth, attached to *also*, and the listener has already
started sorting the earlier sentences. That is why a doctor often repeats back: *so — your
stomach?*

The second real wrong version is the opposite failure: a body part with no time.

> My back hurts.

Actionable, and half the information. The doctor's next question is always *since when?*, and you
knew that. Put it in the same breath.

## In real life

People under pain say fewer nouns and more feelings, in every language. That is not a language
problem and it does not go away with practice. What changes with practice is that you have one
prepared sentence — the body part, the day, the thing you cannot do — that survives the worry.

What a native listener does with the mood version: they ask the questions, kindly, and the
conversation still works if there is time. What happens when there is no time — a crowded clinic,
a phone line, a pharmacy — is that they answer the general question you asked with a general
answer, and you both move on with nothing changed.

And the one that matters most: if somebody else ever has to speak for you — a child, a neighbour,
a colleague — they can only repeat what you told them. Body part, day, and the thing you cannot
do, travel. *Not feeling well* does not.
