## One-line answer

An adjective is a word that narrows a noun, it stands either right in front of the noun or after
*is*, *are* or *was*, and it never takes a plural *-s* however many things you are describing.

## The story

You leave a bag on a bus. At the lost property counter they give you a form with one line on it
that says *description*, and you write:

> A bag.

The man behind the counter turns round. Behind him there are about sixty bags on shelves.

You try again:

> A blacks bag, bag of cloth, is big.

He finds it in the end, because you waited, and because you could point at the shelf. But every
word on that line either narrowed the sixty down or did not, and yours mostly did not.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **noun** — the word for a thing, a person or a place: *bag*, *man*, *station*.
- **adjective** — a word that tells you which one or what kind: *black*, *big*, *old*, *cloth*.
- **narrow** — to cut a big group down to a smaller one.
- **plural** — more than one: *bags*, *shelves*.
- **link verb** — *is*, *are*, *was*, *were*, which join a noun to an adjective.

Think of the shelf of sixty bags. Each adjective you add takes bags off the shelf.

| What you write | How many bags are left |
| --- | --- |
| a bag | sixty |
| a black bag | maybe twelve |
| a big black bag | maybe four |
| a big black cloth bag | one |

That is the whole job of an adjective: it removes things that are not yours. An adjective that
removes nothing — *a nice bag* — is a word you spent for free.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every piece of writing left in this phase asks you to make one thing distinguishable from all the
other things like it: the item you lost, the room you are describing, the part that broke, the
person somebody should look for at the station. That is adjectives doing work, and doing it in an
order a reader can follow.

## The rule

**Position one — in front of the noun.**

> a black bag
> a broken handle
> an old phone

**Position two — after a link verb.**

> The bag is black.
> The handle was broken.

Both were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Adjective order](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adjective-order),
which gives before the noun:

> "a nice red dress"
> "a big black car"

and after a link verb:

> "Our teacher was ill."
> "My uncle was very glad when he heard the news."

**The rule that beginners break most: an adjective never becomes plural.** The noun does. The
adjective does not.

```text
   one   black   bag      →   two   black   bags
                                      ↑        ↑
                          no -s here        -s here
```

> two black bags — not *two blacks bags*
> three old houses — not *three olds houses*

**A noun can work as an adjective.** *Cloth*, *leather*, *plastic*, *school*, *water* all stand in
front of another noun and narrow it:

> a cloth bag · a plastic box · a school bus

**Two adjectives need no comma when they come from different families** — a size and a colour, for
example:

> a big black bag

Same family, and you use a comma or *and*:

> a cold, wet morning
> tired and hungry

**And the one to be careful with:** *very* is not an adjective. It is a word that strengthens one.
*Very* on its own describes nothing:

> It was very. ✗
> It was very big. ✓

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **plural adjective**:

> two blacks bags

It comes from a good instinct — everything else in the sentence took an *-s*, so this should too.
A reader understands it and sees a learner immediately. The fix: take the *-s* off the adjective
and leave it on the noun.

The second is the **adjective after the noun**:

> a bag black

This is normal word order in many languages, and English does not allow it in front position. The
listener has to reassemble the phrase. The fix: swap them, or use a link verb — *the bag is
black*.

The third is **the empty adjective**:

> a nice bag, a good bag, a normal bag

Sixty bags are still on the shelf. Nothing was narrowed. The fix is to replace every opinion word
with a fact word: colour, size, material, marks.

The fourth is the **missing link verb**:

> The bag black.

A word is gone, not a small one. The fix: *the bag is black*.

## In real life

Real descriptions written to strangers use fact adjectives almost exclusively, because the reader
is matching your words against objects in front of them. Colour, size, material, and one mark or
fault — that is what a lost property desk, a spare parts shop and an insurance form all want.

Under pressure people write opinions, because opinions are what they feel: *my good bag*, *a very
important document*. A stranger cannot match those against anything.

What a careful reader does with an empty description: they ask for a photo. What a formal reader
does: they write *insufficient detail* and close the file.

And a real difference: in speech you can add adjectives one at a time as the other person
searches — *black … cloth … with a long strap*. In writing you get one go, so the fact adjectives
have to be in the first sentence.
