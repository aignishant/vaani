## One-line answer

When two or three adjectives stand in front of one noun, English puts them in a fixed order —
opinion, size, shape, age, colour, nationality, material — and a reader feels the wrong order
before they can say why.

## The story

The lost property form has room for one line. Two people write about the same bag.

> A cloth black big bag.
> A big black cloth bag.

Both are made of the same four words. The man behind the counter reads the first one twice. He
reads the second one once and walks to the shelf.

Nothing in the first line is wrong in the way a spelling is wrong. It just does not sound like
English, and the reader spends a moment reordering it instead of looking for your bag.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **order** — which word comes first, second, third.
- **opinion** — what you think about it: *nice*, *horrible*, *beautiful*.
- **fact** — what anybody would agree about it: *big*, *black*, *round*, *cloth*.
- **material** — what it is made of: *cloth*, *plastic*, *leather*, *wooden*.
- **nationality** — which country it comes from: *Italian*, *Japanese*.

There is an order, and almost no English speaker can recite it. They can all hear it. That is
worth knowing, because it tells you what kind of rule this is: not a rule anybody enforces, but a
sound a reader expects.

The useful part of the rule is small. **Opinion first, material last, and facts in between.** The
closer an adjective is to the noun, the more permanent it is.

| Position | Family | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | opinion | horrible |
| 2 | size | big |
| 3 | shape | round |
| 4 | age | old |
| 5 | colour | black |
| 6 | nationality | Italian |
| 7 | material | cloth |

Read the table downwards and you get *a horrible big round old black Italian cloth bag*, which
nobody would ever write. In real writing you use two, occasionally three.

This is the difference between a description a stranger can act on and one they have to decode.

## The rule

**Two adjectives is the normal number.** Take one fact from two different families.

> a big black bag
> an old wooden door
> a small red car

These were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Adjective order](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adjective-order),
which gives the two-adjective examples:

> "a big black car"
> "a nice red dress"
> "a silly old man"

and, for three or more:

> "a big black American car"
> "a nice handsome young man"

**The order, as a picture:**

```text
   opinion  →  size  →  shape  →  age  →  colour  →  nationality  →  material  →  NOUN
   nice        big              old       black                        cloth       bag
                     ← less permanent            more permanent →
```

**Three is the ceiling for ordinary writing.** Beyond three, a reader stops holding them and
starts skimming. If you need more, use a second sentence:

> It's a big black cloth bag. There's a red mark on the strap.

**The page is careful, and so is this part.** The same reference notes that the categories are a
guide and that the word *generally* matters: a speaker can move an adjective to stress it. What is
not flexible is opinion before fact and material next to the noun.

**Punctuation.** No commas between adjectives from different families:

> a big black bag

Commas or *and* between adjectives from the same family:

> a cold, grey morning
> tired and hungry

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **material first**, which is the story:

> a cloth black big bag

Every word is right and the reader has to rebuild the phrase. The fix: put the material against
the noun — *a big black cloth bag*.

The second is **opinion buried in the middle**:

> a black nice bag

English wants the opinion out in front, because opinion is the least permanent thing about an
object: *a nice black bag*.

The third is the **pile**:

> a nice lovely big large black dark cloth bag

Six adjectives, two pairs of them saying the same thing twice. A reader takes nothing from this
except that you are unsure. The fix: pick the two that narrow the most.

The fourth is **the comma sprayed between everything**:

> a big, black, cloth, bag

The commas here are noise, and the last one has cut the adjective off from its noun.

## In real life

Real writing almost never uses three adjectives. It uses one or two and then a second sentence
with the distinguishing mark in it, because marks beat adjectives:

> A big black cloth bag with a broken zip and a library card inside.

A zip and a card find a bag. *Nice* does not.

Under pressure — the form, the queue, the phone call — people reach for opinion words first,
because opinions are how we hold objects in our heads. Every one of them costs a space that a
colour or a material could have used.

What a native reader notices about the wrong order: nothing they could name. They hear something
foreign and read the phrase again. It is not a mistake that gets corrected; it is one that gets
tolerated, which is why it survives for years unless somebody points at it, as this part just did.
