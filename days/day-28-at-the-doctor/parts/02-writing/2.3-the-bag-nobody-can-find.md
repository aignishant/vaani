## One-line answer

Write your description twice — once with opinion words and a scrambled order, once with two fact
adjectives and a mark — put both in front of somebody with a pile of real objects, and count which
one they can pick from.

## The story

Somebody wrote a description of a lost water bottle for a notice on a wall:

> Lost: my very nice favourite bottle, plastics blue one, quite big. Please return, it is very
> important for me.

Three people brought bottles to the desk that week. None of them was hers.

Read it again as a stranger holding a blue bottle. *Very nice* removes nothing. *Favourite*
removes nothing. *Quite big* removes almost nothing, because big compared to what? *Plastics* has
an *-s* that should not be there. The only word doing any work is *blue*, and half the bottles in
the building are blue.

She knew exactly which bottle it was. That was the problem: she wrote for somebody who already
knew.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Filling a description with
opinion words and leaving out the one mark that identifies the object is the most common failure
in this kind of writing, which is why it is today's deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **identify** — to pick the right one out of many.
- **mark** — a scratch, a tear, a stain, a name written on it: the thing that makes it yours.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

A description is not judged by whether it is true. It is judged by whether a stranger holding six
similar objects can pick yours. That is a test you can actually run, with real objects, on a real
table, today.

## Why Vaani needs it

Written descriptions are the part of this phase that gets read by somebody who has no way to ask
you a follow-up question. A lost property desk, a form, a message to a shop about the wrong item
delivered, and the phase 2 gate note all work this way. If the description fails, nothing else in
the note matters.

## The rule

**Step 1 — gather the objects.** Put six similar real things on a table: six pens, six cups, six
bags, six shirts. They must be genuinely similar. One of them is yours.

**Step 2 — write the RED description, deliberately.** About your object, using only opinion words,
a scrambled adjective order, and at least one plural adjective:

> My nice favourite bag, cloth blacks big one, very good quality.

Write **deliberate** at the top of it.

**Step 3 — write the GREEN description.** Same object, using
[part 2.2](2.2-the-order-they-stand-in.md): at most two fact adjectives in the right order, plus
one sentence with a mark in it.

> A big black cloth bag. There's a tear near the left corner and a bus ticket in the front pocket.

**Step 4 — run the test.** Give one other person the RED description and ask them to pick the
object from the table. Then give a second person the GREEN one. If only one person is available,
give them the RED one first, note what they pick, then the GREEN one on a fresh sheet.

**Step 5 — fill this in from what happened, not from memory.**

| GREEN | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check.** The RED description must fail — a wrong object, or a right object picked by luck
with the reader saying they were guessing. If it succeeds confidently, your RED was not red
enough, or your six objects were not similar enough. Fix whichever it was and run it again.

**Step 6 — repair.** Rewrite the GREEN description in one line short enough for a form, and count
its words. Then write the mark sentence on its own and ask yourself whether that alone would have
been enough. Usually it would.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version, the one people write without meaning to, is a mix — two fact adjectives
buried in four opinion ones:

> I lost my lovely, very old and very important bag. It is a black one, cloth, medium size, and
> it means a lot to me because my father gave it to me.

The facts are all in there. They arrive third, fifth and sixth, after two sentences that narrow
nothing, and the reader has already started skimming.

The second real wrong version is the **description with no mark**. Every adjective correct, order
correct, and still six objects fit it:

> A big black cloth bag.

Correct English. Not an identification. The mark is the sentence that finishes the job.

## In real life

Desks, offices and shops match your words against objects. They do not read for feeling, and the
sentence about why it matters to you is the one they skip — not from coldness, but because it
cannot be matched against anything on a shelf.

Under pressure people write more, not less, and what they add is emphasis: *very important*,
*please help*, *urgent*. Emphasis narrows nothing.

What a careful reader does with a description they cannot match: they hold it and wait for a
better one, and when a second person describes the same object with a mark in it, the object goes
to the second person. What a formal reader does: files it and asks for a photograph.

And the real-life shape worth copying: one line of two fact adjectives, one line with a mark, one
line saying where and when you lost it. Everything else can go.
