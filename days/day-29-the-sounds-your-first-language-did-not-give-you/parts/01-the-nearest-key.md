# The nearest key your mouth already has

`PR-12` · Day 29

## The one-line answer

There may be English sounds your mouth has never been asked to make, so it plays the nearest key
it already owns — quietly, consistently, and without telling you — and today you find out which
ones.

## The model

Think of the sounds you can make as keys you learned as a child and have played ever since. You
did not choose them. They were handed to you, you got extremely good at them, and you have never
once had to think about where your tongue goes.

English asks for a few keys that may not be on your set. When that happens your mouth does
something sensible and invisible: it plays the nearest key it has. You do not hear the
substitution, because to you it is not one — the nearest key *is* the key. The person listening
hears a slightly different word and works out what you meant from the rest of the sentence, which
they can nearly always do, which is why nobody has ever corrected you.

So this day cannot be taught to you. It has to be found by you, in your own recording. Nobody has
told you which keys are missing, and nobody is going to guess on your behalf. **The recorder
knows and you do not, yet.**

Four places to look. Every check below is one you can run in your own mouth or in a mirror, so
you never have to take anybody's word for it.

**The tongue between the teeth.** English *th* comes in two versions, one with the voice on and
one with it off. Put a finger on your throat and say *think*, then *this* — the second one buzzes
and the first does not. Now the visual check: in a mirror, saying *think*, the tip of your tongue
should be visible, touching the bottom edge of your top teeth or poking just past them. If your
tongue goes instead to the ridge behind your teeth, nothing shows in the mirror. That is a key.

**Teeth on lip, against lips rounded.** For *v* your top teeth rest on your bottom lip and the air
buzzes through the contact. For *w* the lips round and touch nothing at all. Physically the two
are nothing alike, which is the good news — this one you can see and feel rather than only hear.
Mirror check on *very well*: teeth on lip for the first word, lips round and apart for the second.

**The tongue tip up, against the tongue tip nowhere.** For English *l* the tip lands on the ridge
just behind your top teeth and stays there while the sound comes out around the sides. For English
*r* the tip lands on nothing — it hangs, pulled back, and the sound comes from the shape of the
mouth rather than from a contact. Say *light, right, light, right* and pay attention to one thing
only: does the tip land, or does it not?

**The back of the tongue, and the air out of the nose.** The *ng* at the end of *running* is the
back of your tongue closing against the soft part of the roof of your mouth while the air leaves
through your nose. Pinch your nose and try to hold that sound — it stops immediately. That test
is unambiguous. If you can hold it with your nose pinched, you are making something else. Then
check the other half: say *running away* and listen for whether a small *g* escapes in the middle.

**The test that finds your gaps.** Say these four pairs into the recorder, slowly, twice each:

> think · sink
> vine · wine
> light · right
> sin · sing

Then play it back and answer one question per pair: **different, or the same?** Not *is it
correct* — you have no way to judge that from inside your own head, and it does not matter. Just:
on the recording, are your two words distinguishable from each other? If they are not, that pair
is a missing key. It is now a row in `docs/ERRORS.md` and a thing you work on for the rest of this
course.

That is the whole diagnostic, and it is small on purpose. A list of every sound in English would
hand you a project. A pair that came out identical hands you something to do tomorrow.

**Today's phrases** — ordinary sentences with the hard keys stacked inside them, because a sound
you can only make on its own is not a sound you own. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md`
tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Nothing worth worrying about. | Waving off a small problem |
| We were wondering whether … | Raising something on behalf of two of you |
| I've never been very good at that. | Admitting a limit without making a speech of it |
| I'd rather not, really. | Declining without giving a reason |
| Everything's running behind. | Explaining a delay |
| Thanks for holding on. | Somebody waited for you |
| Three or four, roughly. | A rough count you are not committing to |
| The whole thing, or just the one? | Checking how much of it they mean |

## Say it now

Each line three times: once slowly enough to feel where everything lands, once at normal speed,
once faster than is comfortable. The third pass is the diagnostic one — speed is where the nearest
key comes back.

1. *Nothing worth worrying about — {{the thing that went slightly wrong}}.*
2. *We were wondering whether {{the thing you want}}.*
3. *I've never been very good at {{the thing you are bad at}}.*
4. *I'd rather not, really — I'd rather {{what you would do instead}}.*
5. *Everything's running behind because {{the reason}}.*
6. *Thanks for holding on — {{what you were doing}}.*
7. *Three or four, roughly — {{what there are three or four of}}.*
8. *The whole thing, or just the {{part of it}}?*

Then say all eight as one continuous run, twice, with no gaps between them at all. Running them
together removes the breath you were using to set your mouth up, and that is precisely the
condition you want to record.

## The deliberate failure

Take three of today's phrases and say them with the substitution on purpose, everywhere. Every
*th* becomes a *t* or an *s*. Every *v* becomes a *w*. Every *r* becomes an *l*. Say them
confidently, at normal speed, as though nothing at all were wrong.

**What breaking sounds like:** it is understandable. That is the finding, and it is an
uncomfortable one. Nothing collapses. Nobody would ask you to repeat yourself. The sentence
arrives. What you are hearing on playback is precisely why a substitution can live inside somebody's
English for years without ever being corrected — there is no pressure anywhere in the system to
force it out. Only a recording finds it.

Then say the same three phrases with the key restored, and notice the second thing: it is slower
and it costs effort, and the effort is in your mouth rather than in your head. That is what a new
key feels like. It stops feeling like that through repetition, not through understanding.

## When it breaks in the wild

You say one word in a noisy room and it lands as a different word. Somebody repeats it back to
you, wrong. So you say it again, louder. Louder does not fix it, because volume was never the
missing thing — the key was. You end up saying it three times, everybody is slightly
embarrassed, and you change the subject.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — *think*, as in *I think so*.

Give the word inside a phrase they already know, rather than saying the bare word again. A phrase
carries the word for you. An isolated word has nothing holding it up, and it is the one condition
where your weakest key is doing all the work alone.
