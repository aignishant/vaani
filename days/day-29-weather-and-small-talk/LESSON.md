## §1 Where we are

A stranger at a bus stop says something about the cold. The speaking folder is about what that
line is really for, the two small machines that keep a short conversation alive, and the polite
one-word answer that a listener hears as a closed door.

An email lands on a friend's phone while they are on a bus. The writing folder is about the five
parts that make it worth opening, the one question that makes it worth answering, and what comes
back when you send the empty version and the real one to two real people.

Choose either folder first. Each stands on its own. Follow the parts in that folder's order.

## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** In [part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-answer-that-closed-the-door.md), count the
turns in the deliberately closed conversation and play both takes to one other person. If the
closed version ran past six turns, the break did not happen — record it again and let the stranger
give up. If your listener cannot hear any difference between the two takes, that is RED. If they
say the closed one sounds rude, that is GREEN and it is the finding worth keeping.

**Writing:** In [part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-the-email-that-asks-for-nothing.md), send both
emails to two real people and fill in the reply table from what actually arrives. If the two
replies are the same, your GREEN question was not answerable or not specific to that person —
rewrite it and send the repaired version.

Only record what you actually heard or saw. A deliberate exercise is labelled deliberate.

## §7 Traps

- *Yes.* said alone to an opener — correct, polite, and heard as *not now*.
- The tag said flat: *Cold today, isn't it.* with the voice going down.
- An opener about family, money or health with somebody you have never met.
- A whole illness told in answer to a question about the weather.
- Four questions in a row, which is an interview, not a conversation.
- The missing *oh no* when somebody tells you something bad.
- No way out, so the conversation stops instead of ending.
- A subject line that says *Hi*, and an email nobody can find again.
- *Hi,* with no name, which reads as sent to forty people.
- No reason sentence, so the reader does not know why the email exists.
- Four questions in an email; the reader answers the last one.
- The ask softened into *let me know if anything*, and then read as a refusal.
