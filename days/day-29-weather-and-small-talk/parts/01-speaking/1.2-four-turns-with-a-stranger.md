## One-line answer

A short conversation with a stranger runs on two small machines — a noise that shows you are
listening, and a question that hands the turn back — and four turns is a complete, finished
conversation.

## The story

Two people were waiting outside the same clinic.

> — Long wait today.
> — Yes. Very long.
> — …

That was the whole thing. Both of them were friendly. Neither of them did anything wrong. The
conversation simply had nothing to run on: the second speaker agreed and stopped, and the first
one had already spent their one opener.

Later the same week, the same two people:

> — Long wait today.
> — I know. I've been here since they opened.
> — Really? That's a long morning.
> — It is. Are you waiting for the same doctor?
> — No, the other one. — Ah, she's quick, you'll be fine.

Four turns each, and both of them went home having had a small good moment. The only difference
was two habits: showing interest, and handing the turn back.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **turn** — one person's piece of the talking.
- **hand back** — to end your turn in a way that makes it easy for the other person to speak.
- **showing interest** — short words and noises that say *I am listening and I want more*.
- **echo** — repeating one word of what they said.

A small conversation needs only two machines:

1. **The interest noise.** Short. Said fast. It costs you nothing and it tells the other person
   the door is still open.
2. **The handback.** A question, or an echo said with a rise. It moves the turn across the gap.

Everything else — vocabulary, grammar, being interesting — is optional. A conversation dies
because one side stopped handing the turn back, not because somebody used the wrong tense.

## Why Vaani needs it

Twelve turns is the phase 2 gate. Twelve turns is not twelve sentences you prepared; it is six
handbacks. This part teaches the handback, and it is the single most portable thing in the whole
phase: it works at a counter, on a phone call, at a desk, and with your neighbour.

## The rule

**Machine one — show interest.** These were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Showing interest](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/showing-interest):

> "Really?" · "No way!" · "How cool!" · "You're joking!" · "Oh no, poor you." · "I'm sorry to hear
> that." · "That's brilliant!"

They are not decoration. Pick by what you were told:

| They told you something | You say |
| --- | --- |
| good | That's brilliant! · How cool! |
| bad | Oh no. · I'm sorry to hear that. |
| surprising | Really? · You're joking! |
| ordinary | Right. · I know. |

**Machine two — hand the turn back.** Three ways, easiest first:

```text
   echo + rise        →  Since they opened?
   short question     →  Are you waiting for the same doctor?
   your fact + and you?  →  I've got the nine o'clock. What about you?
```

**Put the two together and you have a turn:**

> Really? That's a long morning. Are you waiting for the same doctor?

That is the whole skill: **noise, then a fact of yours, then the turn back.** Say it as one breath.

**Four turns is a whole conversation.** It has a shape:

| Turn | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | the opener — the safe fact |
| 2 | agree and add one thing |
| 3 | interest noise and a handback |
| 4 | an answer, and the way out |

**The way out is part of the conversation, not the end of it.** Say it warmly and leave:

> Anyway — good luck in there. Nice talking to you.

**And the repair, for when you did not catch something.** Strangers speak fast and it is not your
fault:

> Sorry, I didn't catch that.

## When it breaks

The commonest break is the **dead agree**:

> — I've been here since they opened.
> — Yes.

Nothing wrong, nothing handed back. The other person now has to start the conversation for a
second time, and most people do not.

The second is the **interview**, which happens when a learner knows questions are good and uses
only questions:

> Where are you from? How long have you been here? What is your job? Do you have children?

Every one is grammatical. Together they are a form being filled in. The fix: give one fact of your
own before each question.

The third is **the missing noise**. The stranger tells you something bad and you go straight to
your next question:

> — My mother's inside, she had a fall.
> — Are you waiting for the same doctor?

In English the missing *oh no* is heard as coldness. It is one word and it is not optional.

The fourth is **no way out**. The conversation just stops, or you walk off mid-sentence. The
stranger is left wondering what they said. One line fixes it.

## In real life

Real small talk with strangers is asymmetric: one person is more willing than the other, and the
willing one carries it. Sometimes that will be you, and carrying it is four handbacks, not
brilliance.

Under pressure — a fast speaker, a noisy street, an accent you have not heard before — the first
thing to go is the interest noise, because your whole attention is on decoding. What survives
best, and is worth drilling until it is automatic, is *really?* and *right*, said at the right
moment.

What a native listener notices: not your grammar. They notice whether you seemed interested, and
they judge that almost entirely on the noises and the handbacks. What a native listener forgives:
almost everything else.

And the thing nobody says out loud: many strangers will not want to talk, and their *yes* is a
full stop. That is not a failed conversation and it is not about your English. Say your line,
accept the answer, look at the road.
