## One-line answer

Record a small conversation where every answer is correct and one word long, listen to where it
dies, and hear that politeness and coldness sound exactly the same in English.

## The story

Somebody practised small talk with his cousin and asked afterwards how it went. His cousin said,
carefully, that he had sounded *a bit not interested*.

He played the recording back. Every answer he gave was correct English:

> — Terrible weather today.
> — Yes.
> — Have you been waiting long?
> — No.
> — Is this the right stop for the museum?
> — Yes.

Three correct answers. Three closed doors. He had been concentrating so hard on getting each
answer right that he had answered exactly the question and nothing more — and in English, exactly
the question and nothing more is what you say to somebody you want to stop talking to.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. The one-word answer is the
most common thing a careful beginner does in small talk, which is why it is today's deliberate
break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **closed answer** — an answer with nothing for the other person to hold.
- **open answer** — an answer that gives them something to say next.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you heard it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

In English the size of your answer is a message. It is not about grammar and nobody will ever
correct you, because nothing is wrong.

| What you say | What the other person hears |
| --- | --- |
| Yes. | I am busy, or I don't want to talk. |
| Yes, freezing. | I'm friendly but I'm leaving it here. |
| Yes, freezing — I couldn't get warm all morning. | Talk to me. |

Today you build the top row on purpose, record it next to the bottom row, and listen to the two
next to each other.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is twelve turns, and a twelve-turn role-play built on one-word answers is
impossible — it ends at turn four whatever the topic is. More than that: the one-word habit is the
one that quietly decides whether people at your work, your building or your child's school talk to
you again, and no one of them will ever tell you why.

## The rule

**Take one — the closed version, deliberately.** Record a whole small-talk conversation, both
voices. The stranger is warm and asks good questions. You answer every single one correctly, and
in one or two words, with no fact added and no question back. Say "this take is deliberately
closed" first, out loud.

> — Cold today, isn't it? — Yes.
> — Have you been waiting long? — No.
> — Do you take this bus every day? — Yes.

**Take two — the open version.** Same questions, same stranger, agree-plus-one every time and at
least three handbacks, using [part 1.2](1.2-four-turns-with-a-stranger.md).

**The check, and it has two ways to go RED.**

- Count the turns in the closed version. If it ran past six turns, your stranger was doing all the
  work and the break did not happen — record it again with the stranger giving up naturally.
- Play both takes to one other person and ask one question: *does this person want to talk?* If
  they cannot hear the difference, that is RED for the exercise. If they say the closed one sounds
  rude, that is GREEN and it is also the finding worth keeping — because you did not intend rude,
  you intended careful.

**Then repair.** Record five pairs, closed then open, fast, one after the other:

> Yes. — Yes, since seven. It's freezing out here.
> No. — No, first time. Is it usually this slow?
> I don't know. — I don't know, sorry. I'm new here too.

That last pair matters most: *I don't know* is a closed door, and *I don't know, sorry — I'm new
here too* is a conversation.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is rarely a whole conversation of one-word answers. It is a conversation
where the first two answers are open and then, as soon as a question is hard, the answers close:

> — Do you work near here? — Yes, in the market, just behind the school.
> — Oh, which part? — ... Yes. The market.

The closing is the sound of somebody's English running out, and a stranger cannot tell that from
somebody losing interest. They hear the same thing.

The fix is one sentence that says the true reason, and it is worth learning by heart:

> Sorry — my English isn't quick. I work in the market, behind the school.

Nobody minds that. What they mind is being shut out with no reason given.

## In real life

Strangers make a decision about you in about two turns, and the closed answer is read as a
decision you made about them. They will not test it twice.

Under pressure — an accent you cannot follow, a fast question, a word you did not catch — closing
is the natural move, because a short answer is a safe answer. That is exactly when the repair
sentence earns its keep.

What a native listener does with a run of one-word answers: stops asking, kindly, and turns back
to their phone. Nothing is said, nothing is corrected, and you will never know it happened. That
silent cost is why this failure is worth building on purpose, once, where you can hear it.
