## One-line answer

A short friendly email has five parts — subject, greeting, the reason you are writing, the middle,
and a sign-off — and the reason is the one people leave out.

## The story

A friend of yours moved to another city and you have not written for months. You sit down and
write:

> Hi,
>
> How are you? Everything is fine here. My work is the same. The weather is hot. I hope you are
> well. Bye.

You send it. She reads it on a bus, thinks *that's nice*, means to reply properly later, and does
not.

Nothing in it was wrong. There was also nothing in it: no subject line to catch her eye, no reason
for the email to exist, nothing she could answer, and no question that needed her.

An email that asks for nothing gets nothing.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **subject line** — the short line at the top that says what the email is about.
- **greeting** — the first line: *Hi Priya*, *Dear Sir*.
- **sign-off** — the last line before your name: *See you soon*, *Take care*.
- **informal** — friendly, for somebody you know.
- **reason for writing** — the sentence that says why this email exists.

The five parts, and what each is for:

| Part | What it is for | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subject line | so they open it, and so they can find it again | a few words |
| Greeting | so it is addressed to a person, not to nobody | one line |
| Reason for writing | so they know what this is before they read | one sentence |
| The middle | the actual thing you have to say | two to four sentences |
| Sign-off | so it ends instead of stopping | one line |

The reason and the subject line are the two that beginners skip, and they are the two that decide
whether the email is read now or later.

## Why Vaani needs it

Two of the phase's remaining written pieces are emails, and the phase 2 gate note has to do three
jobs in a hundred words — which is only possible if every sentence knows why it is there. Learning
the five parts on a friendly email is the cheap way to learn them, because a friend will forgive a
clumsy sentence and an office will not.

## The rule

**The model.** This one was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[An email to congratulate a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/email-congratulate-colleague),
and the whole shape is visible in it:

> "Subject: Great presentation!"
> "Hi Jon"
> "I'm just writing to say well done on your presentation this morning."
> "You were well prepared. The content was relevant and easy to follow."
> "See you later"

**Its own tips say the same five things**, from the same page, checked the same day:

> "Write a clear subject line."
> "It's OK to start with Hi for an informal or friendly email."
> "Start with your reason for writing: I'm just writing to say …"
> "You can finish with See you later or Speak soon."

**The reason sentence — three shapes that always work:**

```text
   I'm just writing to say ...        →  I'm just writing to say thank you for Sunday.
   I wanted to tell you about ...     →  I wanted to tell you about my new job.
   I've got some news.                →  I've got some news. I'm moving in October.
```

**The subject line is not a greeting.** *Hello* is a bad subject line. The subject line says what
is inside:

| Weak | Works |
| --- | --- |
| Hello | My new job |
| Hi from me | Sunday — are you free? |
| Email | Photos from the wedding |

**The greeting, for a friend:** *Hi Priya,* or *Hi Priya*. A comma after the name is normal and
so is nothing. The name matters more than the punctuation: an email that starts *Hi,* with no name
reads like it was sent to forty people.

**The sign-off, for a friend:** *See you soon*, *Speak soon*, *Take care*, *Write soon*. Then your
name on the next line.

**One topic per email.** Two topics is two emails, or one email the reader answers half of.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **email with no reason**, which is the story:

> Hi, How are you? Everything is fine here. Bye.

It is polite and empty. The reader has nothing to do. The fix is one sentence near the top saying
why you wrote: *I'm just writing to tell you I've moved.*

The second is the **subject line that says nothing**: *Hi*, *Hello*, *Message*. Six months later
neither of you can find the email that had the address in it.

The third is the **greeting with no name**:

> Hi,

It costs one word to fix, and the word is their name.

The fourth is **no sign-off**, so the email just stops after the last fact. It reads as if you were
interrupted.

The fifth is **everything at once** — the new job, the health problem, the money question, and a
photo request, in one block. Whatever the reader answers, three things are dropped.

## In real life

Most friendly emails are read on a phone, between other things. The reader sees the subject line
and the first line, and decides then whether to read now or later — and *later* usually means
never.

So real writers put the point in the subject line and the reason in the first sentence, and keep
the whole thing short enough to be read in one screen. The long warm email is a real thing and it
is for people who are expecting it.

Under pressure people write the middle and forget the top, because the middle is the part they
care about. An email with a strong middle and no subject line still loses to one with both.

What a friend does with a shapeless email: replies eventually, or not. What a formal reader does
with one: files it under a subject line they wrote themselves, and answers the part they
understood.
