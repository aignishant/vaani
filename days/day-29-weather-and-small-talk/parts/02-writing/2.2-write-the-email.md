## One-line answer

Write one email about one thing, end it with one question the reader can answer in a line, and it
will get a reply — the question is what makes an email a conversation instead of an announcement.

## The story

Two emails arrived at the same friend on the same evening.

The first:

> Subject: Hello
>
> Hi, hope you are good. Life is busy here, work is the same, my brother came last week, the
> weather has been bad, and also I am thinking about changing my job but not sure. Anyway. Bye.

The second:

> Subject: I might change jobs — can I ask you something?
>
> Hi Meera,
>
> I'm writing because I need your opinion about something. There's a job going at the place near
> the station, and it's more money but further away.
>
> You did the same thing two years ago. Was the travelling as bad as you expected?
>
> Speak soon,
> Anand

The first one was answered with *good to hear from you!* The second one got four paragraphs back,
because it asked one clear question that only that person could answer.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **one topic** — one subject for the whole email.
- **the ask** — the question or request at the end.
- **answerable** — a question the reader can answer without work.
- **paragraph** — a block of sentences with a blank line before and after it.

An email that gets replies has three properties, and none of them is good English:

1. **One topic.** The reader can hold it.
2. **One question, near the end.** The reader knows what to do.
3. **Short paragraphs.** The reader can see the shape on a phone screen.

The question is the engine. A question that only this person can answer — because of what they
know, or did, or saw — is the strongest kind, and it is why the second email got four paragraphs.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the last piece of writing in the phase that is allowed to be purely friendly. After it,
the written pieces ask for things: a favour, an answer, an explanation. The shape you practise
here — reason, middle, one clear ask — is exactly that shape, learned on a reader who likes you.

## The rule

**The layout on the page.** This is part of the writing, not decoration:

```text
   Subject: one line, says what is inside

   Hi <name>,
   <blank line>
   <the reason you are writing — one sentence>
   <blank line>
   <the middle — two to four sentences, one topic>
   <blank line>
   <your question>
   <blank line>
   <sign-off>
   <your name>
```

**The reason sentence, again**, in the shape checked on 2026-09-22 at
[An email to congratulate a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/email-congratulate-colleague):

> "I'm just writing to say well done on your presentation this morning."

Yours will be:

> I'm just writing to tell you about the job I'm thinking about.

**The middle — two to four sentences, and each one adds a fact, not a feeling.**

> There's a job going at the place near the station. It's more money, but it's an hour further
> away. I have to answer them by Friday.

**The question — one, specific, and answerable in a line.**

| Weak question | Works |
| --- | --- |
| What do you think? | Was the travelling as bad as you expected? |
| Any news? | Are you free on Sunday afternoon? |
| How are things? | Did your sister find a flat in the end? |

The weak ones are not wrong; they simply hand the reader a big empty space, and a tired reader
puts a big empty space down.

**The sign-off**, from the same page, checked the same day:

> "See you later" · "Speak soon"

**Short forms are right here.** *I'm*, *it's*, *don't*, *I've*. A friendly email written entirely
in full forms reads stiff, and this course teaches the apostrophes for them on their own day.

**Punctuation that decides how this reads:** a full stop at the end of every sentence, a comma
after *Hi Meera*, a question mark on the question, and a blank line between every block. Four
sentences run together in one block is the most common reason a friendly email looks like a
complaint.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **block**: five sentences with no blank lines, no paragraph
breaks and three different topics in them. On a phone it is a grey wall, and the question inside
it is invisible.

The second is the **four questions**:

> How is your family? Did you get the photos? Are you still at that job? Is your mother better?

The reader answers the last one and forgets the rest. One question gets a full answer; four get
one.

The third is the **apology opening**:

> Sorry I haven't written for so long, I have been very busy, sorry again, I am a bad friend.

Three lines spent before anything is said. One short line is enough — *sorry for the slow reply* —
and then the reason for writing.

The fourth is **the ask hidden in the middle**:

> … and it's an hour further away, and I wondered what you thought, and also my brother came last
> week …

The question is buried between two facts. Put it on its own line.

## In real life

Real friendly emails between busy people are short: a reason, three facts, one question. The long
one is saved for news that deserves it — and even then it starts with the reason.

Under pressure — a long silence between friends, a difficult subject, a favour to ask — people
write more and ask less. The apology grows, the middle grows, and the question shrinks until it is
a polite *anyway, let me know*.

What a friend does with an email that asks nothing: sends a warm line back, and the thread ends.
What a friend does with one clear question: answers it, and usually adds their own news on top —
which is how the letter you actually wanted arrives.

And the real-life difference between this and the office version, which you will need later: the
office version says the same five parts with a *Dear* at the top and no *xx* at the bottom. The
shape does not change. Only the temperature does.
