## One-line answer

Send the empty version of your own email to a real person, then the real one, and compare the two
replies — the reply is the only honest measure a piece of writing has.

## The story

Somebody wrote two emails to two cousins on the same evening. Same length, same warmth, same
English.

The first:

> Subject: Hi
>
> Hi, how are you? All is well here. Work is going on. Hope your family is fine. Take care.

The second:

> Subject: Your old college books
>
> Hi Ravi,
>
> I'm writing because I'm clearing out the back room and I've found your old college books.
>
> Do you want me to keep them, or can I give them away?

The first got a reply three days later: *all good here, hope you're well too.* The second got a
reply in an hour with an answer in it.

The difference was not grammar. Both emails were correct. One of them gave the reader a job.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. The friendly email that asks
for nothing is the most common piece of writing beginners produce, which is why it is today's
deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **empty** — containing nothing the reader can act on.
- **reply rate** — whether people actually answer you.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

A written piece can be graded two ways. You can read it yourself and decide it is good — which
tells you almost nothing, because you already know what you meant. Or you can send it and see what
comes back, which tells you everything.

Today you run that second test on purpose, with both versions, and with two real people.

## Why Vaani needs it

Everything left in this phase is writing that has to make somebody do something: answer, agree,
send, come, stop. The phase 2 gate note asks for something, thanks somebody and gives a direction.
This part is where you find out, on real people, that the asking is the part that works — and it
is far better to find that out on a cousin than on a landlord.

## The rule

**Step 1 — write the RED email, deliberately.** To a real person you actually know. It must be:

- warm and correct,
- about nothing in particular,
- with a subject line that says nothing — *Hi*, *Hello*, *Message*,
- with no reason sentence and no question.

Keep a copy, and write **deliberate** on your copy, not in the email itself.

**Step 2 — write the GREEN email.** To a different real person, the same length, using
[part 2.2](2.2-write-the-email.md): a subject line with the topic in it, a reason sentence, a
middle on one topic, one specific question on its own line, a sign-off.

**Step 3 — send both.** This is the part that makes today real. Two real people, two real emails
or messages, sent.

**The check.** The GREEN email should get a longer or faster reply, and a reply with an *answer*
in it. If both replies are the same, look at your GREEN email again and ask the hard question: was
the question really answerable in one line, and was it a question only that person could answer?

If the RED email gets the better reply — and this happens when that person simply likes you — then
the finding is about the reader, not the writing, and it is worth writing down too. Some people
answer anything. Most people answer questions.

**Step 5 — repair.** Rewrite the RED email properly and send that as a second message to the same
person: subject line, reason, one question. Then write down what came back.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not usually as empty as the deliberate one. It is an email with a real
topic and a dissolved ask:

> … so I might come to the city next month, it depends on work. Anyway, let me know if anything.
> Hope you're well!

There is a question in there — *can I stay with you?* — and it was never written. The reader
cannot answer a question that is not on the page, and the writer ends up believing the reader said
no.

The second real wrong version is the **question that is not a question**:

> It would be nice to meet sometime if you are free.

Kind, and unanswerable, because nothing was proposed. *Are you free on Sunday afternoon?* is a
question. *Sometime* never happens.

## In real life

People reply to emails in the order of how easy the reply is. An email with one clear question is
easy and gets answered on the spot; an email with no question waits for a moment of free time that
may not come. This is not rudeness, and it is not about how much they like you.

Under pressure — asking for something awkward, a long silence, a favour — writers soften the ask
until it disappears, then read the silence as a refusal. The softening is where the email failed,
not the relationship.

What a friend does with an empty email: sends warmth back. What a busy or formal reader does:
files it and waits for you to say what you want. And what happens with the important ones — the
flat, the job, the appointment, the favour — is that the unanswered version arrives in your head
as *they said no*, when nobody said anything at all.
