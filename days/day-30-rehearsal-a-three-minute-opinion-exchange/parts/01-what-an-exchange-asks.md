# What an exchange asks that a monologue doesn't

`CF-08` · Day 30

## The one-line answer

Every piece in your portfolio so far you controlled from the first word to the last — a
three-minute opinion exchange is the first one where somebody else gets to hit the ball back, and
the whole skill is in the returns.

## The model

Look at what you own. Five recordings, all of them monologues. You started, you ran, you stopped.
Even the ones that went badly went badly on your own terms.

An exchange is a rally. Two people, one ball, and the piece only exists while it is in the air.
Which means there are two ways to lose it, and they are opposites.

**You never let go of it.** Somebody asks what you think and you deliver a small speech. Fluent,
coherent, and by the end you can hear yourself restating your first point in new words, because
you ran out of material and had no way to stop. Nobody can return a speech.

**You never hold it.** Somebody says something, you agree in three words, and it drops. Now they
have to serve again. Do that four times and they stop serving.

Three minutes is a specific and slightly cruel length. It is too long to hold alone — you will run
out. It is too short to fill with agreement — the topic will die first. It is exactly long enough
to force the thing this day exists for: **hold, then hand back, several times over.**

**Holding.** A turn worth returning has a position and a reason attached, and it puts the position
first.

> The way I see it, …
> …which is why I think …

That order is the trick. Position first, reason after. Reason first is where most people stall:
you build a case for something you have not named yet, you can hear yourself getting further from
the point, and by the time you arrive at the position the turn is spent.

Then one concrete thing, because an abstraction cannot be returned.

> Take this, for example —

And one line that marks the load-bearing part, so the other person knows where to hit.

> That's the bit I keep coming back to.

**Handing back.** This is the half that gets skipped, and it is the half that makes it an
exchange rather than two people waiting.

> Where do you stand on that?
> Right, your turn — I've been talking.

You already have *What about you?* from Day 6, and it still works. What it does not do is point at
anything, and after a real opinion turn a vague handback gets a vague return. *Where do you stand
on that?* names the thing, and it cannot be answered with *yeah*.

The second one is for when you notice you have held the ball too long. Say it out loud. It costs
nothing, it is true, and it buys back everything the long turn spent.

**What today spends.** You are not learning opinion language today — you have it. Day 27 gave you
the frames for saying what you think and taking the edge off it. Day 28 gave you the dial:
*No, you're right*, *Yeah, up to a point*, *That's true, although…*, *I see what you mean, but…*,
*That's where I'd disagree*. Today those get used at speed, against somebody who is not waiting
politely for you to finish. Have them loaded. Do not sit and re-teach them to yourself.

**Today's phrases** — all of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| The way I see it, … | Opening a turn with your position instead of a run-up |
| …which is why I think … | Attaching the reason without letting the turn stall |
| Take this, for example — | Buying your next sentence with something concrete |
| That's the bit I keep coming back to. | Marking the one part you actually care about |
| Where do you stand on that? | Handing back so it cannot be answered with *yeah* |
| Right, your turn — I've been talking. | Handing back once you notice you have held it too long |

## Say it now

Pick one claim you have a real opinion about. The same claim for all six lines.

1. *The way I see it, {{your position, in one sentence}}.*
2. *…which is why I think {{what follows from it}}.*
3. *Take this, for example — {{one concrete thing}}.*
4. *That's the bit I keep coming back to. {{the part that matters}}.*
5. *Where do you stand on that?*
6. *Right, your turn — I've been talking.*

Then run lines 1 to 5 as one continuous turn, twice, and stop dead on the question. Add nothing
after it. The stopping is the rep — a handback with a sentence trailing off behind it is not a
handback, and you will only hear the difference by doing both.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d30-free.m4a`.**

> Three short turns on three different claims. Each turn: position, reason, one concrete example,
> handback. Stop dead on the handback every time, and leave the silence in the recording.

Do not fill the gap. The gap is doing real work — it is the signal that the ball is theirs now,
and you need to be able to hear yourself make one.

- **Solo form:** as written. Leave the silences in and do not stop the recorder between turns.
- **With a partner:** they return each handback with one sentence and then say nothing. You take
  it back and hold again. Three rounds, no coaching in between.

## The deliberate failure

Take one claim and hold it for as long as you possibly can. Do not hand back. When you run out,
keep going anyway.

**What breaking sounds like:** first the sentences get longer and the joins get thinner — *and,
and also, and I think as well* — because you are buying room. Then you will hear yourself say
your first point again in different words. That restatement is the sound of a turn that should
already have ended, and once you have heard it in your own voice you will start catching it while
it is happening.

Now run the same claim again and hand back at the first natural end. It will feel much too early.
Listen to both. The short one is the better piece, and it is not close.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are in a group. Somebody asks the room what people think. You have an actual opinion, you
take one careful sentence to say it, and somebody else picks the topic up and runs with it. Your
point is now in the room with none of your reasoning attached to it, and there is no obvious way
back in.

**The repair:**

> Can I come back to that? The way I see it, …

Re-entry is an ordinary move and it does not need an apology in front of it. What actually loses
the point is treating one missed turn as the end of your turn.

## Check yourself

Play back `audio/d30-free.m4a` once. Pass if:

- [ ] All three turns say the position before the reason. If any one of them builds first and
      lands the position at the end, that is the one to run again.
- [ ] Each turn ends on a handback and then silence — you can hear the gap, and nothing of yours
      comes after it.

The second condition is the one worth being strict about. The gap feels much longer from the
inside than it is on the recording, and that mismatch is why most people talk through their own
handbacks without ever knowing they do it.
