## One-line answer

Most of the vowels in spoken English are not the vowel the spelling promises — they collapse into
one short, dull sound made in the middle of the mouth, and that sound has a name and a symbol.

## The story

A man asks you where the station is. You say it carefully, one clear piece at a time:

> It is a-BOUT ten minutes. Take the SEC-OND road.

He understands you, and then he answers, and what comes back is:

> Yeah, it's abt ten minutes, second road, past the c'mputer shop.

You heard *abt*. You heard *c'mputer*. He did not swallow those sounds by accident and he was not
being lazy about you — that is simply where those vowels go in every English sentence he says.

Until you know that sound, half of what English speakers say will sound like it has holes in it.
And your own speech will sound like every syllable is shouting.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **vowel** — a sound made with the mouth open, no tongue or lips blocking it: the *a* in *bat*,
  the *ee* in *see*.
- **syllable** — one beat of a word. *Ba-na-na* has three.
- **stressed syllable** — the loud, long one. *baNAna*.
- **unstressed syllable** — all the others: short, quiet, quick.
- **schwa** — the dull vowel that lives in unstressed syllables, written **ə**.

The name and the symbol were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Schwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwa), which says: "The **mid central vowel** is a type of
vowel sound, used in some spoken languages. A reduced mid central vowel is known as a **schwa**",
and gives the symbol as ⟨ə⟩, "a rotated lowercase letter e".

Here is the thing that makes it worth a whole day: **the spelling does not tell you where it is.**
Five different letters all become the same schwa when the syllable is unstressed.

| Word | The letter on the page | What comes out of the mouth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| about | a | ə |
| banana | the first and last a | ə |
| teacher | er | ə |
| computer | o and er | ə |

Same sound, four spellings. There is no rule in the spelling. The rule is in the **stress**.

## Why Vaani needs it

Two things break without it. You will not *hear* English properly — the unstressed syllables
arrive as blurs, and you will think people are speaking too fast when in fact they are speaking
normally and you are listening for vowels that are not there. And your own English will sound
heavy, because every syllable that comes out full and strong makes the listener work to find the
important word.

This is also the day that makes day 13's rhythm real: the rhythm of English is loud syllables with
schwas squashed between them.

## The rule

**Where the schwa lives: every syllable that is not the loud one.** Find the loud syllable first,
and assume the rest are schwa until you hear otherwise.

```text
   a - BOUT          ə - ˈbaʊt          the first syllable is a schwa
   ba - NA - na      bə - ˈnɑː - nə     the first and the last are schwas
   TEA - cher        ˈtiː - tʃə         the second is a schwa
   com - PU - ter    kəm - ˈpjuː - tə   the first and the last are schwas
```

**Listen to all four, and then say them.** Each of these pages has a British pronunciation with a
sound file on it. All four were opened and checked on 2026-09-22.

| Word | Listen here | What the page gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| about | [about](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/about) | /əˈbaʊ̯t/, with audio for southern England and for General American |
| banana | [banana](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/banana) | /bəˈnɑːnə/ for Received Pronunciation, with audio; /bəˈnænə/ for General American, with audio |
| teacher | [teacher](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/teacher) | /ˈtiː.t͡ʃəː/ for Received Pronunciation, with audio for the UK and southern England |
| computer | [computer](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/computer) | /kəmˈpjuː.tə/ for Received Pronunciation, with audio for southern England |

**The instruction is always the same: listen, then say it with the recorder on, then play both
back.** Not once — four or five times each, until your version and theirs start at the same place.

**How to make the sound.** This is the easiest vowel in English and the hardest to believe.

```text
             your mouth, from the side

        front  ←   middle   →   back
              \      ə      /        relax the tongue
   open          \   ·   /           relax the jaw
                   \ | /             make a short voiced sound
                     ‾               and do not aim at anything
```

Say *ah*. Now say it with your mouth almost closed and your tongue doing nothing at all, very
short, very quiet. That is it. The schwa is the sound your mouth makes when it is not trying.

**The test that proves it is there.** Say *banana* three times, faster each time. The first and
last vowels get shorter and duller and the middle one does not. That difference is the schwa.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the spelling vowel**, said full and proud:

> ah-BOUT · ba-NA-nah · TEA-cher with a clear *er* · com-PYOO-ter with a clear *o*

Every one is understandable. Together, across a whole conversation, they make English that sounds
strange in a way listeners cannot name — and they also make you slower, because full vowels take
time.

The second is **stress on the wrong syllable**, which then puts the schwa in the wrong place:

> COM-puter → the *u* goes dull instead of the *o*, and the word stops being recognisable.

This one really does cost understanding. Vowels can be wrong and the word survives. Stress in the
wrong place and it often does not.

The third is the opposite mistake, made by learners who have just discovered the schwa: **schwa
everywhere**, including in the stressed syllable. *bənənə*. Now the word has no loud syllable at
all and nobody can find it.

## In real life

Fast English is not faster consonants. It is more schwas: the unstressed syllables get squeezed
until they nearly vanish, while the stressed ones stay roughly where they were. That is why
listening feels like the hard part.

Under pressure — reading aloud, saying your address to somebody official, spelling something out —
speakers of every language go back to full vowels, because full vowels feel careful. They are
also the version that makes you hardest to follow at normal speed.

What a native listener notices: not individual schwas. They notice a speaker who sounds like they
are reading, and they slow down for you — which is kind and also means you get less practice at
normal speed.

What is forgiven completely: the exact quality of the schwa. It is a dull, unaimed sound, and
nobody is listening closely to it. That is the freedom in today's lesson — you cannot make a bad
schwa, only a schwa that is too loud.
