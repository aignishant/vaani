## One-line answer

The small words that hold a sentence together — *a*, *to*, *of*, *for*, *and*, *can*, *was* —
almost all lose their vowel to the schwa when they are not the important word, and saying them
full is what makes a fluent sentence sound like a list.

## The story

You ask somebody in the corridor what they are doing later and they say:

> I've gotta go tə the bank fə my mother.

You hear four words you know — *go*, *bank*, *mother* — and three blurs. You ask them to repeat it.
They repeat it exactly the same way, because as far as they are concerned they said it clearly,
and the second time you hear one more word.

Now say your own version out loud:

> I have to go TO the bank FOR my mother.

Correct. Careful. And every single word in it is the same size, so nothing in it is important.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **content word** — a word that carries meaning: *bank*, *mother*, *go*, *red*.
- **function word** — a small word that holds the sentence together: *a*, *to*, *of*, *for*,
  *and*, *the*, *can*, *was*, *from*.
- **strong form** — the word said in full, the way it sounds alone.
- **weak form** — the same word with its vowel collapsed to a schwa.

English has two pronunciations for most of its small words, and they are not optional extras. The
existence of the pair was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Connected speech](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/articles/connected-speech),
which states: "There are a large number of words in English which can have a 'full' form and a
'weak' form", and explains that because English is stress-timed, speakers reduce the words that
are not carrying the meaning — conjunctions, pronouns, prepositions, auxiliaries and articles — to
keep the rhythm between stressed syllables.

So the rule that runs the rhythm of the whole language is this:

> **Content words stay strong. Function words go weak.**

| Word | Strong form | Weak form | In a sentence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| to | too | tə | I'm going tə work. |
| of | ov | əv | A cup əv tea. |
| for | fore | fə | It's fə you. |
| and | and | ən | bread ən butter |
| a | ay | ə | ə minute |
| can | can | kən | I kən come. |
| was | woz | wəz | He wəz here. |
| from | from | frəm | It's frəm my sister. |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the day your listening changes. Nearly every word a learner "misses" in fast speech is a
weak-form function word, and once you know the weak forms exist you stop hearing gaps and start
hearing words.

It also changes what a listener hears from you. When every word is strong, the listener has no way
to know which word matters; when the small ones go weak, the important ones stand out without you
raising your voice at all.

## The rule

**Squeeze the function word against the word after it.** They become one piece:

> to the → tə thə
> a cup of tea → ə cup əv tea
> for my mother → fə my mother

**Say the sentence in beats, not in words.** The strong words are the beats, and everything else
is squeezed into the gaps:

```text
   I'm going    to the     BANK       for my      MOTHER
      ·        tə thə      BEAT        fə my       BEAT
```

Two beats in that sentence. Nine words.

**Three sentences to drill, slowly then fast:**

> I'd like ə cup əv tea.
> I kən meet you ət four.
> He wəz here fə ten minutes ən then he left.

**The one big exception: at the end of a sentence, the weak form comes back strong.** Compare:

> Who's it for? — *for* is the last word, so it is strong: *fore*.
> It's for you. — *for* is in the middle, so it is weak: *fə*.

Same with *to*, *of*, *from*, *at*. Last word in the sentence, strong. Anywhere else, weak.

**And the other exception: when you correct somebody, the small word goes strong because it is now
the important word.**

> I said it's FROM my sister, not TO my sister.

That is the whole system in one line: the strong form is for the word that matters, and the weak
form is for everything else.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **all-strong sentence**, which is the story:

> I have to go TO the bank FOR my mother.

Nothing is wrong, and a listener has to do the work of deciding what mattered. Over a paragraph it
makes you sound like somebody reading a list.

The second is **weakening a content word**, which is what happens when learners over-apply this:

> I'd like ə cup əv tə.

*Tea* is the point of the sentence. Weaken that and the sentence has no beat left. Function words
go weak; the words carrying the meaning never do.

The third is the **weak form at the end**:

> Who's it fə?

The listener hears an unfinished sentence and waits for the rest. At the end, say it in full.

The fourth is **squeezing so hard the word disappears completely**:

> I'm going bank.

The weak form of *to* is short and quiet, not absent. There must be a small sound between *going*
and *bank*, and a listener notices its absence immediately.

## In real life

Weak forms are the difference between listening comprehension that works and listening
comprehension that collapses whenever somebody speaks at normal speed. They are also the main
reason recorded English on a course sounds easy and English in a corridor does not: the course
recording is full of strong forms.

Under pressure — a phone line, noise, somebody official — native speakers also use more strong
forms, deliberately, to be clear. So when you ask somebody to repeat, they often *do* give you the
strong version, which means the repeated sentence is genuinely easier to understand. That is not
imagination.

What a native listener notices about all-strong speech: care, and effort, and foreignness — never
a mistake. Nobody has ever been corrected for saying *for* in full. The cost is only that your
important words have no room to stand out.

And the useful thing to carry away: you do not need to *produce* weak forms to be understood. You
need to *recognise* them to understand. Producing them is a bonus that makes you easier to listen
to.
