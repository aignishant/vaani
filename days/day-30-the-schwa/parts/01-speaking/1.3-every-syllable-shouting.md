## One-line answer

Say a paragraph with every vowel full and every syllable equal, on purpose, and listen to English
that is perfectly clear and impossible to follow.

## The story

Somebody read a short paragraph into a recorder to check her pronunciation, and she was pleased
with it: not one sound was wrong.

> I AM GO-ING TO THE MAR-KET TO BUY SOME VE-GE-TA-BLES AND THEN I AM GO-ING TO MY SIS-TER'S HOUSE
> FOR TEA.

Then she played it to her nephew and asked what she had said. He said: *you're going somewhere and
buying something.*

Every word had arrived. Nothing had been emphasised, so nothing had stood out, so nothing had been
remembered. Twenty-three equal words went past his ear and left two.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Saying every syllable
equally strong is what careful beginners do — carefulness is the cause — which is why it is
today's deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **flat** — with no loud and quiet, all the same.
- **beat** — the stressed syllable a listener hangs the sentence on.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you heard it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

A listener does not process every sound you make. They catch the beats and rebuild the rest. Give
them twenty-three equal beats and you have given them no beats at all, which is more work than
giving them four.

Today you record the flat version on purpose. Not to punish it — to hear what your careful voice
actually sounds like from outside, because carefulness feels like clarity from the inside.

## Why Vaani needs it

Everything after this phase gets longer: a paragraph about your work, a ten-minute interview, a
role-play of twelve turns. In a long stretch of speech, flatness is what makes a listener stop
following — not mistakes. Mistakes wake a listener up. Flatness puts them to sleep.

## The rule

**Take one — the flat version, deliberately.** Choose a real paragraph of six or seven sentences
about your own day. Record it with:

- every vowel full, exactly as the spelling suggests,
- every syllable the same length and the same loudness,
- every small word — *to*, *of*, *for*, *a*, *and* — in its strong form.

Say "this take is deliberately flat" at the start, out loud.

**Take two — the normal version.** The same paragraph, using
[part 1.2](1.2-the-small-words-go-weak.md): two or three beats a sentence, everything else
squeezed, schwas in the unstressed syllables.

**The check, and it must be done by an ear, not by a feeling.** Play the flat take to one other
person, once, and ask them to tell you what they heard. Write down what they say. Then play the
normal take to the same person, once, and write that down too.

**It is also RED, differently, if the two takes sound the same to your listener.** That means the
flat take was your ordinary voice, and that is the most useful finding available today: your
normal speech is already flat, and the repair work starts there.

**Then repair.** Take three sentences from the paragraph. For each one, decide the two words that
matter, and record it three times with only those two loud:

> I'm going tə the MARKET tə buy some VEGETABLES.
> Then I'm going tə my SISTER'S for TEA.
> She's been ILL since TUESDAY.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not a whole flat paragraph, because most learners are not flat all the
time. It is **flatness that arrives with difficulty**: the sentence starts with normal rhythm, and
the moment the speaker reaches a word they are unsure of, every syllable after it goes equal and
careful.

> I went to the — the mu-ni-ci-pal of-fice — and I asked about the form.

The listener hears the speaker leave the conversation and start reading. It is a completely normal
thing to do, and it is worth being able to hear in yourself, because the repair is not to say the
hard word better. The repair is to keep the rhythm and let the hard word be imperfect.

The second real wrong version is flatness caused by **reading aloud from something you wrote**.
Written sentences do not have the shape of spoken ones, and reading your own page out is the
fastest way to produce a flat recording. That is why the reps in this course are always *say*,
never *read*.

## In real life

Nobody will tell you that your English is flat. They will ask you to repeat things, or their eyes
will move, or they will answer the part of your sentence they caught. All of that reads as *my
pronunciation is bad*, and usually the pronunciation is fine and the rhythm is missing.

Under pressure — an official, a phone, an important conversation — everybody goes flatter, because
carefulness is what we reach for when the stakes rise. This is one of the rare cases where trying
harder makes the result worse, and knowing that is most of the fix.

What a native listener notices in flat speech: they will say you speak "very clearly" and then ask
you to repeat the important part. That combination is the signature. When you hear it, the thing
to change is not the sounds. It is which two words in the sentence you made loud.
