## One-line answer

*There is* and *there are* are how English says that something exists somewhere — *there* is not a
place in these sentences, and the choice between *is* and *are* is decided by the noun that comes
after it, never by *there*.

## The story

A cousin is coming to stay in your flat for a week while you are away, and you leave her a note
about the neighbourhood. You write:

> Near the house have a good shop. Is a chemist also. Not have any bank here.

She finds the shop. She never finds the chemist, because she is not sure from your note whether
there is one or whether you meant the shop sells medicine, and she walks half an hour to a bank
that is in fact two streets away.

Every fact in your note was correct. The three sentences that carried them were missing the words
English uses to say *this exists here*.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **exist** — to be there, to be real.
- **countable noun** — a thing you can count: *one shop, two shops*.
- **uncountable noun** — a thing you cannot count one of: *water*, *milk*, *money*, *sugar*.
- **singular** — one.
- **plural** — more than one.

In many languages, "a shop exists near the house" is said with the verb *have*: *here has a shop*.
English does not do that. English has a fixed pair of words for it, and the only choice you make
is *is* or *are*.

```text
   There  is   + one thing            →  There is a chemist on the corner.
   There  are  + more than one thing  →  There are two banks near the station.
```

And **the word *there* is doing no pointing at all**. It is not the *there* of *put it over
there*. It is a small empty word that English needs at the front of the sentence, and the real
subject arrives after the verb.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every written piece left in this phase describes a place to somebody who is not in it: a note for
a guest, an email about a problem, a description of a room, the hundred-word gate note. *There is*
and *there are* are the sentences that put things into that place, and the *have* version is the
single most recognisable beginner sentence in English.

## The rule

All of the following were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Using 'there is' and 'there are'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/using-there-there-are),
which states: "We use *there is* to say that something exists or is in a place."

**One thing:**

> "There is a restaurant in the station."

**More than one thing:**

> "There are two cafés in the shopping centre."

**The short form — and the one that does not exist.** From the same page: *there's* is the short
form of *there is*, and it is what people say; **there is no short form of *there are***.

> There's a chemist on the corner.
> There are three chemists on this road. — never *there're* in writing.

**Saying something is not there:**

> "There isn't a pharmacy near the hotel."
> "There aren't any restaurants near the hotel."

**Or with *no*, which is shorter and very common:**

> "There's no milk."
> "There are no toilets in the park."

**Asking:**

> "Is there a café near here?"
> "Are there any toilets in the park?"
> "Is there any milk in the fridge?"

**Short answers:** *Yes, there is.* · *No, there isn't.* · *Yes, there are.* · *No, there aren't.*

**Uncountable nouns take *is*, always**, because you cannot count them: *there is some milk*,
*there is no sugar*, *there is water in the tank*.

**The word after the verb decides everything.** This is the whole rule in one line:

| The noun | The verb |
| --- | --- |
| a chemist | there **is** |
| two chemists | there **are** |
| some milk | there **is** |
| any shops | there **are** |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***have* instead of *there is***, which is the story:

> Near the house have a good shop.
> In my street has many shops.

A reader understands it and it is unmistakably a beginner sentence. The fix is fixed: *there is a
good shop near the house*.

The second is the **missing *there***:

> Is a chemist also.

English cannot start that sentence with *is*. Without *there* it reads as a question with the
question mark missing.

The third is ***there is* with a plural**, which is very common and very quiet:

> There is many shops here.
> There is two banks.

The eye slides over it. Fix: the noun is plural, so the verb is *are*.

The fourth is **the invented short form in writing**:

> There're three chemists.

People say something like this. Writing it looks wrong to a reader, so write *there are* in full.

The fifth is ***it is* where *there is* belongs**:

> It is a good shop near my house.

*It is* points at a shop you have already mentioned. *There is* introduces one for the first time.
Mixing them means your reader is looking for a shop you never named.

## In real life

Notes, adverts, listings and messages about places are almost entirely built from these two
sentences, and they are the sentences people read fastest. A flat listing — *there is a park at
the end of the road, there are two buses from the corner* — is exactly what a stranger needs.

Under pressure people write the *have* version, because it is the shape their first language
offers, and because *there is* feels like an empty word to somebody who is trying to be brief.

What a reader does with *here has a shop*: understands it perfectly and remembers that the writer
is not a native speaker. What a formal reader does — an office, a landlord, a form: nothing kind
or unkind. It simply marks the writing, permanently, and it is one of the cheapest things in
English to fix.

And the real-life pattern worth copying: when you list what a place has, mix the two — *there's a
chemist on the corner, and there are two buses that stop outside* — because a whole paragraph of
*there is* sentences reads as a list, and one *there isn't* sentence is often the most useful line
in a note.
