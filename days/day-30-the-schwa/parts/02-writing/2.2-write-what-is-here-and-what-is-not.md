## One-line answer

A useful note about a place tells a stranger what is there, how far it is, and — the part everyone
leaves out — what is *not* there, because the missing thing is what ruins somebody's afternoon.

## The story

Two notes were left for the same guest in the same flat.

The first:

> There is a shop. There is a park. There is a bus stop. There is a chemist. There is a market on
> Sunday.

The second:

> There's a small shop at the end of the road — it opens early and it's open on Sundays. There's a
> bus stop outside the gate, and there are two buses from it that go to the station. There isn't a
> cash machine anywhere on this road, so take money with you before you come back. The market is
> on Sunday morning, about ten minutes' walk, past the school.

Both are correct English. The first is a list of things that exist somewhere in the world. The
second one can be used.

The difference is three things: distance, a negative, and a consequence.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **distance** — how far away something is: *at the end of the road*, *two streets away*.
- **negative sentence** — a sentence that says something is not so.
- **consequence** — what the reader should do because of the fact: *so take money*.
- **grouping** — putting facts that belong together in the same sentence.

A note about a place answers three questions, and the third is the one that separates a useful
note from a true one:

| Question | The sentence that answers it |
| --- | --- |
| What is here? | There's a small shop at the end of the road. |
| How far? | It's about ten minutes' walk, past the school. |
| What is *not* here? | There isn't a cash machine on this road. |

The negative is the most valuable sentence you can write, because a reader can find things that
exist. They cannot discover, until it is too late, that something does not.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing job of the whole phase in miniature: a real reader, alone, acting on your
words with no way to ask you. The phase 2 gate note asks for something, thanks somebody and gives
a direction. Giving a direction somebody can follow, including what they will *not* find, is
exactly this part.

## The rule

**Group the facts. One sentence per thing, not per fact.**

> There's a small shop at the end of the road, and it's open on Sundays.

That is one thing — the shop — with two facts attached. Two separate sentences would make the
reader hold the shop twice.

**Attach a distance to every place.** A place with no distance is not a place yet:

| Useless | Usable |
| --- | --- |
| There's a chemist. | There's a chemist two streets away, next to the bank. |
| There's a market. | There's a market on Sunday morning, about ten minutes' walk. |

Measure in landmarks and streets, not in the time it takes you — you walk faster or slower than
your reader.

**Write at least one negative, and put the consequence after it.** The shapes were checked on
2026-09-22 at
[Using 'there is' and 'there are'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/using-there-there-are):

> "There isn't a pharmacy near the hotel."
> "There aren't any restaurants near the hotel."
> "There's no milk."

And in your note, with the consequence attached:

> There isn't a cash machine on this road, so take money with you.
> There's no hot water in the morning until seven.

**Mix the shapes.** A paragraph of nothing but *there is* reads as a list. Use *there's*, *there
are*, a negative, and one ordinary sentence with a real verb in it:

> The market is on Sunday morning.

**Order the note the way the reader will use it**: what they need first, first. Somebody arriving
in the evening needs food and the door key before they need the Sunday market.

**Punctuation this part depends on:** a comma before *so* and before *and* when a whole sentence
follows; an apostrophe in *there's* and *isn't*, and nowhere else; a full stop at the end of every
sentence, because a note written as one long sentence with commas is the hardest kind to use.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **existence list**, which is the story:

> There is a shop. There is a park. There is a bus stop.

Correct, complete, and it tells a stranger nothing they can act on. The fix is a distance on each
one and a joining of the two that belong together.

The second is the **note with no negative**. Everything in it is true and the reader still walks
to the bank that is not there. Nothing in the note is wrong; the hole is what is missing.

The third is the **time measure that belongs to you**:

> The market is five minutes away.

Five minutes for whom, with what bags, in what weather? Landmarks travel; your pace does not.

The fourth is **the wrong verb with a plural**, which hides easily in a long note:

> There is two buses from the stop outside.

The fifth is **everything in one paragraph with no order**, so the reader reads about the Sunday
market before they know where the key is.

## In real life

Real notes for real people get skimmed once, in a hurry, usually while standing up. That means
short sentences, one thing each, and the urgent thing at the top. Anything the reader needs after
dark goes first.

Under pressure — leaving in a rush, writing for somebody arriving tonight — people write what is
interesting about the place rather than what is needed: the nice park, the good restaurant, the
view. The reader needs the door, the shop, the bus and the thing that is missing.

What a careful reader does with a list-note: reads it, thanks you, and asks you three questions by
message later. What a reader with no way to ask does: guesses, and walks to a bank that closed
last year.

And the pattern worth copying, because it is what good notes everywhere look like: a short
paragraph of what is here, one sentence of what is not, and one sentence saying what to do about
that.
