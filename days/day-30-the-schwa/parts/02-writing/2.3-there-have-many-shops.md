## One-line answer

Write your note again with *have* for existence and *is* for plurals, on purpose, then read both
versions to somebody and find out that the broken one is perfectly understandable — which is
exactly why it survives for years.

## The story

Somebody had been writing English at work for two years and wrote this in a message to a supplier:

> In our office have two printers but one is not working. Also there is many boxes in the corridor.

The supplier understood it completely and sent the engineer. Nobody said anything. The same two
mistakes were still in that person's writing two years later, because nothing had ever gone wrong
because of them.

That is the whole problem with this pair of mistakes: they cost you nothing today, they mark every
sentence you write, and no reader will ever tell you.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. *Have* for existence and
*there is* with a plural are the two most persistent errors in beginner English writing, which is
why they are today's deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **fossil** — a mistake that stops being corrected and stays for years.
- **understandable** — the reader gets the meaning anyway.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

Some mistakes stop you being understood. Those get fixed fast, because the world fixes them for
you — somebody misunderstands, something goes wrong, you learn.

Other mistakes never stop anybody understanding anything. Nothing in the world pushes back. They
become **fossils**, and the only thing that removes a fossil is seeing it on purpose, next to the
correct version, in your own handwriting.

Today you make the fossils visible.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 2 ends with a written piece that will be read by somebody judging your English, and phases 3
to 6 build on the assumption that sentences like these are gone. A fossil carried past this phase
appears in every piece of writing for the next ninety days.

## The rule

**Step 1 — the GREEN note.** Take the note you wrote in
[part 2.2](2.2-write-what-is-here-and-what-is-not.md), or write a new one, correct.

**Step 2 — the RED note, deliberately.** Copy it out with exactly these three changes and nothing
else:

- every *there is* / *there are* that introduces a place becomes *have* or *has*,
- every *there are* that remains becomes *there is*,
- delete *there* from one sentence entirely.

> GREEN: There's a small shop at the end of the road. There are two buses from the stop outside.
> RED: At the end of the road have a small shop. There is two buses from the stop outside.

Write **deliberate** at the top.

**Step 3 — the reading test, and this is the check.** Give somebody the RED note and ask them to
tell you what it says. Write down their answer, exactly.

> My office have two printers. — There are two printers in my office.
> There is no any milk. — There's no milk.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not usually as consistent as the deliberate one. It is a note where
*there is* and *have* alternate in the same paragraph, because the writer knows the rule and
reaches for it only when they are paying attention:

> There's a good shop near the house. Also in the next street have a chemist, and there is two
> buses that go to the station from outside.

Sentence one is correct. Sentences two and three are the fossils, and they arrive exactly where
the writer stopped thinking about grammar and started thinking about the shop.

The second real wrong version is the one that comes from over-correcting: *there* pushed into
sentences that never needed it.

> There is my brother lives in that house.

The fix is that a sentence with a real verb — *lives* — does not need *there is* at all: *my
brother lives in that house.*

## In real life

Nobody corrects adult writing. Colleagues, landlords, suppliers and friends all read past these
sentences, because the meaning is clear and correcting somebody's English unasked is rude in most
places.

Under pressure the fossils return first, because they are the sentence shapes your hand produces
without thinking. That is what makes them fossils rather than mistakes.

What a formal reader does with a note full of them: understands it, deals with it, and forms a
quiet opinion about the writing. That opinion shows up later, in who gets asked to write the
message to the client.

And the practical consequence for you: the only reliable way to remove a fossil is the one in this
part — write it wrong on purpose, put the right one beside it, and then check for exactly those
two shapes every time you read your own writing back. Two shapes. It is a short list, and it is
worth carrying for the rest of the course.
