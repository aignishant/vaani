## One-line answer

You need three different repairs, not one — *I didn't hear you*, *you were too fast*, and *I don't
know that word* are three different problems, and asking for the wrong repair gets the same
sentence again at the same speed.

## The story

You are at a counter. The woman behind the glass says something with about nine words in it, and
you catch *form* and *Tuesday*. You say:

> Sorry?

She says it again, exactly the same, exactly as fast. You catch *form* and *Tuesday* again.

You say *sorry?* a second time. Now she is slightly louder, which was never the problem, and there
are two people behind you, and you can feel the moment when asking a third time stops being
possible.

Nothing went wrong with your English. You used the repair for *I didn't hear you* when your
problem was *that was too fast for me*, and you got exactly what you asked for.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **repair** — a sentence that fixes a break in the conversation.
- **repeat** — to say the same thing again.
- **slowly** — with more space between the words.
- **catch** — to hear and understand something as it goes past.
- **polite** — said in a way that does not annoy anybody.

There are three different reasons a sentence does not arrive, and each has its own repair.

| What went wrong | What you need | What you say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I didn't hear it | the same words again | Sorry? · Could you say that again, please? |
| It was too fast | the same words, slower | Can you repeat that more slowly, please? |
| I don't know a word in it | different words | Sorry, what does *deposit* mean? |

The second one is the sentence nobody teaches beginners and everybody needs. *Sorry?* asks for a
repeat at the same speed, which is no use if speed was the problem, and most of the time speed
*is* the problem.

## Why Vaani needs it

Everything in the rest of this course happens with real people who do not slow down for you: the
phase 2 gate role-play, the counters and offices in phases 3 and 4, and the unscripted interview
at the end. The ability to keep asking, politely, without leaving the conversation, is worth more
than any amount of vocabulary — because vocabulary you do not have can be asked for, if you know
how to ask.

## The rule

All of these were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Checking understanding](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding).

**When you did not hear:**

> "I'm sorry?"
> "I can't hear you very well."
> "Could you say that again, please?"

**When it was too fast — the one to learn first:**

> "Can you repeat that more slowly, please?"

**When you did not understand at all:**

> "I don't understand."

**When you want to check you got it right**, which is the most useful of all, because it moves the
conversation forward instead of back:

> "Do you mean the hammer?"
> "Is this it?"

And the answer you are hoping for:

> "Yes, that's right!"

**The shape of a polite repair: *sorry* + the ask + *please*.**

```text
   Sorry —   could you say that again,     please?
   Sorry —   could you speak a bit slower, please?
   Sorry —   what does that word mean,     please?
```

*Sorry* at the front is not an apology. It is the signal that a repair is coming, and without it
the same words sound like an instruction.

**Say what you *did* catch.** This is the difference between a beginner who gets stuck and one who
does not:

> Sorry — I got the form and Tuesday, but not the part in the middle.

Now the other person repeats one piece instead of nine words, and they know exactly which piece.

**And the true sentence, which is always allowed:**

> Sorry, my English isn't very quick. Could you say that again slowly?

Almost everybody slows down after this, and nobody minds it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***sorry?* on a loop**, which is the story. Three identical repairs
for a problem that is not about volume. The fix is to name what you need: *slower*, or *which
word*.

The second is **silence and a nod**. The conversation continues, you have missed a fact, and it
will surface later as a wrong date or a missing document.

The third is the **repair that sounds like an order**:

> Repeat, please. Again. Say again.

Grammatically fine, and it lands as impatience, because English puts the softness in *could you*
and *sorry*, not in the tone alone.

The fourth is **asking for the whole thing again** when you needed one word:

> Could you repeat everything, please?

You will get nine fast words again. Ask for the piece.

The fifth is **apologising for yourself instead of asking**:

> Sorry, sorry, my English is very bad, sorry.

Now the other person has to reassure you, and the missing information is still missing.

## In real life

Real conversations break constantly, for everybody, in every language — a noisy street, a phone
line, a name nobody caught. Fluent speakers repair two or three times in an ordinary conversation
and think nothing of it, which means the repair itself is completely normal; it is only the
learner who feels it as a failure.

Under pressure — a queue, an official, an expensive mistake waiting — people stop asking after the
second attempt. That is the exact point where this part earns its keep: the third ask is the one
that gets you the date, and it is available if you change what you are asking for.

What a native listener notices about somebody who asks well: that they are paying attention. A
repair with a piece named in it — *not the part in the middle* — reads as competence, not weakness.

And one real difference to know: on a phone line, say the repair early. There is no face to read,
so silence is not interpreted as thinking; it is interpreted as a bad line, and the other person
keeps talking.
