## One-line answer

Asking for help is three sentences — what you are trying to do, where you are stuck, and what you
want the other person to do — and then one more, repeating their answer back so you both know it
arrived.

## The story

Two people stood at the same counter with the same problem.

The first said:

> Sorry. This form. Problem.

The man behind the counter looked at the form, could not see what the problem was, asked three
questions, and eventually took the form and filled in the box himself — which solved today and
taught nothing.

The second said:

> Sorry, could you help me with something? I'm trying to change the address on my account. I've
> filled in this part, but I don't know what to put in this box here. What should I write?

He pointed at the box, said one line, and she wrote it down. Then she said:

> So — my old address here, and the new one at the bottom. Is that right?

And he said yes. Thirty words, and she left knowing how to do it next time.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **ask for help** — to say that you need somebody to do or explain something.
- **stuck** — not able to go on.
- **repeat back** — to say what you understood in your own words, to check it.
- **keep the turn** — stay in the conversation instead of handing your problem over.

An effective ask for help has three parts, and beginners usually say only the middle one.

| Part | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The goal | tells them what you are trying to achieve | I'm trying to change my address. |
| The stuck point | tells them exactly where you stopped | I don't know what goes in this box. |
| The ask | tells them what you want | What should I write here? |

Without the goal, helpers guess what you want and often solve a different problem. Without the
ask, they explain a system to you when you wanted one word.

Then there is the fourth sentence, which is not about politeness at all: **repeat it back**. That
is how you find out whether what arrived is what they sent.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate at the end of this phase is a role-play where you have to get something done through
another person. Everything in phases 3 and 4 — banks, offices, work — is built from asks. And the
repeat-back is the habit that stops a whole category of expensive mistakes: the wrong day, the
wrong window, the wrong document.

## The rule

**Open the door first.** One short line so the other person knows a request is coming:

> Sorry, could you help me with something?
> Excuse me — can I ask you something?

**Then the three parts, in order:**

```text
   the goal          I'm trying to  ...
   the stuck point   I've done ... , but I don't know ...
   the ask           What should I ... ?  /  Could you ... ?
```

**Make the ask a real question with a verb in it.** These are the four that cover nearly
everything:

| You want | You say |
| --- | --- |
| information | What should I write here? |
| an action | Could you check this for me, please? |
| permission | Is it OK if I bring it tomorrow? |
| a repeat | Could you say that again more slowly, please? |

**Then repeat it back, in your own words.** The check phrases were confirmed on 2026-09-22 at
[Checking understanding](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding):

> "Do you mean the hammer?"
> "Is this it?"
> "Yes, that's right!"

So yours will be:

> So the old address goes here and the new one at the bottom. Is that right?

**Write the answer down while they are still standing there.** This is not a language skill and it
saves more conversations than any language skill: numbers, dates, window numbers, names of forms.

**And if the answer was long, take it in pieces:**

> Sorry — first I go to window four, and then what?

Asking for the next piece keeps you in the conversation. Asking for the whole thing again sends
you back to the start.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **problem with no goal**, which is the story:

> This form. Problem.

The helper now has to find out what you want, and their first guess is usually to do it for you.

The second is the **ask with no verb**:

> Sorry, address change?

Understandable, and it puts all the work on the listener. Two more words fix it: *how do I change
my address?*

The third is **taking the answer and walking away without checking**. You heard *window four* and
they said *window fourteen*, and neither of you will know until you are standing in the wrong
queue.

The fourth is the **apology instead of the ask**:

> Sorry to disturb you, sorry, I know you are busy, sorry …

One *sorry* opens the door. Three of them close it, because the other person starts managing your
feelings instead of your problem.

The fifth is **asking a closed question when you need an explanation**:

> Is this right?

They say no. You are exactly where you were. *What should I write here?* gets you the answer.

## In real life

People help. That is the finding that surprises learners most: a clear, short ask with a goal in
it almost always gets a real answer, from strangers, at counters, in offices, in shops.

Under pressure — a queue behind you, somebody impatient, a language you are still learning —
learners shrink the ask, and the shorter the ask gets the more work it hands to the other person.
The short version feels polite and is actually more demanding.

What a native listener notices about a good ask: nothing at all. It passes as ordinary, which is
exactly the aim. What they notice about the repeat-back: that you are careful, and they often
correct one detail — which is the whole reason to do it.

And a real difference worth knowing: at a busy counter, the goal sentence has to come first,
because if you start with the stuck point they will answer the wrong question and call the next
person.
