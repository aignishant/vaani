## One-line answer

Record the conversation where you stop asking after the second attempt, then work out on paper
exactly what you walked away not knowing — because the cost of not asking never arrives during
the conversation.

## The story

Somebody went to collect a document. The clerk explained where to go, fast. He asked once. She
repeated it, fast. He asked again. She repeated it again, slightly slower, and he caught *second
floor* and something about a receipt.

He said *yes, thank you*, and left.

Upstairs there were two desks. He chose one. It was the wrong one, and they sent him back down,
and by then the first clerk had gone for lunch. He came back the next morning with the receipt he
had not known he needed.

There was one question standing between him and all of that, and he had it ready: *sorry — which
desk, and what do I need to bring?* He did not ask it because he had already asked twice, and a
third time felt like too much.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Stopping after the second
attempt is what nearly every learner does, because asking feels expensive and not asking feels
free, which is why it is today's deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **the cost of not asking** — what goes wrong later because a question was not asked.
- **give up point** — the attempt at which you stop asking.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw the cost.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

Not asking is a decision, and it is made with bad information: in the moment, the third question
feels like it costs something, and the trip back tomorrow costs nothing because it has not
happened yet.

Today you record both versions of the same conversation and put the costs next to each other on
one page.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate role-play has twelve turns, and three or four of them are repairs. More
seriously, everything this course is for — offices, work, a doctor, a landlord, an interview —
runs on information that arrives once, fast, from somebody who is busy. The habit of the third ask
is the difference between getting it and not.

## The rule

**Take one — the give-up version, deliberately.** Record a real situation, both voices: somebody
explains something fast, you ask twice, you do not understand, and you say *yes, thank you* and
leave. Say "this take is deliberately a give-up" at the start, out loud.

> — Take this to the second floor and bring the receipt to the desk on the right after four.
> — Sorry?
> — Second floor, receipt, desk on the right, after four.
> — Sorry, again please?
> — Second floor. Receipt. Desk on the right.
> — Yes. Thank you.

**Take two — the third-ask version.** The same conversation, except the third attempt is a
different kind of ask: name the piece you are missing, or say the true sentence about your English.

> — Sorry — I got the second floor. Which desk, and what do I need to bring?

**The check.** Three or more missing facts with a real cost beside each means the break worked —
you have made the invisible cost visible, in your own handwriting.

Fewer than three means the give-up take was too kind to itself: you understood most of it, so
record it again with a faster, longer instruction.

**And the second check, which is the honest one.** Listen to take one and write down the exact
moment you gave up, and what you felt just before it. Most people find the same thing: it was not
the English. It was the queue, or the face, or the idea that two asks are the limit.

**Then repair.** Record five third-asks, each one different in kind:

> Sorry — I got the day but not the place.
> Sorry, what does *receipt* mean?
> Could you write the number down for me, please?
> So, second floor, desk on the right. Is that right?
> Sorry — my English isn't quick. Could you say that part again slowly?

That third one, asking somebody to write it down, is the repair that learners forget exists, and
it works everywhere.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version does not sound like giving up. It sounds like politeness:

> — Yes, yes. Thank you. Very kind.

Said warmly, with a nod, to end a conversation that had not finished. The other person hears
agreement and closes the file. This is the most expensive sentence in beginner English, and it is
also the most pleasant to say, which is why it wins.

The second real wrong version is **asking somebody else afterwards** — a relative, a colleague, a
stranger outside — instead of the person who actually knows. The second-hand answer is often the
wrong one, and it arrives without the authority to fix anything.

## In real life

Busy people repeat things two, three, four times a day for everybody, not only for learners.
Almost nobody is counting your attempts. The learner is the only person in the conversation who
thinks a third question is an event.

Under pressure — the queue, the phone, an official with a screen to get back to — the give-up
comes earlier, at the second attempt or even the first. The countermeasure is not confidence. It
is having a different *kind* of third ask ready, because the reason the third feels impossible is
that you are about to ask the same thing a third time.

What a native listener does when you ask a third time, naming the piece: they give you the piece,
usually more slowly, often with something written down. What they do when you say *yes, thank
you*: they move to the next person, believing they helped you.
