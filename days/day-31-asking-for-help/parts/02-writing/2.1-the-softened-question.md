## One-line answer

In writing there is no voice and no face, so politeness has to be built out of words — a longer
question, a reason, and a date — and the direct question that sounds fine when spoken reads as an
order on a screen.

## The story

Somebody needed a price list from a colleague in another office and wrote:

> Send me the new price list today.

It was true, it was clear, and it was what she needed. The reply came back two days later, short,
with the list attached and nothing else in it. Something had cooled and she did not know what.

She had written the sentence she would have *said*, where her face and her voice would have
carried the warmth. On a screen there was no face. There was one line that told somebody what to
do and when, with no reason and no room to say no.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **request** — asking somebody to do something.
- **direct** — straight, with nothing softening it.
- **soften** — to make a request gentler with extra words.
- **deadline** — the day or time by which you need it.
- **reason** — why you need it.

Spoken English carries politeness in the voice. Written English cannot, so it uses three tools
instead, and all three are made of words:

| Tool | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A longer question | gives the reader room to answer | Do you think you could send it? |
| A reason | shows the request is not a whim | We're presenting to a client on Monday. |
| A date | removes the guessing | Could I have it by Thursday? |

The longer question is the strange one for learners, because in most languages and in spoken
English, shorter is clearer. In written English, **longer is politer** — up to a point — and the
extra words are doing real work: they turn an instruction into something that can be refused.

## Why Vaani needs it

Everything you will write from here that matters — to a landlord, an office, a teacher, a company,
a colleague — is a request. The phase 2 gate note asks for something. Getting the softening right
is the difference between a request that is answered and one that is resented or ignored, and
neither of those two outcomes ever explains itself.

## The rule

The whole shape below was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[An email to ask a colleague to do something](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/email-ask-colleague-do-something).

**The softened question, from that page:**

> "Do you think you could email it to me?"

**The softened deadline, from the same page:**

> "I would be grateful if we could have it by close of business on Thursday 6 December."

**And its tips say, in order:** clarify why you are making the request; say when you need the
response; write dates with complete clarity; soften the request with phrases like *Do you think
you could…?* or *I would be grateful if…*; and close with *Best regards* when the reader does not
know you.

**The four levels of directness**, from hardest to softest — all four are correct English, and
choosing is the skill:

```text
   Send me the list today.                          an instruction
   Please send me the list by Thursday.             direct, neutral
   Could you send me the list by Thursday?          normal, polite
   Do you think you could send me the list by
   Thursday? I'd be grateful.                       soft, for a favour
```

Use the instruction only for somebody who works for you. Use the third line for almost everything.
Use the fourth when you are asking for a favour, or when you have asked before.

**Always give the reason, and put it before the ask:**

> We're presenting to a new client on Monday and they've asked about the V1698 creams.
> Do you think you could send me the current price list?

**Always give the date, and write it so it cannot be misread:**

| Unclear | Clear |
| --- | --- |
| soon | by Thursday |
| as soon as possible | by Thursday 6 December |
| next week | before Friday afternoon |

**Close warmly, and make the closing match the reader:** *Thanks very much for your help.* for a
colleague; *Best regards* for somebody you do not know.

**Punctuation that changes the temperature:** a question mark on a softened question — without it,
*Could you send me the list.* is an instruction wearing a question's clothes. And no exclamation
marks in a request; they read as pressure.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **bare instruction**, which is the story:

> Send me the price list today.

Nothing is grammatically wrong, and to an English-speaking reader it lands as an order from
somebody with no authority to give one. The fix is one word — *could* — and one question mark.

The second is the **request with no reason**, which makes the reader decide whether you matter
enough:

> Could you send me the price list by Thursday?

Better, and still bare. A reason costs one sentence and doubles the reply rate.

The third is the **vanishing request**, which is what over-polite writers produce:

> If it is not too much trouble and if you have time, maybe it would be possible to think about
> sending the list, whenever is convenient for you.

The reader cannot find the ask, and the date has dissolved. Politeness that hides the request has
failed at being a request.

The fourth is the **hidden deadline**:

> Please send it as soon as possible.

Every reader reads this as *whenever I get to it*, because it is not a date.

The fifth is **please with an imperative**, which many writers believe is polite and English does
not:

> Please do the needful and send urgently.

A reader outside your office reads this as impatient. *Could you…?* is the fix.

## In real life

People answer requests in the order of how easy they are to answer. A request with a reason, a
date and a single clear ask goes to the top; a soft, shapeless one waits for a free moment.

Under pressure — a favour, a complaint, asking somebody senior — writers go to one extreme or the
other: too blunt because they are angry, or dissolved because they are nervous. Both of them cost
the same thing: the reader does not know exactly what is being asked or by when.

What a reader does with a bare instruction: does it, and remembers. What a formal reader does with
a dissolved request: replies *let me know what you need* and waits, and you have lost a day.

And the real-life pattern worth copying: reason, ask, date, thanks. Four lines. It works for a
landlord, a school, a shop that sent the wrong thing, and a colleague you have never met.
