## One-line answer

A written request that works has four blocks in a fixed order — why, what, when, thanks — and the
ask sits on its own line where a reader skimming on a phone cannot miss it.

## The story

The same request, written twice.

> Hi, hope you are well. I wanted to ask about something. We have a client meeting coming up and
> there are some questions about the products and the prices, and I think the old list is out of
> date, so I was wondering if maybe you could have a look when you get a chance, thanks a lot.

And:

> Hi Yuki,
>
> We're presenting to some new clients next Monday and they're asking about the V1698 creams.
>
> Do you think you could send me the new price list?
>
> I'd be grateful if I could have it by Thursday, so we've got time to prepare.
>
> Thanks very much for your help.
>
> Best regards,
> Gabriela

The first is friendly and the reader has to hunt for the ask, then guess the deadline. The second
can be answered in the time it takes to attach a file.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **block** — a group of sentences with a blank line before and after it.
- **skim** — to read quickly, looking for the important part.
- **the ask** — the sentence that says what you want.
- **attachment** — a file sent with an email.

A written request is read in this order by a busy person: the subject line, the first line, and
then their eyes go looking for a question mark. Anything not in those three places may as well not
be there.

So the request is built to match:

| Block | What it holds | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | why you are writing | one or two sentences |
| 2 | the ask, on its own line | one sentence, ends in a question mark |
| 3 | when you need it, and why that date | one sentence |
| 4 | thanks, and the sign-off | one line each |

Blocks 2 and 3 are the ones that get merged into a paragraph, and merging them is what hides the
request.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the shape of the hundred-word gate note at the end of the phase: it asks for something,
thanks somebody, and gives a direction. It is also the shape you will use for the rest of your
life in English, for landlords, schools, offices and companies — and the one place where a beginner's
writing is judged by somebody who has no reason to be generous.

## The rule

**The layout, with the blank lines doing real work:**

```text
   Subject: <what you want, in a few words>

   Dear <name>,          or     Hi <name>,
   <blank>
   <why — one or two sentences>
   <blank>
   <THE ASK — one sentence, on its own line, with a question mark>
   <blank>
   <when, and what depends on it>
   <blank>
   <thanks>
   <sign-off>
   <your name>
```

**Block 1 — why, with one concrete fact in it.** The model was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[An email to ask a colleague to do something](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/email-ask-colleague-do-something):

> "We are presenting to some new clients next Monday 10 December and they are interested in our
> V1698 face creams."

**Block 2 — the ask, alone on its line**, from the same page:

> "Do you think you could email it to me?"

**Block 3 — the date, softened**, from the same page:

> "I would be grateful if we could have it by close of business on Thursday 6 December."

And the page's tips say exactly why each block is there: say why you are making the request; say
when you need the response; write the date with complete clarity; soften the ask; and use *Best
regards* when the reader does not know you.

**The subject line carries the ask, not the topic.**

| Weak | Works |
| --- | --- |
| Question | Price list for V1698 — needed by Thursday |
| Hello | Request: copy of my rent receipt |
| Urgent!! | Repair to the kitchen tap — can somebody come Friday? |

**One request per email.** Two asks in one email get one answer. If you need two things, either
send two emails or number them:

> 1. Could you send the price list?
> 2. Could you confirm the delivery date?

Numbering is not rude. It is the kindest thing you can do to somebody who has to answer both.

**Say what happens next**, so the reader knows the shape of the whole thing:

> Once I have the list I'll put the presentation together and send you a copy.

**Punctuation that decides the temperature:** a question mark on the ask, a comma after the
greeting, no exclamation marks, and a blank line between every block — because a request inside a
wall of text is a request nobody found.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **buried ask**, which is the story: the request hidden in the
middle of a friendly paragraph, usually attached to *I was wondering if maybe*.

The second is the **subject line with no ask in it** — *Question*, *Hello*, *Regarding* — which
means your email is opened last and cannot be found again.

The third is the **two-ask email**, where the second question is answered and the first is not,
and you have to write again — which makes you the person who writes twice.

The fourth is the **wall**: seven sentences, no blank lines. On a phone screen it is grey, and
grey is skipped.

The fifth is the **date with no consequence**:

> Could you send it by Thursday?

Better than *soon*, and a reader with a busy Thursday still moves it. Add the *why*: *so we've got
time to prepare*. A date with a reason attached is a date people meet.

## In real life

Most requests are read on a phone, standing up, between two other things. Your email is competing
with everything else in that list, and the thing that wins is the one that can be finished fastest.

Under pressure — a complaint, a favour, somebody senior, a second attempt after silence — writers
add words. More context, more apology, more background. Every added word pushes the ask further
down the screen.

What a reader does with a clear request: answers it now, because it is the cheapest thing in their
list. What they do with a buried one: leave it for later, and later is where email goes to die.

And the real difference between this and the friendly email you wrote earlier in this phase: a
friendly email can afford to wander, because the reader wants to hear from you. A request cannot,
because the reader is deciding whether to spend their time.
