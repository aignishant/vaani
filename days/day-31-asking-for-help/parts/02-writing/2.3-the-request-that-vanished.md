## One-line answer

Write the same request three ways — an order, a fog, and a clear ask — give all three to real
readers, and find out that the two broken ones fail in opposite directions for the same reason:
the reader cannot tell exactly what to do.

## The story

Three versions of one request went out to three people in the same week.

> Send the receipt today.

> Hi, hope you're well. I was wondering, if it's not too much trouble and whenever you have a
> moment, whether it might be possible to maybe look for the receipt from last month, no rush at
> all, thanks so much!

> Hi Sam, I need the receipt from last month for my tax form, which is due on the 15th. Could you
> send me a copy? Thursday would be perfect if that works. Thanks very much.

The first got the receipt and a cold reply. The second got a warm reply and no receipt. The third
got the receipt on Wednesday.

Two failures, opposite in style, identical underneath: neither told the reader exactly what to do
and by when.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Swinging between an order
and a fog is what writers do when they are unsure of their standing, which is why it is today's
deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **fog** — a request with so much softening that the ask disappears.
- **order** — a request with no softening at all.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

Politeness in English writing is not a straight line from rude to polite. It is a range with a
failure at each end:

```text
   ORDER ←————————— works ——————————→ FOG
   Send it today.   Could you send    Maybe if possible
                    it by Thursday?   sometime?
   does the job,    does the job      does not do the job
   costs goodwill                     at all
```

Today you write both ends on purpose and test all three on real readers.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate note at the end of this phase asks for something in a hundred words, and it will be read
by somebody deciding whether your English works. But more than that: this is the writing that
decides whether your landlord fixes the tap, whether the office sends the form, whether the shop
takes the item back. The fog costs you those things silently, and the order costs you the next
favour.

## The rule

**Step 1 — write the three versions of one real request** that you actually need to make.

- **ORDER:** the bare imperative, no reason, no softening. *Send the receipt today.*
- **FOG:** every softener you can fit, no date, no clear ask. *I was wondering whether it might be
  possible sometime…*
- **GREEN:** the four blocks from [part 2.2](2.2-write-the-request.md) — why, the ask on its own
  line, the date with a consequence, thanks.

Label the first two **deliberate** on your copy.

**Step 2 — the reader test.** Give all three to two or three real people — not the person the
request is for — and ask each one two questions:

1. *What exactly is this email asking you to do?*
2. *By when?*

Write their answers down word for word. Do not explain anything first.

| FOG | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| GREEN | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check.** It is GREEN when your readers can state the ask and the date correctly from the
GREEN version, and cannot state one or both from the FOG version. The ORDER version usually passes
on clarity and fails on tone — write down the exact words they use for it, because those words are
what your reader would have felt and never said.

It is RED when readers get the ask right from the fog — which means your fog was too clear, so add
more softeners and remove the date — or when they get it wrong from your GREEN version, which is
the more valuable RED: something in your clear version is not clear.

**Step 3 — send the GREEN version** to the person it is actually for, and write down what came
back and how long it took, in lines and not in hours of the day.

**Step 4 — repair.** Write out five fogged sentences from your own past writing and the clear
version beside each:

> I was wondering if maybe you could look at it sometime. — Could you look at it before Friday?
> Let me know if anything. — Could you confirm by Thursday?
> It would be nice to meet sometime. — Are you free on Sunday afternoon?

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not a pure fog. It is a clear request with a fogged ending, because the
writer loses their nerve in the last line:

> Could you send me the receipt by Thursday? But no rush at all, whenever suits you, honestly it's
> fine.

The last sentence deletes the first one. The reader now reads the whole email as *whenever*, and
they are right to, because that is what it says.

The second real wrong version is the fog with an emergency inside it, which is the one that does
real damage:

> Sorry to bother you, I know you're busy, but if possible maybe someone could look at the water
> problem at some point, thanks.

There is a leak in that sentence. Nobody will come this week. When they find out later how bad it
was, they will ask why you did not say so — and you did, in the politest possible way, which is to
say you did not.

## In real life

Readers take the temperature of a request in the first line and decide their priority from the
date. Anything that undoes the date later undoes the request.

Under pressure — a complaint, a leak, a deadline, a second attempt — writers fog more, not less,
because being firm feels risky when you need something. The pattern is worth knowing about
yourself, because it is invisible from the inside: the fog feels like consideration.

What a reader does with an order: complies, and lowers you slightly in their list of people to
help. What a reader does with a fog: replies warmly, does nothing, and believes they were kind —
and they were. The silence you then hear is not a refusal. It is an email that never asked.

And the honest, useful version of the rule: if the thing genuinely is urgent, the polite thing is
to say so plainly. *The water is coming through the ceiling. Could somebody come today?* is not
rude. Fogging that sentence is what would be.
