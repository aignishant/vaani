## One-line answer

Spoken instructions work when each sentence holds exactly one action and starts with a word that
says where you are in the sequence — *first*, *then*, *next*, *finally*.

## The story

Your cousin is staying while you are at work, and she has never used your stove. You are already
at the door with your bag, and you say:

> The gas is under there you turn it and press the knob and hold it and the tea is in the green
> box on the second shelf but don't use the big pan because the handle is loose.

She says yes. You leave.

You come home to a kitchen where nothing is broken and nothing was made, because somewhere in that
sentence there were five actions, one warning and one location, and she could not tell which one
came first.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **instruction** — telling somebody how to do something.
- **step** — one action in a sequence.
- **sequence** — the order the steps happen in.
- **sequencer** — a word that says where you are: *first*, *then*, *next*, *finally*.
- **check** — a short question that makes sure the listener is still with you.

Spoken instructions are different from written ones in one important way: **the listener cannot go
back.** A reader can re-read step two. A listener gets one pass.

So spoken instructions obey three rules that written ones can relax:

1. **One action per sentence.** If your sentence contains *and*, it is probably two steps.
2. **A sequencer at the front of each one**, so the listener always knows their position.
3. **A check every few steps**, so you find out where they stopped following you.

## Why Vaani needs it

Instructions are the most common thing you will be asked for at work, in a family, in any place
where you know something somebody else does not. It is also the shape of the phase 2 gate
role-play in its second half — explaining, being asked, explaining again — and the safest kind of
long speech to practise, because the listener's understanding is testable: they either do it or
they do not.

## The rule

**The shape: sequencer, then one action.** These were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Giving instructions](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/giving-instructions):

> "First, you have to put the correct amount of coffee in here."
> "Next, you put this here and turn it until it's quite tight."
> "Then you press it down with this."
> "After that, you just press this button."

**The four sequencers, and what each is for:**

| Word | Where it goes | What it tells the listener |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | step one only | we are starting |
| Then / Next | the middle steps | one more, in order |
| After that | the middle steps | the same, with a small gap before it |
| Finally | the last step only | we are finishing |

*Finally* is the one beginners drop, and it is the most useful: it tells the listener they can
stop holding the sequence in their head.

**Two ways to say the action itself, and both are normal:**

```text
   the plain command     →  Put two spoons of tea in the pot.
   you + verb            →  You put two spoons of tea in the pot.
```

The *you* version sounds gentler and is what the model lines above use. The plain command is
shorter and is what people use when their hands are busy. Use either; do not mix them inside one
step.

**Check as you go.** From the same page, checked the same day, the listener's side sounds like
this:

> "OK, that seems clear."
> "Yes, that's right."
> "Is there anything else?"
> "That's it. Just press the button."

So your side needs the questions that invite those answers:

> Is that OK so far?
> Have you got that?
> Shall I say that again?

**Put warnings before the step, not after it.**

> Careful — the handle is loose. Don't use the big pan.

A warning after the action has already happened is not a warning.

**Say where the thing is, at the moment it is needed:**

> The tea's in the green box on the second shelf. Take two spoons.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **one-sentence instruction**, which is the story: five actions
joined with *and*. The listener keeps only the last one. The fix is full stops and sequencers.

The second is the **missing first step**, which happens because the expert forgets it exists:

> Then you press it down.

Press what down? The speaker has already turned the gas on in their head. This is the failure the
whole of part 1.3 is about.

The third is **no checks**, so the speaker discovers at the end that the listener stopped at step
two and has been nodding since.

The fourth is the **warning that arrives late**:

> … and hold the knob. Oh, and don't touch the top, it's hot.

The fifth is **mixing the two action shapes inside one sequence**:

> First, you put the water in. Then boil it. Next, you add the tea. Finally pour.

It is not wrong, and it makes the sequence feel less like a sequence, because the listener is also
tracking a change in your voice.

## In real life

Real instructions are interrupted constantly: *wait, which one?*, *sorry, how much?*. A good
instruction-giver expects it and treats each interruption as the check they did not have to ask
for.

Under pressure — you are late, the other person is nervous, something is on the stove — instructions
get compressed into exactly the run-on sentence in the story, and the warnings drift to the end.
Compression is the enemy, and the cure is the sequencer: it takes one extra syllable and it holds
the whole structure up.

What a listener does when they lose the sequence: they nod. Almost nobody stops an expert
mid-flow. That is why the checks have to come from you.

And the real-life difference worth having: when you are giving instructions to somebody who is
*doing it while you talk*, you wait after each step until the action finishes. Saying the next step
over the top of somebody's hands is the most common way an instruction fails between two people
who understood each other perfectly.
