## One-line answer

Teaching a thing you do every day means saying out loud the parts your hands have stopped noticing
— the amounts, the waiting, and the way you know when it is ready.

## The story

Somebody asked a man how he made his tea, because it was better than theirs. He said:

> You boil the water, put the tea in, and that's it.

They did that, and it tasted like nothing.

What he had not said, because his hands did it without him: he warms the pot first. He uses two
spoons and a bit more for the pot. He waits — not by a clock, but until the colour turns. He
stirs once, near the end, not at the start.

None of that is difficult English. All of it is invisible to the person who does it every morning.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **amount** — how much: two spoons, half a cup.
- **until** — up to the moment when something happens.
- **ready** — finished, done, at the right point.
- **judge** — to decide by looking, smelling or tasting.
- **assume** — to think somebody already knows something.

An instruction has four kinds of content, and beginners — and experts — give the first kind only.

| Kind | Example | Usually said? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The action | Put the tea in the pot. | yes |
| The amount | Two spoons, and one for the pot. | often not |
| The waiting | Leave it until the colour changes. | rarely |
| How you know it is right | It's ready when it's the colour of honey. | almost never |

The last two are what makes the difference between instructions somebody can follow and
instructions somebody can follow *successfully*.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the first long piece of spoken English in the course where the listener has to *act*, so
it is the first one whose success is measurable. It is also the direct preparation for the phase 2
gate, which is a role-play with a real outcome, and for the last phase, where you will be asked to
explain things you know to somebody who does not.

## The rule

**Say the amount inside the step, not after it.**

> Put two spoons of tea in the pot — and one extra for the pot itself.

**Say the waiting with *until*, and give a sign rather than a length.** Signs travel; your kettle
does not:

```text
   Leave it until   the colour turns dark.
   Wait until       it starts to steam.
   Keep stirring until the sugar disappears.
```

**Say how they will know it is ready.** One sentence, near the end, and it is the sentence people
remember:

> It's ready when it's the colour of honey, not brown.

**Use the checks from [part 1.1](1.1-one-step-one-sentence.md) every two or three steps**, and
leave a gap after each one. The gap is the check; the words are just the invitation.

**Handle the interruption without losing your place.** The listener asks something mid-sequence:

> — How much water?
> — About half the pot. So — that's the water in, and then the tea goes in.

Answer, then say where you both are. That short repeat is what stops a question from costing you
the sequence.

**End by handing it over.** The model for the close was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Giving instructions](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/giving-instructions):

> "That's it. Just press the button."
> "Is there anything else?"

So:

> That's it. Finally, pour it through the strainer. Anything you want me to go over again?

**And the test, which is the whole point of today:** somebody who has never done it follows your
instructions, exactly, without asking questions. What comes out of the pot is your grade.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **expert's summary**, which is the story:

> You boil the water, put the tea in, and that's it.

Everything in it is true. Nothing in it is usable, because the amounts, the waiting and the sign
are all inside the speaker's hands.

The second is the **length instead of the sign**:

> Leave it for a bit.

*A bit* is your kitchen, your kettle, your idea of strong tea. *Until the colour turns dark* works
in anybody's kitchen.

The third is **vocabulary you have and they do not**:

> Then you strain it.

If the listener does not have *strain*, the sequence stops there. The fix is to say it and then
show it in plain words: *strain it — pour it through the little net thing so the leaves stay
behind.*

The fourth is **answering an interruption and never coming back**:

> — How much water? — Half the pot, or a bit more if you like it weak, and my mother always used
> the small pot for two people …

Three sentences later, neither of you knows which step was next.

The fifth is **no ending**, so the listener does not know whether there is a step six. *That's it*
is a real sentence and it belongs at the end of every instruction you give.

## In real life

People remember the sign, not the steps. Ask anybody how they know the rice is done and they will
tell you something about the look of it, not a length. So when you teach, give at least one sign —
it is what your listener will carry.

Under pressure — a kitchen with something already on the heat, a colleague waiting, a job that has
to be handed over before you leave — instructions shrink to the actions and the signs go first.
Then the person doing it for the first time gets the sequence right and the result wrong, and
neither of you knows why.

What a listener does when they cannot follow: they carry on, and do something reasonable. That is
the dangerous part. They do not stop. They improvise, politely, and you find out at the end.

And the real difference between giving instructions to somebody standing next to you and somebody
on the phone: next to you, *this* and *here* do most of the work. On the phone, every *this* has to
become a name. Practise the phone version, because it is the one that shows whether the
instructions are really in your words.
