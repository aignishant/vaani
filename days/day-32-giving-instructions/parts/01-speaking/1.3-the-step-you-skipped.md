## One-line answer

Take one step out of your instructions on purpose, have a real person follow them exactly, and
watch them do something reasonable and wrong — which is what happens every time an expert forgets
that a step exists.

## The story

A woman explained to her nephew how to lock up the shop. Eleven steps, clearly said, in order. He
followed all eleven.

The alarm went off at nine that night, because step one — the one she had never said out loud in
her life — was to switch the alarm panel to *night* before touching the shutter. In her hands that
was not a step. It was just what you do before you start.

When she listened to her own recording of the instructions, she heard herself say: *so, first,
pull the shutter halfway down.* First.

He did exactly what he was told. That is the thing about a missing step: the person following you
does not notice a hole. They notice nothing at all.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Leaving out the step your
hands do automatically is what every expert does, in every language, which is why it is today's
deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **assumed step** — a step so familiar that the expert does not know it is a step.
- **follow exactly** — do only what was said, and nothing else.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you watched it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

A missing step does not produce a question. It produces a **reasonable wrong action**, because the
person following you fills the hole with the most sensible thing they can think of — and sensible
is not the same as right.

Today you make one hole on purpose and watch what fills it.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate has a real outcome, and phases 3 to 6 are full of moments where you have to hand
something over: a job, a route, a recipe, a process at work. Every handover has assumed steps in
it. The habit built today — watch somebody follow it, do not help — is the only reliable way to
find them.

## The rule

**Step 1 — the GREEN instructions.** Use your recording from
[part 1.2](1.2-teach-somebody-to-make-tea.md), or record them again: every step, in order, with
amounts and signs.

**Step 2 — the RED instructions, deliberately.** The same instructions with **exactly one step
removed**, and nothing else changed. Choose the step your hands do without thinking — the one that
happens before you think the job has started. Say "one step is deliberately missing" at the start
of the recording, so nobody who finds it later believes it.

**Step 3 — the follow test, and this is the check.** Ask a real person to do the thing using the
RED instructions. Then:

- Play or read the instructions once.
- **Do not help.** Do not nod, point, correct, or make a face. This is the hard part and it is
  where the finding comes from.
- Write down exactly what they did at the missing step.

That is the finding: **a missing step produces a confident, wrong action, not a question.**

It is RED when they stop and ask you. That means the hole was too visible — you removed a step
that announces itself, like the last one. Remove an earlier, quieter step and run it again.

Almost everybody finds at least one assumed step that was missing from the GREEN version too. That

**Step 5 — repair.** Record the instructions a final time with every assumed step in, and add one
sentence at the very start that names the state everything begins from:

> Before you start: the stove is off, the pot is empty, and the alarm is on *day*.

That sentence — the starting state — is what professional handovers open with, and it is where
assumed steps go to be caught.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not a whole missing step. It is a step said in words that assume:

> Turn it on the usual way.
> Do the normal check.
> Set it like we do in the morning.

Each of those is a hole with a lid on it, and it is worse than a missing step, because the
listener hears something that sounds like an instruction and has nothing to follow.

The second real wrong version is the **step that is in the wrong place**: it is said, but after
the step that needed it.

> Put the tea in. Then warm the pot.

Follow that exactly and you have warm leaves and cold water. Order is a kind of content.

## In real life

Handovers fail on assumed steps far more often than on hard ones. The complicated part gets
explained carefully, because everybody knows it is complicated. The switch flicked on the way in
is what burns down.

Under pressure — leaving for a train, a shift ending, somebody waiting — the starting state is the
first thing to go, and it is the thing that holds every assumed step.

What a person following your instructions does when they hit a hole: something reasonable, without
mentioning it, because they assume you know your own process and they do not want to look slow.
You will hear about it only when the result is wrong, if you hear about it at all.

And the fix that costs nothing, in any language: watch somebody do it once, in silence. One
silent watch finds more holes than an hour of explaining, and it is the same test whether you are
handing over a shop, a recipe or a job.
