## One-line answer

Written instructions use a sentence with no subject at all — the verb comes first, *turn off the
alarm* — and English marks the order with *firstly*, *then*, *next* and *finally* rather than with
the verb.

## The story

You are away for three days and your neighbour is feeding the cat. You leave a note:

> The cat food is in the cupboard. Morning and evening. The back door key is with the plant. If
> she doesn't eat you can give the wet food but not too much.

Three days later the cat is fine, the neighbour is slightly annoyed, and the wet food is all gone.

Everything in your note is true. Nothing in it is an instruction. There are four facts, one
condition and no verbs telling anybody to do anything, so the neighbour had to turn your note into
instructions herself — and she did it differently from how you would have.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **imperative** — a sentence that tells somebody to do something: *Close the door.*
- **subject** — the person or thing doing the verb: *you*, *she*, *the cat*.
- **sequencer** — a word marking the order: *firstly*, *then*, *next*, *finally*.
- **condition** — an *if* sentence: *if she doesn't eat, give the wet food*.

The imperative is the simplest sentence in English and the only one with **no subject written
down**:

| Ordinary sentence | Imperative |
| --- | --- |
| You close the door. | Close the door. |
| You don't use the big pan. | Don't use the big pan. |
| You turn off the alarm. | Turn off the alarm. |

The *you* is understood by everybody and written by nobody. That missing word is what makes an
instruction feel like an instruction on the page.

And because the verb never changes shape, the order has to be carried by something else — which is
what the sequencers are for.

## Why Vaani needs it

Instructions are the most-written kind of practical English there is: notes for a neighbour, a
handover at work, a form for somebody covering your shift, steps in a message. The phase 2 gate
note has to give a direction, and a direction written as a fact is a direction nobody follows.

## The rule

**The imperative: verb first, nothing before it.** Checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Instructions for a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/instructions-colleague),
whose tips say: "Use imperative verb forms to give orders and instructions: Turn off the alarm /
make sure to sign the logbook."

> Turn off the alarm.
> Feed her twice a day.
> Lock the back door.

**The negative imperative: *don't* + verb.**

> Don't use the big pan.
> Don't give her more than one packet.

**Never** is stronger and is for the things that matter:

> Never leave the gas on.

**The sequencers, written.** From the same page, the model text opens and closes with them:

> "Firstly, when you enter the shop, remember to turn off the alarm"
> "Finally, you need to finish everything above before 8.45 a.m. and open the shop at 9 a.m."

And the ones in between are *then*, *next* and *after that*. In writing they take a comma:

```text
   Firstly,   turn off the alarm.
   Then,      open the shutter.
   Next,      check the fridge.
   Finally,   unlock the front door.
```

**Say where things are, because the reader does not know.** The same page's tips say: "Tell
people where things are – they might not know: It's near/behind/over/under the …"

> The cat food is in the cupboard above the sink.

**Say exactly what and when.** From the same tips: "Tell people exactly what you want them to do
and when: You need to do everything above before 8.45 a.m."

> Feed her once in the morning and once in the evening.

**Conditions go with *if*, and the *if* part comes first:**

> If she doesn't eat the dry food, give her half a packet of the wet food.

**And the softener for a favour, because a neighbour is not an employee:** one line at the top
changes the whole note without changing a single instruction.

> Thanks so much for doing this. Here's what she needs:

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **note of facts**, which is the story:

> The cat food is in the cupboard. Morning and evening.

A fact is not an instruction. The reader has to guess the verb, and they will guess a reasonable
one that is not yours. The fix: *feed her twice a day — one scoop each time. The food is in the
cupboard above the sink.*

The second is ***you have to* everywhere**:

> You have to turn off the alarm. You have to open the shutter. You have to check the fridge.

Correct, and it reads as nagging by the third sentence. Mix: use the plain imperative for the
steps and *you need to* for the one that really matters.

The third is the **missing sequencer**, so four instructions arrive as a set rather than an order,
and the reader does them in the order that suits them.

The fourth is the **condition written backwards**:

> Give her the wet food if she doesn't eat the dry food, but only half a packet, if she does eat
> it don't give any.

By the end of that sentence nobody knows what to do. Put the *if* first, one condition per
sentence.

The fifth is the **polite instruction that is not an instruction at all**:

> Maybe you could feed her sometime in the morning.

That is a suggestion. If it must be done, write it as an imperative with a thank-you on top.

## In real life

Real instruction notes are read standing up, once, with the reader already holding something.
Numbered lines, one action each, are the shape that survives that. Whole paragraphs do not.

Under pressure — leaving for a train, a shift ending, an emergency — writers produce facts instead
of instructions, because facts are what is in their head. The reader then converts them into
actions, and every conversion is a chance to be wrong.

What a reader does with a note of facts: their best. What a formal reader — a colleague covering
your job, an agency, a contractor — does: the minimum that the note literally says, and anything
not written is not their fault.

And the one that matters most in a language you are learning: an imperative can feel rude to
write, so learners soften everything and end up with suggestions. In English, written imperatives
in an instruction note are completely normal and completely neutral. The politeness goes in one
line at the top, not in every step.

## Check yourself

- **Write:** `TODO(me)` Pick a real task somebody else might have to do for you — feeding an
  animal, watering plants, locking up, a job at work. Write ten instructions as plain imperatives,
  verb first. Then write two negative ones with *don't*, one with *never*, two with *if*
  conditions written the right way round, and one line saying where each of three things is. Then
  add sequencers to the whole set and one thank-you line at the top.
- **Say out loud:** Read your note as if you were the neighbour. Say which line you would have to
  guess at, and then fix it.
