## One-line answer

Written instructions are laid out so a reader can find their place after looking away — numbered
lines, one action each, the location beside the thing, and a line at the top saying what state
everything starts in.

## The story

Two handover notes for the same shift.

> Open up, check the fridge temperature and write it in the book, put the bread out, turn the
> coffee machine on and let it warm up, unlock the front, and don't forget the delivery comes at
> eight so someone needs to be at the back door, also the alarm.

And:

> Before you start: the alarm panel is in the back room, and the key for the shutter is on the hook
> by the door.
>
> 1. Turn the alarm off (code is in the envelope).
> 2. Open the shutter with the key from the hook.
> 3. Turn the coffee machine on. It needs a while to warm up, so do this early.
> 4. Check the fridge temperature and write it in the book on the shelf.
> 5. Put the bread out.
> 6. Finally, unlock the front door at nine.
>
> The delivery comes at eight. Wait at the back door.

Same shop. Same facts. The first one has to be held in your head all at once. The second one can
be put down and picked back up, which is what actually happens when somebody is working.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **layout** — how the writing sits on the page.
- **numbered list** — steps written 1, 2, 3, each on its own line.
- **starting state** — what is true before step one.
- **parallel** — every step written in the same shape.

A reader following written instructions looks away constantly — at their hands, at the thing, at
the room. Each time they look back, they have to find their place again.

So layout is not decoration here; it is the whole functional difference:

| Feature | What it does for the reader |
| --- | --- |
| Numbers | gives every step an address to come back to |
| One action per line | stops half a step being missed |
| The same shape each time | lets the eye skip to the verb |
| Location beside the thing | removes the search |
| Starting state at the top | catches the steps you would assume |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the most immediately useful writing in the whole phase: it is what you leave for a
colleague, a neighbour, a family member or the person doing your job while you are away. The phase
2 gate note has to give a direction, and directions are judged by whether they can be followed —
which is a test with a right answer.

## The rule

**Open with the starting state.** One or two lines before step one:

> Before you start: the alarm panel is in the back room, and the shutter key is on the hook by the
> door.

**Number every step, one action each.**

```text
   1. <imperative verb> <object> <where, if they need it>
   2. <imperative verb> ...
```

**Keep the steps parallel** — every one starting with a verb. Not this:

> 1. Turn the alarm off.
> 2. The shutter key is on the hook.
> 3. You should put the bread out.

but this:

> 1. Turn the alarm off.
> 2. Open the shutter with the key from the hook.
> 3. Put the bread out.

**Put the *why* beside the step when the order matters:**

> 3. Turn the coffee machine on. It needs a while to warm up, so do this early.

Without the reason, a reader who is busy will move that step, and a machine that has not warmed up
is the first complaint of the morning.

**Give the location the first time the thing appears**, from the tips checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Instructions for a colleague](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/instructions-colleague):
"Tell people where things are – they might not know: It's near/behind/over/under the …"

**Say exactly what and when**, from the same tips: "Tell people exactly what you want them to do
and when: You need to do everything above before 8.45 a.m." And the model's own closing line
carries both the sequencer and the time:

> "Finally, you need to finish everything above before 8.45 a.m. and open the shop at 9 a.m."

**Separate the timed things from the sequence.** Anything that happens at a fixed time does not
belong in a numbered list of steps, because it interrupts the order:

> The delivery comes at eight. Wait at the back door.

**Put the warnings where they will be read — beside their step, not at the bottom.**

> 5. Put the bread out. Don't use the trays from the back; they're not clean yet.

**And end with the way to reach you**, which is the line every real handover note has and every
practice note forgets:

> If anything goes wrong, call me. I'll have my phone.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **paragraph**, which is the story. Every instruction is in
there and none of them has an address, so a reader who looks away has to re-read the whole thing.

The second is the **unnumbered list**, which is better and still loses the order the moment two
steps look similar.

The third is **mixed shapes**, where some lines are instructions and some are facts. The eye
learns to expect a verb, and then hits a fact and stops.

The fourth is the **warning at the bottom**:

> P.S. Don't use the back trays.

It is read after the bread is out.

The fifth is the **step with two actions in it**:

> 4. Check the fridge and write it in the book and put the bread out.

Three actions on one line. The reader does the first and the last.

The sixth is **no starting state**, which is where assumed steps hide — and the reader finds out
by getting it wrong.

## In real life

Real handover notes get pinned to a wall and read in pieces, by somebody who is already doing the
job. They are also read by people who are nervous, new, or covering at short notice, and a list
they can follow is a kindness that will be remembered longer than the English in it.

Under pressure — leaving in a hurry — writers produce paragraphs, because paragraphs are faster to
write. The time saved comes out of the other person's morning.

What a reader does with a paragraph note: does what they remember, in their own order. What a
careful colleague does: writes their own list from it, which costs them time and introduces their
own guesses. What a formal reader does: does exactly what the words say and nothing else.

And the real-life test worth knowing: a good instruction note can be picked up by somebody halfway
down the page and still make sense. If your note only works read from the top in one go, it is a
story about the job, not instructions for it.
