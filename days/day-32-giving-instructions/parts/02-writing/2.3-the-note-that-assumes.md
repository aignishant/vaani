## One-line answer

Rewrite your instructions as facts instead of imperatives, with the words *usual*, *normal* and
*as always* in them, hand them to somebody who has never done the task, and watch a note that
looks complete produce a result that is not.

## The story

Somebody left this for the person covering her desk:

> Everything is the same as usual. The morning report is in the normal place, just do it like we
> always do and send it round. The files are where they were. Check the inbox as normal.

Four sentences. Every one of them true. Not one of them usable by somebody who has never done the
job, because every single one points at knowledge the reader does not have: *as usual*, *the
normal place*, *like we always do*, *where they were*, *as normal*.

The report went out at four in the afternoon, to the wrong list, in the wrong format. Nobody had
done anything wrong.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Writing instructions that
point at shared knowledge is what everybody does when handing over a task they know well, which is
why it is today's deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **assume** — to write as if the reader already knows something.
- **pointer word** — *usual*, *normal*, *the same*, *as always*, *like before*.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

A **pointer word** is a word that points at knowledge instead of carrying it. To the writer it
feels like information. To a reader without the knowledge, it is a hole with a lid.

| The note says | The reader knows |
| --- | --- |
| in the usual place | nothing |
| do it as always | nothing |
| the normal format | nothing |
| the same as last time | nothing |

Today you write the pointer-word version on purpose, watch what it produces, and then hunt for the
pointer words hiding in your good version.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every handover you write from now on — at work, for a neighbour, for a family member — will be
written by somebody who knows the task, for somebody who does not. That gap is where all the
damage lives, and pointer words are how the gap disguises itself.

## The rule

**Step 1 — the GREEN note.** Your numbered note from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-instructions-somebody-can-follow.md), with a starting-state line, one action
per step, and a location for everything.

**Step 2 — the RED note, deliberately.** The same task, written as facts and pointer words, with
no imperatives, no numbers and no locations:

> Everything is the same as usual. The things are in the normal place. Do it the way we always do
> it, and the rest is like last time.

Write **deliberate** at the top of your copy.

**Step 3 — the follow test.** Give the RED note to a real person who has never done this task and
ask them to do it, or — if the task cannot be done safely by them — to write down, step by step,
exactly what they would do.

**Do not help.** Do not answer questions. Write down what they produce.

| | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| What did they do first? | `TODO(me)` |
| Which pointer word stopped them? | `TODO(me)` |
| What did they invent to fill the hole? | `TODO(me)` |
| How different was the result from yours? | `TODO(me)` |

**The check.** It is GREEN when the result is visibly different from what you would have done, or
when they stop and cannot begin. If they get it right, the task was simpler than you think or the
note was not red enough — put more pointer words in and run it again.

**Step 4 — the pointer-word hunt, which is the real work.** Take your GREEN note and circle every
one of these words in it:

```text
   usual · normal · the same · as always · like before · obviously
   just · simply · of course · you know the one · the right one
```

*Just* and *simply* are on the list because they hide steps: *just send it round* is a whole
process wearing one syllable.

| The line in my GREEN note | The pointer word in it | What it should say instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

Almost every real note has two or three. Finding them in your own careful version is the point of
the day.

**Step 5 — repair.** Rewrite the note with every pointer word replaced by the fact it was pointing
at, and give it to the same person a second time. Write down what changed.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not a note made entirely of pointer words. It is a good numbered note
with two of them in it:

> 1. Turn the alarm off.
> 2. Open the shutter with the key from the hook.
> 3. Do the usual fridge check and write it up.
> 4. Put the bread out.

Steps one, two and four can be followed by anybody. Step three cannot be followed by anybody who
has not done it, and it is sitting in the middle of a list that has taught the reader to trust it.

The second real wrong version is the note that assumes a **name**:

> Give it to Reception.
> Put it in the blue folder.

Which reception, which blue folder, on whose desk. Names are pointer words too when the reader has
not met them.

## In real life

Handover notes are almost never tested before they are used, and the writer is usually away when
they are. That is the whole problem: the one person who could answer the question is the one
person who is not there.

Under pressure — packing up, last hour of a shift, a phone call waiting — pointer words multiply,
because they are quick to write and they feel complete. *Do it as usual* takes a second and covers
a page.

What a reader does with a pointer word: invents something reasonable, exactly as with a missing
step, and does not mention it. What a careful reader does: messages you, on your day off, with a
question you thought you had answered.

And the test that makes all of this cheap: give the note to one person who does not know the task,
before you need it. One reading finds every pointer word in it, and nothing else does — including
reading it yourself, twice, carefully.
