# The half nobody practises

`IN-03` · Day 32

## The one-line answer

Small talk is three moves, not one — an opener, a bridge, and an exit — and the exit is the move
everybody skips, which is why so many pleasant little conversations end up remembered as awkward
ones.

## The model

Think of small talk as falling into step with somebody for a short stretch of the same corridor.
You didn't arrange it. Neither of you is going anywhere together. You walk alongside for a bit, and
then one of you peels off. Nobody minds any of that. What people mind is the bit where neither of
you knows how to peel off, so you keep walking together long past the point where either of you had
anything left to say.

That's the whole problem, and it isn't a vocabulary problem.

**The opener** is small on purpose. Its only job is to establish that you're both willing. It
should be about the thing in front of both of you — the room, the queue, the weather doing something
mildly notable, the fact that they're clearly having a day of it. Not about them personally, because
you don't know them yet, and not about you, because you haven't been asked.

> Busy one today?

> Have you come far?

**The bridge** is where most people go wrong in a way that's hard to spot. A bridge is not a
question. If you answer their opener and then ask a question straight back, you're conducting an
interview, and it's exhausting from the other side. A bridge gives something of yours first — one
sentence, a small offering — and *then* opens the floor. Give, then ask. That's the shape.

> Same here, actually. I've been on since first thing.

Hear the difference. *Same here* is the give. The second sentence is the offering. Only after those
does a question earn its place, and half the time you don't need one, because the other person will
walk into the space you left. That's what *Oh, is that right?* is for — it asks for more without
being a question you have to answer.

**The exit** is the half nobody practises, and it decides everything. Here's the thing: a
conversation that ends well is remembered as a good conversation, almost regardless of what was in
the middle of it. A conversation that dies of natural causes — the pauses stretching, both of you
saying *yeah, no, definitely* to nothing in particular — is remembered as awkward, even if the first
part of it was genuinely nice. **The ending is what stays.**

An exit has two parts and takes one breath. A signal that you're going, and a reason that is
nobody's fault:

> Anyway, I'll let you get on.

> Right — I'd better go and find my coat.

Note what the good ones do. *I'll let you get on* hands them the credit: they're the busy one, you're
being considerate. *I'd better go and find* gives you somewhere to be, so leaving isn't a verdict on
them. Neither one is honest, exactly, and both are completely standard. Everyone knows. That's fine
— an exit line is a signal, not a statement, and it works precisely because both of you recognise it.

And the crucial bit of timing, which is the one audible rule in this whole day: **the exit lands on
top of talk, not on top of a gap.** You leave while there is still something being said. Wait for
the silence and the silence has already happened — you're now exiting an awkwardness rather than a
conversation, and it sounds like it. On the tape tonight you'll hear the difference immediately.

**Today's phrases** — three to fall into step, two to walk a little way, four to peel off cleanly.
All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Busy one today? | Opening with somebody who's working while you talk |
| Have you come far? | Opening with a stranger who has arrived somewhere |
| So how do you know everyone? | Opening in a room where you're the one who doesn't |
| Same here, actually. | Bridging by matching, before you add your own bit |
| Oh, is that right? | Asking for more without making them answer a question |
| Anyway, I'll let you get on. | The plain exit, when nothing at all is wrong |
| I won't keep you. | Exiting and handing them the credit for being busy |
| Right — I'd better go and find … | Exiting with somewhere else to be |
| It was good to meet you. | Closing with a stranger you may not see again |

## Say it now

Run each line, then run the whole thing as an arc. The arc is the point — these three moves are one
movement, and drilling them separately is how you end up with an opener you can do and an exit you
can't.

1. *Busy one today? — {{what you can see them doing}}.*
2. *Have you come far? — {{where you've come from}}.*
3. *So how do you know everyone? — {{how you know them}}.*
4. *Same here, actually. {{Your one sentence}}.*
5. *Oh, is that right? {{Nothing else — leave it there}}.*
6. *Anyway, I'll let you get on.*
7. *I won't keep you — {{what they're clearly in the middle of}}.*
8. *Right — I'd better go and find {{a person or a thing}}.*
9. *It was good to meet you. {{Their name, or nothing}}.*

Then run three complete arcs out loud with no stopping: opener, bridge, exit, one after another,
three different imagined people. After that, run lines 6 to 9 on their own, cold, ten times over,
with no run-up at all. That's the drill that matters. You will never get a warning before you need
an exit, and an exit you have to compose is an exit that arrives too late.

## The deliberate failure

Run one more arc, and this time you're not allowed to exit. No exit line, no signal, nothing. Keep
it alive until it dies on its own. Do not stop the recorder early — let it run right through the end
and into whatever comes after.

**What breaking sounds like:** the pauses get longer in a way you can measure on the tape. The
content thins out to *yeah* and *yeah, no* and *definitely*. Somebody repeats something that was
already said. And then the last thing on the recording is a sentence that doesn't finish — a trailing
*so…* with nothing after it.

Here's the uncomfortable finding. Play the whole thing back from the start. The first half is
genuinely fine. It's warm, it's easy, both people are doing well. Nothing went wrong in the
conversation — the conversation was good. What went wrong is that nobody ended it, and the ending is
the only part either person is going to remember. You've almost certainly walked away from a real
version of this thinking *I'm bad at small talk*, when what actually happened is that you were fine
for the whole thing except the last breath of it.

## When it breaks in the wild

You've done the exit. Clean line, good timing, everybody happy. And then you both turn and walk in
exactly the same direction, because it turns out you're both heading for the same door. Now you're
walking side by side with a conversation you have officially finished.

Do not restart it and do not pretend the other person isn't there. Name it. Naming it is funny, and
it buys you the rest of the walk.

**The repair:**

> Well — apparently we're going the same way.

Then you're allowed a second, much smaller exit at the door, and it doesn't need to be clever:
*Right, this is me.*
