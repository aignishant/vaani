# The verdict, not the weighing

`FN-11` · Day 33

## The one-line answer

When somebody asks you to compare two things they are asking for a verdict, and the comparison is
only the short bit of weighing you do out loud on the way to giving them one.

## The model

Picture the two things sitting in your two hands. You lift them, you feel which way the scales tip,
and then — this is the whole day — **you say which way they tipped.** The weighing is not the
answer. The weighing is the noise you make while arriving at the answer, and if you never arrive,
all the other person got was noise.

This matters more than it sounds like it should, because comparing is one of the things you'll be
asked to do most often in ordinary English. Which one should I get. Is this place better than that
one. Was the second one any good. Every single one of those questions is a request for a verdict
dressed up as a request for a comparison.

Fluent speakers land it in an arc with three parts, and it's short:

**Name the gap.** One sentence on how far apart the two things actually are. This is the part
learners skip and it's the part that makes everything after it easy, because it tells the other
person how much your verdict is worth. *There's not a lot in it* means *either is fine, I'll pick
for you but don't agonise*. It's an honest and extremely useful thing to be able to say.

> There's not a lot in it, to be honest.

**Give the one difference.** Not all of them. One. The difference that actually decides it for the
person standing in front of you. A comparison with four differences in it is a comparison the other
person has to do themselves, which is what they asked you to do.

> It's much the same, only the second one's a lot easier to get to.

**Land the verdict.** A sentence with you in it. *I'd go for* — *If it were me* — *You can't go wrong
with*. The verdict has to be a thing you'd actually pick, said as a thing you'd actually pick.

> I'd go for the second one, personally.

And then the useful extra move, which is what turns a verdict into a recommendation: **attach a
condition.** Not a hedge — a condition. There's a difference and it's audible. A hedge takes the
verdict back. A condition tells the person which of them they are.

> For what you're after, the first one. If you were doing it every week I'd say the other.

Hear what that does. It's decisive twice. It gives them a verdict and it gives them the rule you used
to get there, so they can overrule you if they know something about themselves you don't. That's the
most useful sentence shape in this entire day and it costs one extra clause.

One thing to watch, because it's where the whole arc collapses. *On the other hand* is a lovely
phrase and it is a trapdoor. Every time you say it, you go back to weighing, and the scales reset.
Once is fine — it's balance. Twice and the person listening has stopped waiting for a verdict, because
you've now demonstrated twice that a verdict isn't coming.

**Today's phrases** — three for the gap, three for the verdict, three for the recommendation. All of
these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| There's not a lot in it. | The two are genuinely close and you're saying so, honestly |
| It's much the same, only … | Two things that differ in exactly one detail |
| The only real difference is … | Cutting a long comparison down to the thing that decides it |
| I'd go for the …, personally. | Giving the verdict and owning it |
| If it were me, I'd … | Giving the verdict by standing where they're standing |
| Nine times out of ten I'd pick … | A verdict with its own exception built into it |
| You can't go wrong with … | The safe recommendation, when they can't lose either way |
| I'd give that one a miss. | Recommending against something |
| For what you're after, the … | Tailoring the verdict to the person who asked |

## Say it now

Each line twice. First time, say it as though you're thinking about it. Second time, say it as
though you decided this weeks ago. The second delivery is the one that gets believed, and it's the
same words — all that changed is that the end of the sentence went down instead of up.

1. *There's not a lot in it — {{the two things}}.*
2. *It's much the same, only {{the one difference}}.*
3. *The only real difference is {{what actually decides it}}.*
4. *I'd go for {{one of them}}, personally.*
5. *If it were me, I'd {{what you'd do}}.*
6. *Nine times out of ten I'd pick {{one}} — but {{the exception}}.*
7. *You can't go wrong with {{the safe one}}.*
8. *I'd give {{the other one}} a miss.*
9. *For what you're after, {{the verdict}}. If you were {{different}}, I'd say the other.*

Now run them in a different order: verdict first, gap second, difference last. Line 4, then line 1,
then line 3, straight through, three times, with three different pairs of things. It'll feel back to
front and slightly rude. Do it anyway — that's the order you'll need when somebody's holding two
things up in a shop and waiting, and getting comfortable with the verdict arriving first is most of
what stops you rambling.

- **Solo form:** as written, straight through. Then go back to the two you had no preference on and
  say those again with a condition attached, so the verdict has a reason standing behind it.
- **With a partner:** they ask the question and they're allowed exactly one follow-up: *so which
  one?* If they have to use it, that pair was a fail and you say it again.

## The deliberate failure

Take one pair — ideally the pair you know most about — and compare them for as long as you possibly
can without ever choosing. Every time you feel a verdict coming, say *on the other hand* and turn
around. Keep going until you run out of things to say.

**What breaking sounds like:** play it back and go looking for the sentence where you decided. It
isn't there. But that's not the finding. The finding is what it sounds like while you're listening:
**it sounds good.** It sounds balanced, considered, fair-minded, well-informed. It sounds like
somebody who knows what they're talking about, which — and this is the awkward part — you do.

That's exactly why this is your habit. The endless comparison is the version that makes you sound
most competent and it's the version that is least useful to the person who asked. They came to you
with a decision and they are leaving with the same decision, plus more information to make it with.
You've done work and given them nothing.

Then say the same pair again in three sentences, ending on a verdict. It will feel thinner and less
impressive. Ask yourself which of the two you'd rather have been given.

## When it breaks in the wild

Somebody's standing there with two things in their hands, actually deciding, right now. You give
them the comparison. It's a good comparison. And they wait, and then they say: *…so, which one?*

That question is not a request for more information. It is the sound of a verdict that didn't
arrive, and the only correct response is a very short one. Do not add a reason. Do not re-weigh.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — the second one. Get the second one.

Say it twice like that, the way you would to somebody in a hurry. The repetition isn't padding; it's
what makes it sound like a decision rather than a guess.
