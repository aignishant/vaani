## One-line answer

Answer *what do you do?* in three pieces — the name of the work, what you actually do in a day,
and one thing about it — because a job title alone gives the other person nothing to say next.

## The story

You are at a wedding, sitting next to somebody's uncle. He asks what you do. You say:

> I am working in a company.

He says *oh, nice*. There is a pause. He asks whether you have come far.

Nothing went wrong, and a door closed. *A company* could be anything, and he cannot ask a second
question because you have not given him anything to ask about.

Then, later, somebody else asks the same question and you say:

> I work in a small office near the station — we do the accounts for shops. I'm the one who deals
> with the shopkeepers when something doesn't match.

And she says: *oh, so people are always calling you about mistakes?* And now you are talking.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **role** — the part you play at work: what you are responsible for.
- **responsible for** — the thing that is your job to do.
- **field** — the kind of work: teaching, building, medicine, accounts.
- **deal with** — to handle; to be the person who takes care of something.
- **study** — to learn a subject, at a school, college or on your own.

Most learners answer this question with a **title**. Titles are the least useful part of the
answer, because a title is a word inside somebody else's organisation.

| What you give them | What they can do with it |
| --- | --- |
| I'm an assistant. | nothing |
| I work in a company. | nothing |
| I work in an office that does accounts for shops. | picture it |
| I'm the person shopkeepers call when the numbers don't match. | ask a question |

The three pieces of a working answer:

1. **The name of the work** — a field, not a title: *accounts*, *teaching*, *building work*.
2. **What you actually do** — a verb, in your own day: *I deal with*, *I check*, *I write*.
3. **One thing** — a fact, a feeling, or a person: *most of them call at the end of the month*.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the second question everybody asks, after your name. It arrives at weddings, in waiting
rooms, at the school gate, in every phase of this course, and in the final phase's ten-minute
interview, where it is almost always the opening.

## The rule

**The phrases people actually use** were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Talking about your job](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/talking-about-your-job).

**Asking:**

> "What do you do?"
> "What's your role in the company?"
> "What about you?"

**Saying what you are:**

> "I'm the head of design."
> "I'm a content producer."

**Saying what you do, which is the important half:**

> "I manage artists and graphic designers."
> "I'm responsible for writing."

**And the questions that keep it going**, from the same page:

> "Do you like your job?"
> "What's the best part of your job?"
> "Do you like the people you work with?"

**The shape to build, out loud:**

```text
   I work in   <field>   —   we <what the place does>.
   I'm the one who   <your actual job, in a verb>.
   <one thing>: when it's busy, who you deal with, what you like.
```

**If you are studying**, the same three pieces:

> I'm studying accounts at the college near the market. Most of it is practical — we do real
> company books. The exams are in March, so everything is a bit mad at the moment.

**If you are between jobs, or working at home**, the same three pieces, and they are a complete
answer:

> I'm at home with my two children at the moment. My day is mostly school runs and the house. I'm
> doing an English course in the evenings, which is why my English is improving and my sleep isn't.

Nobody needs an apology attached to any of these. The pieces are the same.

**And always hand the turn back**, from the same page:

> What about you?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **title only**, which is the story:

> I am working in a company.

Correct English, and no door for the other person. The fix is the field and one verb.

The second is the **title in somebody else's words**:

> I'm a junior process associate, level two.

That means something inside your building and nothing outside it. Say what you do instead.

The third is the **wrong tense**, which is very common:

> I am working in an office. (meaning: every day, for three years)

*I work in an office* is the shape for your life. *I'm working* is the shape for a temporary
arrangement or this week. Both exist, and saying the second when you mean the first tells people
your job is new or short.

The fourth is the **long history**:

> First I was in my uncle's shop, then I did two years of college, then I left because of my
> mother's illness, then …

The question was about now. The history is a good story for turn five, not for turn one.

The fifth is the **apology**:

> I'm only a … it's nothing important.

The listener now has to reassure you, and the conversation is about your feelings rather than your
work.

## In real life

*What do you do?* is not a question about your work. It is a request for something to talk about,
and the answer that works is the one with a hook in it — a person, a problem, a time of year,
something that happens.

Under pressure — a room of strangers, somebody with an impressive job, an interview — people
shrink the answer to a title, and the title is the piece with no hook in it. That is the exact
moment to say the verb instead: *I'm the one who deals with…*

What a native listener notices: whether there is anything to ask about. They are not judging the
job; they are looking for a second question. What they do when there is nothing: change the
subject politely, which feels like a judgement and is not.

And a real difference to know: in many places asking what somebody earns, or why they left their
last job, is normal at a first meeting. In English small talk with a stranger it is not, so the
hook you offer should be about the work itself, not the money.
