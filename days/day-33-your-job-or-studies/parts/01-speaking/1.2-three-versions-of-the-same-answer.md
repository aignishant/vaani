## One-line answer

Have three answers ready — one line, three lines, and one for somebody who does the same work —
and choose by how much the other person can use, not by how much you have to say.

## The story

A man was asked what he did three times in one evening.

The first was at the door, by somebody holding two plates. He said:

> I fix air conditioners.

Exactly right. That person had one hand free and no time.

The second was over dinner, by somebody with nothing to do for an hour. He said the same five
words, and then stopped, and the conversation had to be restarted by somebody else.

The third was by an electrician, who asked a real question about the work, and he answered it in
the same five words again, because those five words were all he had.

Three different rooms, three different amounts of room, one answer.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **version** — one of several ways to say the same thing.
- **audience** — the person listening.
- **detail** — a small specific fact.
- **jargon** — words only people inside a field understand.

The size of your answer is a decision about the other person, not about you. There are three
sizes, and having all three prepared is what fluent people actually have.

| Version | When | What is in it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One line | passing, busy, noisy, a queue | the field and a verb |
| Three lines | sitting down, a real conversation | field, what you do, a hook |
| Same-field version | they do your work, or nearly | the real detail, the problem, the specifics |

The mistake is not having the wrong version. It is having only one.

## Why Vaani needs it

The ten-minute unscripted interview at the end of this course opens on this question, and the
answer is expected to be the three-line one. The phase 2 gate role-play needs the one-line version
— a shop assistant asking what you do is making conversation, not taking a statement. And the
same-field version is the one that gets you work.

## The rule

**Version one — one line.** Field plus a verb. Nothing else:

> I fix air conditioners.
> I teach small children.
> I'm studying accounts.

**Version two — three lines.** Field, what you actually do, and a hook the other person can grab:

> I fix air conditioners, mostly in shops and offices. I'm the one they call when it stops cooling
> and nobody knows why. Summer is mad — May and June I'm on a ladder every day.

Three hooks in there: *nobody knows why*, *summer*, *a ladder every day*. Any of them is a
question.

**Version three — for somebody in the same work.** Now you can use the real words, and you should:

> Mostly split units, some old window units in the older buildings. The gas leaks are the annoying
> part — you find the leak, you fix it, and two months later it's leaking somewhere else.

**Choose by two signals, and both are visible:** whether they are sitting down, and whether they
asked a second question.

**Ask back, always.** From the page checked on 2026-09-22,
[Talking about your job](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/talking-about-your-job):

> "What about you?"
> "Do you like your job?"
> "What's the best part of your job?"

**Have an answer ready for the follow-up**, because there is almost always one:

| They ask | You need |
| --- | --- |
| Do you like it? | a real answer, not *it's OK* |
| How long have you been doing it? | a number and one sentence about the change |
| How did you get into it? | a two-sentence story, not your whole history |
| Is it busy at the moment? | something about this week |

**And the useful honest line, when your work is hard to explain in English yet:**

> It's hard to explain in English — the short version is I fix air conditioners.

That sentence buys you goodwill and time, and it is true.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **one-line answer in a sitting-down conversation**, which is
the story. Correct, and it hands the other person the job of restarting the conversation.

The second is the **three-line answer to somebody holding two plates**. They will be polite. They
also wanted to get to the table.

The third is **jargon with a stranger**:

> Mostly split units and some ducted, and the gas is R32 now.

To somebody outside the field, those are not words. The three-line version is the one for them.

The fourth is the **answer with no hook**:

> I work in an office. It's fine. It's near my house.

Every sentence closes. Nothing invites a question. The fix is one concrete fact: a person, a busy
month, a problem.

The fifth is **never asking back**, which turns a conversation into an interview with you as the
subject, and after four questions the other person stops.

## In real life

People do not remember your job. They remember one detail from it: the ladder in June, the
shopkeepers who call about mistakes, the child who would not speak for a year. Those details are
the answer's real content, and they do not require good English — they require you to choose one
before you need it.

Under pressure — an interview, a room of people you want to impress — everybody reaches for
version three, because the technical words feel like competence. To anyone outside the field they
are a wall, and the conversation ends with both people being polite.

What a native listener does with a one-line answer when they had time: asks one more question, and
if that one also closes, moves on. What they do with a good hook: ask about the hook, every time.

And the real difference between this and your written version, which you will meet on its own day:
in speech you can adjust after two seconds of their face. On paper you have to choose once.
