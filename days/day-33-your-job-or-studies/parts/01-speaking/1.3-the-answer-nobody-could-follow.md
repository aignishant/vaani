## One-line answer

Answer the question with your job title and the words from inside your workplace, play it to
somebody outside it, and ask them one thing: what would you ask me next? — the silence is the
finding.

## The story

Somebody recorded her answer to *what do you do?* and was pleased with it, because it was accurate
and she had used no wrong grammar at all:

> I'm a senior associate in the back-office team. I handle reconciliations and escalations, and I
> also support the onboarding process for new clients.

Then she played it to her neighbour, who has run a vegetable stall for thirty years, and asked him
what he would ask her next.

He thought for a while and said: *is it near here?*

That was the only question available in four lines of correct English. Everything else in it was
made of words that belong inside one building.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Answering with the words
from inside your workplace is what everybody does, in every language, which is why it is today's
deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **jargon** — words used inside one field or workplace.
- **outsider** — somebody who does not do your kind of work.
- **follow-up question** — the next question a listener asks.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you heard it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

A spoken answer can be graded by exactly one thing: **how many questions it makes available.**

Not how accurate it is. Not how impressive. A title and three jargon words can be entirely true
and offer zero.

Today you record that version on purpose, and count the questions it leaves.

## Why Vaani needs it

The last phase of this course ends in a ten-minute unscripted interview, and its first question
will be this one. Ten minutes is built out of follow-ups. An opening answer that offers nothing to
follow up makes the next nine minutes into an interrogation, for both of you.

## The rule

**Take one — the jargon version, deliberately.** Record your answer using:

- your job title, or your exact course name,
- at least three words from inside your workplace or field,
- no plain-words explanation of any of them.

Say "this take is deliberately full of inside words" at the start, out loud.

**Take two — the three-line version**, from
[part 1.2](1.2-three-versions-of-the-same-answer.md): field, what you actually do, one hook.

**The check, and it needs a real outsider.** Play take one to somebody who does not do your work —
a neighbour, a relative, anybody — and ask one question: *what would you ask me next?* Write down
what they say, or write down the silence. Then play take two and ask the same thing.

That is the break working.

**It is RED when both takes produce good questions** — which usually means your take two was not
different enough, or your listener is a generous person who will always find something. Try a
second listener, and make take one harder: title first, no verbs about your actual day.

**Then repair.** Record three pairs, jargon then plain:

> I handle reconciliations. — When two lists of numbers don't match, I find out why.
> I support onboarding. — When a new client joins, I set up their account.
> I'm in the back-office team. — I work in the part of the bank the customers never see.

Those plain versions are not simpler English for the sake of it. They are the versions that let
somebody ask a second question.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not usually pure jargon. It is one jargon word dropped into a good
answer, at the exact place where the hook should be:

> I work in a bank near the station. I do the reconciliations. It's busy at the end of the month.

Two good sentences around one closed door. A polite listener will step over the middle sentence
and ask about the end of the month, and the actual content of your job never gets discussed.

The second real wrong version is the opposite: **a plain answer with nothing specific in it at
all**, which learners produce after being told to avoid jargon:

> I work with numbers in an office. It's a normal job.

Nothing to misunderstand and nothing to ask. Plain is not the same as vague, and the hook has to
be concrete: *when two lists don't match, I'm the one who finds out why* is plain **and** specific.

## In real life

Everybody is bored of their own job description and nobody is bored of a problem. The part of your
work that makes a conversation is the part where something goes wrong and you fix it — that is as
true of teaching and cooking and building as it is of offices.

Under pressure — an interview, a person with an impressive job, a room where you feel small — the
jargon comes out, because the inside words feel like proof that you belong. To an outsider they
are the opposite: they are a wall with a person behind it.

What a native listener does with an answer full of inside words: says *oh, right* and changes the
subject. They will not ask what the words mean, because asking feels like admitting ignorance, and
that is why this failure never announces itself.

And the thing worth carrying: the ability to say your work in plain words is not a beginner's
skill. It is what senior people in every field are known for, and you can practise it in English
before you can do anything else well.
