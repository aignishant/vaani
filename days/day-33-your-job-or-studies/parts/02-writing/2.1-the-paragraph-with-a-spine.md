## One-line answer

A paragraph about your work needs one sentence that says what the whole paragraph is about — the
spine — and every other sentence in it has to be holding that sentence up.

## The story

A short profile was needed for a course group, and somebody wrote:

> I am working in a shop. I start at nine. I have two brothers. The shop sells electrical goods.
> Sometimes I do the accounts. I like cricket. In the future I want to do something better. The
> shop is near the bus station.

Eight sentences, all true, in the order they arrived in her head. A reader gets to the end holding
nothing, because there was nothing for the sentences to attach to.

Compare it with the same facts, on a spine:

> I work in an electrical goods shop near the bus station, and most of what I do is the part
> nobody sees. I open up, I keep the stock lists, and I do the accounts at the end of the week.
> The part I like best is when a customer comes in with something broken and we work out what
> happened. I'm studying accounts in the evenings, because I'd like to do that side of it
> properly.

Same person. Same shop. One sentence at the top doing the carrying.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph** — a block of sentences about one thing.
- **spine** — the sentence that says what the paragraph is about, usually the first.
- **support** — a sentence that adds to the spine.
- **relevant** — connected to the thing being talked about.
- **profile** — a short piece of writing about who you are.

A paragraph is not a group of sentences on the same topic. It is one idea, with sentences holding
it up.

```text
   SPINE    ←  the one thing this paragraph says
     │
     ├── support 1   a fact that shows it
     ├── support 2   a second fact
     ├── support 3   a detail or an example
     └── close       what it means now, or next
```

The test is simple and hard: **take any sentence out and ask whether it was holding the spine up.**
*I like cricket* holds nothing in a paragraph about a shop. It is not a bad sentence; it is in the
wrong paragraph.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the shape of every serious piece of writing left in this course: a paragraph about your
work, a paragraph about what you enjoy, the hundred-word gate note, and the longer pieces in
phases 3 to 6. A page of English with no spine in it reads as a list of facts, whatever the
grammar is like.

## The rule

**Write the spine first, before anything else.** One sentence that could stand alone:

> I work in an electrical goods shop near the bus station, and most of what I do is the part
> nobody sees.

A good spine has two halves: **what** and **what about it**. The second half is what makes it a
spine rather than a label.

| Label | Spine |
| --- | --- |
| I work in a shop. | I work in a shop, and most of my job happens before it opens. |
| I am a student. | I'm studying accounts, and it is harder than I expected. |
| I look after my children. | I'm at home with two small children, and my day runs on their timetable. |

**Then choose three supports**, and make them concrete — an action, a number, a person, a time of
year:

> I open up, I keep the stock lists, and I do the accounts at the end of the week.

**Then one detail with a person or a problem in it**, because that is what a reader remembers:

> The part I like best is when a customer comes in with something broken and we work out what
> happened.

**Then close by pointing forward or saying what it means:**

> I'm studying accounts in the evenings, because I'd like to do that side of it properly.

**What a profile paragraph does *not* need.** The tips were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[A personal profile](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/personal-profile):

> "Write about your good points, not your bad points."
> "Don't include every single thing. Choose a few points and focus on them."
> "Don't add your contact details (e.g. address, phone number). You could get unwanted messages."
> "Check your spelling and punctuation before you post."

The second tip is today's whole lesson said in other words: **choose a few points and focus on
them.**

**And the register.** The same page's model is relaxed and warm:

> "I love to travel and I'm always planning my next trip."

A profile is allowed to sound like a person. A paragraph about your work is not a form.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **spineless list**, which is the story: true sentences in
arrival order, with a cricket in the middle of the shop.

The second is the **label spine**:

> I work in a shop. I start at nine …

*I work in a shop* is a fact, not a spine, so nothing can lean on it, and the paragraph turns into
a list again by sentence three.

The third is the **spine with no supports**, which happens when a writer states something large
and then moves on:

> My work is very interesting and I learn many things. I want to improve my English. Thank you.

Nothing concrete holds it up, so the reader has to take it on trust, and nobody does.

The fourth is **two spines in one paragraph** — the job and the family, or the studies and the
holiday. Two ideas need two paragraphs with a blank line between them, and the reader will thank
you.

The fifth is the **paragraph that is one long sentence**, joined with *and* six times. Even with a
spine, a reader cannot see where the supports begin.

## In real life

Profiles, applications and introductions are skimmed by people reading twenty of them. They read
your first sentence properly and skim the rest, so a labelled first sentence loses you the only
attention you were given.

Under pressure — an application, a first day, a group of strangers — writers include everything,
because leaving things out feels like hiding. What actually happens is that the reader remembers
nothing at all, which is worse than remembering one true thing.

What a careful reader does with a spineless paragraph: reads it, and afterwards cannot tell you
what you do. What a formal reader does: looks for the facts they need, does not find them
quickly, and moves to the next person.

And the real-life test, which costs nothing: give it to somebody, take it back, and ask them to
say in one sentence what you do. Their sentence is your spine, whether you wrote one or not.
