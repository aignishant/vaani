## One-line answer

Turn the spine and its supports into a paragraph of five or six sentences by joining them with the
smallest words that show *why* one follows another — *and*, *but*, *because*, *so* — and then cut
whatever the spine is not holding.

## The story

Two versions of the same paragraph, written by the same person half an hour apart.

The first draft:

> I work in an electrical goods shop. It is near the bus station. I open the shop. I keep the
> stock lists. I do the accounts on Friday. Customers bring broken things. We find the problem. I
> am studying accounts. It is in the evening. I want to do it properly.

Ten sentences, all short, all correct, and reading it feels like walking up stairs.

The second draft:

> I work in an electrical goods shop near the bus station, and most of what I do happens before we
> open. I keep the stock lists, and on Fridays I do the accounts. The part I like best is when
> somebody brings in something broken, because we have to work out together what actually
> happened. I'm studying accounts in the evenings, so I can do that side of it properly.

Four sentences. Same facts. The difference is entirely in the joins, and in the three sentences
that were cut.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **join** — to put two sentences together into one.
- **because** — it gives the reason.
- **so** — it gives the result.
- **but** — it marks the part that does not fit.
- **cut** — to remove.

Short sentences are safe and correct, and a whole paragraph of them tells the reader nothing about
how the facts are related. The joining words are where the thinking shows.

| Join | What it tells the reader |
| --- | --- |
| and | there is more of the same |
| but | this part is different from what you expected |
| because | here is the reason |
| so | here is the result |

Two sentences joined with *because* say more than three sentences standing apart, and they are not
harder to write once you have the spine.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate note at the end of this phase has to do three jobs in a hundred words, which is only
possible with joins. And every longer piece from phase 3 onwards is judged partly on whether the
sentences know about each other.

## The rule

**Start from your lines in [part 2.1](2.1-the-paragraph-with-a-spine.md)** — the spine, three
supports, the detail, the close.

**Join in pairs, not in chains.** Two facts to a sentence is plenty:

> I keep the stock lists, and on Fridays I do the accounts.

Three or four joined with *and* becomes the run-on sentence, which is the most common fault in
this kind of writing.

**Use *because* at least once.** It is the word that turns facts into reasoning:

> The part I like best is when somebody brings in something broken, because we have to work out
> together what happened.

**Use *so* for the result**, especially in the closing sentence:

> I'm studying accounts in the evenings, so I can do that side of it properly.

**Use *but* for the honest part**, and one honest sentence makes the whole paragraph believable:

> It's a small shop, but there's more to do in it than people think.

**Punctuation, which is half of this part.** Before *and*, *but* and *so*, put a comma when a whole
sentence follows:

> I keep the stock lists, and on Fridays I do the accounts.
> It's a small shop, but it's busy.

Before *because*, no comma when it gives the reason for the main clause:

> I'm studying in the evenings because I want to do the accounts properly.

**Length: five or six sentences.** If it is longer than that, either there are two ideas in it —
split it — or there are sentences the spine is not holding, and those come out.

**The cut is the part people skip.** Read every sentence and ask one question: *is this holding up
the spine?* If not, it goes, however true it is. The profile page checked on 2026-09-22,
[A personal profile](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/personal-profile),
says the same thing in its tips:

> "Don't include every single thing. Choose a few points and focus on them."

**And check the mechanics before anybody reads it**, from the same tips:

> "Check your spelling and punctuation before you post."

That means, specifically: capital letters on *I* and on every sentence start, a full stop at the
end of each sentence, no *there is* with a plural noun, and your verbs agreeing with their
subjects.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **staircase**, which is the story: ten short correct sentences
with no joins. Nothing is wrong. Nothing shows any thinking.

The second is the **chain**:

> I work in a shop and it is near the station and I open it and I keep the stock lists and I do
> the accounts.

One sentence, five *ands*, no shape. The fix is full stops, then two joins.

The third is the **join with the wrong word**:

> I'm studying accounts in the evenings, but I want to do that side of it properly.

*But* promises a contrast that is not there, and a reader trips on it. It should be *because* or
*so*.

The fourth is the **kept sentence**: the one that is true, fond, and not holding the spine — the
cricket, the two brothers, the bus route. It survives every draft because it feels like part of
you. It is still in the wrong paragraph.

The fifth is the **unchecked mechanics**: a lower-case *i*, a missing full stop, a *there is* with
three things after it. In a paragraph about your work, read by somebody deciding something about
you, those are read as carelessness rather than as learning.

## In real life

Paragraphs like this are read fast, by people who read many of them: a course group, a work
introduction, a form, a message to a new team. The first sentence and the last sentence carry
almost all of the weight, and the middle is skimmed for anything concrete.

Under pressure — an application, a first impression — writers add sentences, and each one dilutes
the spine. The strongest version of this paragraph is nearly always shorter than the first draft.

What a reader does with a staircase paragraph: finishes it and remembers nothing. What a reader
does with a chain: gives up in the middle. What they do with a spine and four joined sentences:
they can repeat what you do to somebody else — which is the entire purpose.

And the real-life extension worth knowing: the same spine, supports and close is what an interview
answer sounds like out loud. Writing it well is how you find out what you think it is.
