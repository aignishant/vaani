## One-line answer

Write the same facts with no spine and no joins, give both versions to a reader, take them away,
and ask what you do — the sentence they say back is the only grade this kind of writing has.

## The story

Somebody submitted a paragraph for a work introduction:

> I am working in the accounts department. I am handling the invoices. I am also doing the
> reports. I have three years experience. I am working with two colleagues. I am also helping in
> the audit time. I am wanting to learn more things.

Seven sentences. Seven facts. The manager read it and introduced her to the team as *she's in
accounts*, which was the only thing that had survived.

Everything she wanted them to know — that she is the one who finds mistakes, that audit season is
hers, that she is training up — was in there. None of it was attached to anything, so none of it
stayed.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Writing a paragraph as a
list of duties is the most common shape for this piece of writing, which is why it is today's
deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **recall** — what a reader can say back afterwards.
- **duty** — a task you have to do.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

A paragraph about your work has exactly one job: **the reader can say what you do, afterwards,
without the paper.** Not while reading it — afterwards.

That is testable, cheaply, today, with one person and a piece of paper turned face down.

## Why Vaani needs it

This paragraph will be read by people who decide things: a course, an employer, a group you are
joining. The gate note at the end of this phase is graded on the same property. And the recall
test you run today is the test to use for the rest of the course on anything you write about
yourself.

## The rule

**Step 1 — the GREEN paragraph.** The one from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-the-paragraph.md): a spine, supports, joins, one detail with a person or a
problem in it.

**Step 2 — the RED paragraph, deliberately.** The same facts, changed in exactly three ways:

- no spine — start with the second most important fact,
- no joins — every sentence separate and short,
- every sentence beginning with *I*.

> I am working in an electrical goods shop. I am opening the shop. I am keeping the stock lists. I
> am doing the accounts on Friday. I am studying accounts also.

Write **deliberate** at the top of your copy.

**Step 3 — the recall test.** Take two readers if you can, one for each version.

1. Let them read it once, at their own speed.
2. Take the paper away.
3. Wait — talk about something else entirely for a while.
4. Ask one question: *what does this person do?*

Write their answer down word for word.

**The check.** It is GREEN when the reader of the good version gives back your spine, roughly in
your own terms, and the reader of the RED version gives back two or three words. If both give back
the same thing, your GREEN paragraph has no spine either — and finding that today is the best
outcome available.

**Step 4 — the *I* count.** Count how many sentences in each version start with *I*.

In the RED version it will be all of them. In the GREEN version it should be two or three at most.
This is not a rule of English grammar; it is what a reader feels as a list. Ways to start a
sentence differently, using the same facts:

> The part I like best is …
> Most of my week is …
> On Fridays …
> The shop is small, but …

**Step 5 — repair.** Rewrite the paragraph a final time with the reader's own sentence as the
spine — the one they gave you back. If their sentence was not what you wanted to be remembered
for, that is the sentence to replace, and now you know exactly what it is competing with.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is a list of duties that is also, quietly, in the wrong tense:

> I am handling the invoices. I am doing the reports. I am helping in audit time.

*I am handling* says this week. *I handle* says this is your job. A whole paragraph in the
temporary shape reads, to an English reader, like somebody describing a placement rather than a
career — and it is entirely invisible to the writer.

The second real wrong version is the **duty list with no person in it**:

> Responsible for invoices, reports, reconciliation and filing.

This is a form, not a paragraph. It is correct in a job application's skills section and empty
everywhere else, because nothing in it happens to anybody.

## In real life

People remember one thing about a stranger and forget the rest, and they remember what was
attached to something: a problem, a person, a time of year. Your paragraph does not compete with
other paragraphs; it competes with your reader's memory, which has room for one sentence.

Under pressure — an application, a new group, somebody senior reading — writers add duties,
because duties feel like evidence. Each one added pushes the memorable sentence further down.

What a reader does with a duty list: extracts the department name and forgets everything else.
What they do with a spine: repeat it to a third person, in their own words, which is the only way
a piece of writing about you ever travels.

And the honest note about the recall test: it is uncomfortable, and it is the only test that
matters here. A paragraph you liked writing and nobody can repeat has not failed at English. It
has failed at being read.
