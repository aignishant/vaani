## One-line answer

Asking the price is one short question — the hard half is catching the number that comes back, so
you say it again out loud before you agree to it.

## The story

You are at a stall. You pick up a bag and ask how much. The man says something fast that ends in a
number you half hear. It might be four hundred. It might be four fifty.

You nod. You give him five hundred. He gives you fifty back, and you are still not sure whether
you paid four fifty or whether he kept fifty by accident, and now you cannot ask, because asking
would mean admitting you agreed to a price you never heard.

You knew the words *how much*. That was never the problem. The problem started after your question
ended.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **price** — what something costs.
- **cost** — how much money you have to give for it.
- **change** — the money you get back when you pay more than the price.
- **each** — for one of them.
- **altogether** — all of it added up.

Asking has three parts, and only the first is a question:

| Part | What it is | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | the question | How much is this? |
| 2 | catching the number | *(this is where it goes wrong)* |
| 3 | saying it back | Four fifty? |

Part three costs you two syllables and removes almost every money problem you can have in a
language you are learning. It is not rude. Sellers do it too.

## Why Vaani needs it

Money is the one everyday conversation where a mistake costs something immediately. It is also a
fixed, small set of sentences — which means it is one of the few areas where a beginner can sound
completely ordinary within a day.

And the phase 2 gate is a recorded shop or café role-play. Half of its twelve turns are prices.

## The rule

**Asking.** The model was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Shopping for clothes](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/shopping-clothes):

> "How much is this?" — "It's £29."

**The four questions that cover a whole market:**

```text
   How much is this?            one thing you are holding
   How much are these?          more than one thing
   How much is it each?         when they are priced per item
   How much is that altogether? at the end, for everything
```

*Altogether* is the one that saves money, because it makes the seller add it up out loud while you
are still able to change your mind.

**Saying it back. Always.** Three ways, all short:

> Four fifty?
> Sorry — how much was that?
> So that's four fifty for both?

**Paying**, from the same page, checked the same day:

> "Can I pay by credit card?" — "Yes, you can. Of course."

And the others you will need:

> Have you got change for this?
> Can I pay in cash?
> Could I have a bag, please?

**Sizes and choices come with prices**, so keep them in the same breath. From the same page:

> "What size do you need?" — "Extra small, please."

**Numbers are the hard part, and they are worth drilling separately.** Two pairs cause almost all
money mistakes:

| Sounds like | And like | The difference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| thirteen | thirty | the stress: thir-TEEN, THIR-ty |
| fourteen | forty | four-TEEN, FOR-ty |

When it matters, do what shopkeepers do: say it a different way.

> Thirteen — one three?
> Thirty — three zero?

**And the sentence for when you have not decided:**

> I'll think about it, thanks.

That is a complete, polite ending, and it is what stops a price question from becoming a purchase.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the nod**, which is the story. You did not catch it, you nodded,
and now the conversation has moved on. The fix is two syllables, said immediately: *sorry, how
much?*

The second is **asking with no thing**:

> How much?

Fine when you are holding the thing. Useless across a stall with twenty items on it, and you will
get a price for something else.

The third is **the missing *altogether***, so you learn the total only when the money is already
in your hand.

The fourth is the **number confusion**, which is not a vocabulary problem but a stress problem:
*thirty* and *thirteen* differ by which syllable is loud, and in a noisy market that difference
does not survive.

The fifth is **agreeing to take it while asking the price**:

> How much is this? Yes, OK.

In many places, once you have said yes, the negotiation is over — which matters for the next part.

## In real life

Sellers say prices fast because they say them a hundred times a day. Nobody is trying to confuse
you. That also means they repeat them without any irritation at all, and often more clearly the
second time.

Under pressure — a queue, a crowded stall, a seller who is already serving somebody else — buyers
stop asking and start guessing, and the guess is usually rounded up in the seller's favour.

What a native listener notices when you repeat a price back: nothing. It is completely standard
behaviour, in every country, in every language. Repeating a number is not doubting the person; it
is how numbers are handled everywhere.

And the real-life habit worth copying: watch what is in their hand and what is in yours. Say the
note you are giving out loud — *five hundred* — before you hand it over. That one sentence ends
almost every change dispute before it starts.
