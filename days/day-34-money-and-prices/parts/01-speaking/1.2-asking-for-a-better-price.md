## One-line answer

Asking for a better price is a question, not a demand — you give a reason, you leave the seller a
way to say no, and you have decided before you open your mouth what you will do if they do.

## The story

Two people wanted the same lamp at the same stall, priced at eight hundred.

The first said:

> Eight hundred? Too much. Six hundred.

The seller said no, and the conversation had nowhere left to go, because a number thrown back is
not a question — it is a position, and now one of them has to lose.

The second said:

> It's lovely. Is eight hundred your best price? I'm taking two of them.

The seller said seven fifty for the pair, and they were both pleased.

The difference is not confidence, and it is not English. The second one gave a reason and asked a
question that could be answered *no* without anybody losing anything.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **discount** — money taken off the price.
- **negotiate** — to talk about a price until both sides agree.
- **reason** — why the seller should give you something.
- **walk away** — to leave without buying.
- **firm** — not changing: *the price is firm*.

Asking for a better price has four moves, and the first one is made before you speak:

1. **Decide your number.** The price you will pay, and the price you will not.
2. **Give a reason.** Two items, a mark on it, cash, you are a regular.
3. **Ask a question**, not a number.
4. **Accept the answer**, warmly, either way.

Most of the discomfort learners feel about this is about move four. Once you know you can say *no
problem, thanks anyway* in English and leave with everybody smiling, the rest is easy.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the most useful English in the whole phase for anybody who buys anything at a market, from
a landlord, or from a workshop. It is also a genuine test of the gate role-play skills — a
negotiation is twelve turns by nature.

## The rule

**Move one — the question forms**, softest first:

```text
   Is that your best price?
   Can you do anything on the price?
   Is there any discount if I take two?
   Would you take seven hundred?
```

The last one names a number and is the strongest. Use it after one of the others, not first.

**Move two — the reasons**, and one is enough:

| Reason | How it sounds |
| --- | --- |
| more than one | I'm taking two. |
| cash | If I pay cash? |
| a fault | There's a mark on this one. |
| a regular | I come here every week. |
| the last one | It's the last one, it's been out in the sun. |

**Move three — hearing the answer.** Sellers have three:

> That's the best I can do. — a no, politely. Accept it.
> I can do seven fifty. — a yes. Say yes back quickly and warmly.
> For you, seven eighty. — a half. One more small ask is allowed: *let's say seven fifty and I'll
> take it.*

**Move four — the endings, and you need both:**

> Perfect, I'll take it. Thank you.
> No problem — thanks anyway. Have a good day.

**And the walk-away, which is not a trick.** If your number is real and theirs is real, leaving is
the honest end of the conversation:

> That's a bit more than I wanted to spend. Thanks anyway.

Say it kindly and mean it. Sometimes you will be called back and sometimes not, and both outcomes
are fine.

**Where this does not apply.** In a shop with printed price labels — a supermarket, a chain store,
a chemist — prices are fixed and asking is out of place. Ask about a **discount for a fault** or
about an offer, never about the price itself:

> Is this one reduced? There's a mark on the box.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **counter-number with no question**, which is the story:

> Too much. Six hundred.

It is understood everywhere, and it turns a conversation into a contest. The question form costs
three words and keeps both of you comfortable.

The second is **saying yes before asking**:

> Yes, I'll take it. So can you make it cheaper?

Once you have agreed, the price is agreed. Ask first, agree second.

The third is **the reason that insults the thing**:

> This is not good quality. It's old. The colour is bad.

You are trying to buy it. Criticising it makes the seller defend it, and nobody gives a discount
while defending something.

The fourth is **the endless ask**, where every price gets another *and a little less?* Two asks is
the limit almost everywhere, and the third one usually costs you the first two.

The fifth is the **cold no**:

> No. Too expensive. (and walking off)

You may go back to that stall next week. A warm *thanks anyway* is cheap and it keeps the door
open.

## In real life

Where bargaining is normal, it is a social exchange as much as a financial one, and the seller's
first price already contains room for it. Where it is not normal, the same sentences used politely
will get you an offer, a reduced item or nothing at all — and the cost of asking is a friendly no.

Under pressure — a seller who is busy, a friend waiting, a crowd — buyers skip the reason and go
straight to a number, or skip the ask entirely and pay. Both cost money, and the second one costs
more.

What a native listener notices about a well-made ask: that you are easy to deal with. Sellers
remember that, and regulars get better prices without asking for them, which is the real long-term
version of this skill.

And the thing worth knowing about yourself: most people who hate bargaining hate move four, not
move three. Practise the endings first — both of them — and the asks stop being frightening.
