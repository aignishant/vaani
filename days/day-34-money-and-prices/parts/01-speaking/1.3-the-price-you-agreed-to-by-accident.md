## One-line answer

Record the version where you nod at a number you did not catch, then work out on paper what it
cost — because the nod always feels cheaper in the moment than the two syllables that would have
fixed it.

## The story

Somebody bought vegetables at a stall where the seller said the total fast, in a dialect he was
not used to, over the noise of the road. He heard something ending in *forty*.

He gave two hundred. He got back a handful of coins he did not count, put them in his pocket, and
walked away feeling that the whole thing had gone well, because nothing embarrassing had happened.

That evening he counted. He had paid a hundred and seventy for vegetables that were not worth a
hundred and seventy, and he had no idea whether the seller had cheated him or whether he had
misheard *one forty* as *forty* and handed over too much.

He will never know. That is the actual cost: not the money, the not knowing.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Nodding at a number rather
than asking again is what nearly every learner does in a busy place, which is why it is today's
deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **the nod** — agreeing without understanding.
- **check back** — saying a number again to confirm it.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw the cost.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

The nod is a trade. You are exchanging a small, certain embarrassment now for an uncertain cost
later. In the moment it feels like the cheap option, and it almost never is.

Today you record both versions and put the costs next to each other, in money, on paper.

## Why Vaani needs it

Numbers are where language failure becomes money. They are also the last thing to become
automatic, because numbers are said faster than any other part of a sentence, and they are the
part the other person has said a thousand times.

And the phase 2 gate is a shop role-play in which prices are exchanged. A role-play where
somebody nods is a role-play with a hole in it.

## The rule

**Take one — the nod version, deliberately.** Record a market conversation, both voices, where:

- the seller says the total fast and quietly,
- you do not catch it,
- you nod and pay with a large note,
- you take the change without counting it.

Say "this take is deliberately a nod" at the start, out loud.

**Take two — the checked version.** The same conversation, with three small additions:

> Sorry — how much altogether?
> A hundred and forty? One four zero?
> Here's two hundred. (said out loud as you hand it over)

And then, out loud, counting the change: *sixty, thank you.*

**The check.** It is GREEN when take one leaves at least one thing you cannot be sure of and take
two leaves none. Write the uncertain amount down as a number. Not *some money* — a number.

**And the second check, which is the honest one.** Listen to take one and write down what stopped
you asking. Everybody's is one of three: the queue behind you, the seller's face, or the feeling
that asking twice is rude. Name yours.

**Then repair.** Record five check-backs, in different shapes, until they are fast enough to use
under pressure:

> Sorry, how much altogether?
> One forty, or one fourteen?
> One four zero?
> So that's a hundred and forty for all of it?
> Here's two hundred — so sixty back?

That last shape is the strongest one in the whole part: **say the change before they count it.**
It is friendly, it is fast, and it makes both of you check at the same time.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not a silent nod. It is the nod with a word on it, which sounds like
understanding:

> — That's one forty altogether.
> — Yes, OK, fine.

Everybody in the conversation now believes the price arrived. The seller will not repeat it, and
you have no way back to it without saying *actually, I didn't understand you* — which is exactly
what you were avoiding.

The second real wrong version is **asking, hearing, and still not confirming**:

> — How much? — One forty. — OK. (handing over two hundred)

You heard *one forty*. Did you hear *one fourteen*? Those two are nearly identical in a noisy
street, and the difference is real money. The two syllables *one four zero?* end it.

## In real life

Sellers, drivers and counter staff repeat numbers all day and expect to. Nobody in that trade
finds it strange, and almost nobody finds it rude — the only person in the conversation who
thinks a second ask is embarrassing is the learner.

Under pressure — a queue, a busy stall, a driver waiting — the nod gets faster and more automatic,
and it stops feeling like a decision at all. That is why the repair has to be practised until it
is faster than the nod.

What a native speaker does in exactly the same situation, in their own language: says *sorry, how
much?* without a second thought, three or four times a week. The difference between them and you
is not the English. It is that they have never once considered that asking is a problem.

And the habit that outlasts this course: count the change in front of the person. Not
suspiciously — just out loud, cheerfully, before you put it away. Everybody does it. It is the
politest possible version of not being uncertain later.
