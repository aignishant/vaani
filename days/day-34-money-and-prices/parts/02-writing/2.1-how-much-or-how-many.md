## One-line answer

*How many* goes with things you can count and takes a plural noun; *how much* goes with things you
cannot count and with money — and the noun decides, not the size of the amount.

## The story

A small shop sends a weekly order by message. One week it said:

> How many rice do you have? And how much chairs are left in the store?

The supplier read it, worked out what was meant, and sent an answer. Nothing went wrong. But the
same two words were the wrong way round every week for a year, and once — the week the order was
read quickly by somebody new — *how many rice* came back as one bag instead of one sack.

The grammar mistake did not cause that on its own. It made a sentence that had to be interpreted,
and interpretation is where the wrong quantity gets in.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **countable noun** — a thing you can count: *one chair, two chairs*.
- **uncountable noun** — a thing you cannot count one of: *rice*, *water*, *money*, *sugar*.
- **quantity** — how much or how many of something there is.
- **container word** — a word that makes an uncountable thing countable: *a bag of rice*.

English divides nouns into two kinds, and the division is not about size or importance. It is
about whether the language lets you put a number in front of the word.

| Countable | Uncountable |
| --- | --- |
| one chair, two chairs | rice |
| a bag, three bags | water |
| a coin, five coins | money |
| a bottle, two bottles | milk |

And then the question word follows the noun automatically:

```text
   How many  + plural countable noun   →  How many chairs are there?
   How much  + uncountable noun        →  How much rice is there?
```

The one that catches everybody: **money is uncountable**, even though money is the most countable
thing in the world. *Coins* and *notes* are countable. *Money* is not.

> How much money have you got?  ✓
> How many money have you got?  ✗

## Why Vaani needs it

Written orders, stock questions, forms and messages to shops are made almost entirely of quantity
questions. Getting the pair backwards is one of the two or three most visible marks of beginner
English — and, unlike most of them, it occasionally changes what arrives.

## The rule

The whole rule was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Nouns: countable and uncountable](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/nouns-countable-uncountable),
which states: "Countable nouns can be counted, e.g. an apple, two apples, three apples, etc.
Uncountable nouns cannot be counted, e.g. air, rice, water, etc."

**The two questions, from that page:**

> "How many chairs are there?"
> "How much orange juice is there?"

**And the other question shapes it gives, which you need in the same message:**

> "Is there an email address to write to?"
> "Are there any chairs?"
> "Is there any sugar?"

**The uncountable nouns that beginners treat as countable.** This list is worth learning as a
list, because there is no rule behind it — English simply decided:

| Uncountable in English | Not *a…* and not *…s* |
| --- | --- |
| money | not *a money*, not *moneys* |
| information | not *an information*, not *informations* |
| advice | not *an advice* |
| luggage | not *a luggage* |
| furniture | not *a furniture* |
| bread, rice, sugar, milk, water | not *a bread* |

That list is taken from the same page, which gives "bread, rice, coffee, information, money,
advice, luggage, furniture" as uncountable.

**Container words make them countable**, and this is how you ask for an exact quantity:

> How many bags of rice have you got?
> How many bottles of water do you need?
> How many pieces of information are missing?

**The matching verb.** Uncountable nouns take *is*; plural countable nouns take *are*:

> How much rice is there?
> How many chairs are there?

**And the answer shapes**, so you can read the reply:

> Not many. · A few. · Six.  ← countable
> Not much. · A little. · Two kilos.  ← uncountable

Those were confirmed on 2026-09-22 at
[Quantifiers](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/quantifiers),
which lists "(not) many, each, either, (a) few, several, both, neither, fewer" for count nouns and
"(not) much, a bit of, a little" for uncount nouns, and gives "We have lots of time" and "Joe has
lots of friends" as the pair that works for both.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **swap**, which is the story:

> How much chairs are left?
> How many rice do you have?

Both are understood and both are marked. The fix is to look at the noun and ask one question: can
I put *two* in front of it?

The second is ***how many money***, which is the most frequent single instance of the swap. *Money*
is uncountable in English, whatever it is in your language.

The third is the **invented plural**:

> I need some informations. · He gave me two advices. · We have many furnitures.

The fix is a container word: *two pieces of information*, *some advice*, *three pieces of
furniture*.

The fourth is the **wrong verb**:

> How much rice are there?

The fifth is **the plural missing after *how many***:

> How many chair do you need?

*How many* always takes the plural form of the noun.

## In real life

Written quantity questions are answered by people scanning quickly, often on a phone, often while
doing something else. A question with the right shape gets a number back; a question that has to
be interpreted gets a number back that reflects somebody's interpretation.

Under pressure — a quick order, a stocktake, a message sent between other jobs — the swap comes
out, because the sentence is being typed at speed. That is precisely when the container word helps
most: *how many bags of rice* is both safer and more exact than either version of the bare
question.

What a reader does with *how many rice*: understands it, answers, and notices. What a formal
reader — a supplier, an office, a form — does: answers the question they think you asked. With
quantities, that is a real risk, and it is the only common grammar mistake in this phase where the
cost can be measured in goods.
