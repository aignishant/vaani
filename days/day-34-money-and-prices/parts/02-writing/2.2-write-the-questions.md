## One-line answer

A written quantity question is answerable when it names the exact thing, the unit it is counted
in, and the day you need the answer by — and numbered questions get numbered answers.

## The story

Two messages went to the same supplier on the same morning.

> Hi, how much stock do you have? Please tell me quickly, we need to order.

and

> Hi Ravi,
>
> We're ordering for the festival week and I need three numbers:
>
> 1. How many bags of the 5 kg rice have you got?
> 2. How much of the loose sugar is left, in kilos?
> 3. How many cartons of milk can you send on Friday?
>
> Could you let me know by tomorrow evening? I have to place the order on Thursday.
>
> Thanks,
> Nadia

The first got back: *we have stock, how much do you want?* — a question in return, and a day gone.

The second got three numbers.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **unit** — what the thing is counted in: bags, kilos, cartons, pieces.
- **specific** — naming exactly which one.
- **numbered question** — a question with 1, 2, 3 in front of it.
- **answerable** — a question somebody can answer with a fact.

A quantity question fails for three reasons, and none of them is grammar:

| Missing | What the reader has to do | What comes back |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the exact thing | guess which item | a question |
| the unit | choose one | a number you cannot use |
| the date | decide when to bother | silence, then a reminder |

*Stock* is not a thing. *The 5 kg bags of rice* is a thing. The difference between them is the
whole part.

## Why Vaani needs it

This kind of message — a short list of quantity questions — is one of the most common written
tasks in working life, and it is the one where beginner English is most often mistaken for
vagueness rather than for learning. It also feeds the gate note directly: ask for something, say
when, thank them.

## The rule

**Name the exact thing.** Add whatever makes it one thing and not a category:

| Vague | Specific |
| --- | --- |
| stock | the 5 kg bags of rice |
| the items | the blue folders |
| some material | the 12 mm plywood sheets |

**Name the unit, inside the question.**

```text
   How many  bags       of the 5 kg rice have you got?
   How much  sugar, in kilos, is left?
   How many  cartons    of milk can you send on Friday?
```

The unit is what turns an answer into something you can order with. *We have a lot of rice* is not
an answer; *nine bags* is.

**Number the questions.** Three questions in a paragraph get one answer. Three numbered questions
get three, because the reader can see the shape of the reply before they start writing it.

**Put the date at the end, with its reason** — the shape practised earlier in this phase:

> Could you let me know by tomorrow evening? I have to place the order on Thursday.

**Use the right question shapes for existence too**, checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Nouns: countable and uncountable](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/nouns-countable-uncountable):

> "Are there any chairs?"
> "Is there any sugar?"
> "How many chairs are there?"
> "How much orange juice is there?"

**And when you are offering or requesting rather than asking a quantity**, the same page notes
that *some* is used instead of *any*:

> "Do you want some chocolate?"
> "Can we have some more chairs, please?"

**Say what you will do with the answer**, in one line. It moves your message up the reader's list:

> Once I have the numbers I'll send the full order the same day.

**Punctuation that matters here:** a question mark at the end of every question, even inside a
numbered list; a capital letter starting each numbered line; and numerals for quantities — *5 kg*,
*nine bags* — because a number written as a numeral is found by a reader skimming and a number
written as a word is not.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **category question**, which is the story:

> How much stock do you have?

It cannot be answered. Every reply is a question back, and the day is gone.

The second is the **paragraph of questions**:

> How many bags of rice have you got and how much sugar is left and can you send milk on Friday?

The reader answers the last one. This is the same failure as the four-question email earlier in
this phase, and numbering is the fix.

The third is the **missing unit**:

> How much rice have you got?

Answered honestly with *a lot*, or *two hundred*, and you still do not know whether that is kilos,
bags or rupees.

The fourth is **urgency instead of a date**:

> Please tell me quickly, it's urgent.

Every reader has their own idea of quickly, and none of them is yours.

The fifth is the **swapped question word** from
[part 2.1](2.1-how-much-or-how-many.md) — *how much bags*, *how many sugar* — which in a list of
quantities is the one place it genuinely risks the wrong amount arriving.

## In real life

People answer quantity questions from a screen or a shelf, in a hurry, and they answer exactly
what they can see. That means your question has to match the way the thing is actually stored —
bags, cartons, boxes — or they have to do a conversion, and conversions are where errors enter.

Under pressure — a festival week, a deadline, a shortage — messages get shorter and vaguer at
exactly the moment precision matters most.

What a reader does with a vague quantity question: replies with a question, and both of you lose
a day. What a supplier does with a numbered list: answers it in the same shape, because you have
made their reply easy to write.

And a real-life habit worth copying: keep the unit in your own records the same as the unit in
your questions. Half of all quantity confusion is somebody counting in kilos and somebody else
counting in bags, with both of them being right.
