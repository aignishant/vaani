## One-line answer

Write the quantity questions with every *how much* and *how many* swapped and every uncountable
noun made plural, then give the list to somebody and watch them answer it correctly — and notice
that one of the answers is a guess.

## The story

An order went out with two swapped questions in it:

> How much bags of rice have you got? How many sugar is left?

The supplier answered: *rice — nine. sugar — 12.*

Nine what, and twelve what. He had read *how much bags* as a question about bags and answered in
bags; he had read *how many sugar* as a question about sugar and answered in kilos, because that
is how he stores it. Both answers were reasonable. One of them was wrong for the order, and
nobody found out until the delivery.

The mistake did not stop the message being understood. It made the units disappear, and the units
were the only thing the message was for.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Swapping *how much* and *how
many* is one of the most persistent beginner errors in written English, which is why it is today's
deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **guess** — an answer the reader worked out rather than read.
- **unit** — what the thing is counted in.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

This failure is not about being misunderstood. It is about being *interpreted*: the reader fills
in the missing structure from what they know, and their filling is invisible to both of you.

Today you find out how much interpreting your questions actually require, by asking your reader
one extra question at the end: *which of these did you have to work out?*

## Why Vaani needs it

Quantities are the one place in this phase where an error can be measured in goods and money. And
the *how much* / *how many* swap is a fossil: nothing in the world pushes back on it, so it
survives until somebody makes it visible on purpose.

## The rule

**Step 1 — the GREEN list.** Your message from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-the-questions.md): three numbered questions, each naming the exact thing and
its unit, with the right question word and the matching verb.

**Step 2 — the RED list, deliberately.** The same three questions with exactly these changes:

- swap every *how much* and *how many*,
- take out every unit,
- make one uncountable noun plural: *informations*, *sugars*, *moneys*.

> How much bags have you got? How many sugar is left? How much milks can you send?

Write **deliberate** at the top of your copy.

**Step 3 — the answer test.** Give the RED list to a real person who knows the place you are
asking about — or, if nobody does, to anybody at all, with the things in front of them. Ask them
to answer all three in writing.

Then ask one more question, and this is the check:

> Which of these did you have to work out, rather than just read?

**The check.** It is GREEN when they answer all three — they will — and at least one answer turns
out to be in a unit you did not intend, or they say they had to decide what you meant. That is the
finding: **the questions worked and the answers are unreliable.**

It is RED when every answer matches exactly what you wanted. That usually means your things only
come in one unit, so pick an item stored two ways — loose and in bags, kilos and packets — and run
it again.

**Step 4 — the fossil pair.** Write these two lines at the top of a page and keep them:

```text
   How many  +  a plural noun you can count    →  How many bags?
   How much  +  a noun with no plural          →  How much rice?  How much money?
```

Then write ten of your own from real things around you, five of each, and check every one by
asking whether *two* can stand in front of the noun.

**Step 5 — repair.** Rewrite the three questions with the question word right, the unit back in,
and the uncountable nouns singular, and send that version to the person it was really for.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is a list with one swap in it, in the middle of two correct questions:

> 1. How many bags of rice have you got?
> 2. How much cartons of milk can you send?
> 3. How much sugar is left, in kilos?

Questions one and three are right, and question two has taught the reader nothing except that they
should read carefully. Most readers do not; they answer in the unit they store it in.

The second real wrong version is ***how many money***, and it is worth naming on its own because
it is the single most common instance in the world:

> How many money do I have to send?

Understood everywhere, wrong everywhere, and it appears in payment messages, which is the worst
possible place to look like you are guessing.

## In real life

Suppliers, colleagues and shopkeepers answer questions in the unit that is easiest for them, and
they are not being careless: your question is the only instruction they have. A missing unit is an
invitation for them to choose one.

Under pressure — a rush order, a short message, a phone typed with one hand — the swap and the
missing unit arrive together, because both are dropped by the same hurry.

What a reader does with a swapped question: answers it, and thinks nothing more about it. What
happens next is a delivery that is technically correct and practically wrong, and no conversation
anywhere in which either of you could have caught it.

And the cheap protection, which works even if the grammar slips: **always name the unit.** *How
much bags of rice* is still wrong English and it is not ambiguous. The grammar is worth fixing for
its own sake; the unit is what protects the order.
