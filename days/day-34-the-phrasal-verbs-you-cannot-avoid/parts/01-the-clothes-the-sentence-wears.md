# The clothes the sentence wears

`FN-12` · Day 34

## The one-line answer

A handful of two-part verbs sit in the middle of ordinary English so often that routing around them
is not neutral — the single-word alternative is grammatically perfect and arrives dressed for the
wrong occasion.

## The model

You know how a suit is not better than a jumper. It's a suit. There are rooms where it's exactly
right and rooms where wearing it makes everybody else feel like they've turned up wrong. Words are
the same, and this is the day where that stops being an abstract idea and starts being a thing you
can hear on a recording of your own voice.

Somewhere in your English education, somebody probably suggested that *postpone* is a better word
than *put off*. It isn't a better word. It's a different set of clothes. In a written notice,
*postpone* is right. Standing in a kitchen telling a friend why you haven't booked the thing yet,
*postpone* is a dinner jacket at the corner shop.

Here's the harder half, and it's the reason this gets a day rather than a footnote. **The two halves
are one word.** *Put off* does not mean *put*. *Turn up* does not mean *turn*. The particle isn't
decoration you can drop for something more elegant — it's carrying the meaning, and there often isn't a
single word that means the same thing. *Something's come up* has no one-word version at all.
Not a formal one, not an informal one. If you avoid it, you don't say it a posher way; you say a
longer, vaguer sentence and the other person waits for the actual point.

So: these are not vocabulary. They're chunks. You learn them the way you learned every other chunk
in this course — inside a whole sentence, said out loud, until the sentence arrives in one piece.
A phrasal verb learned as a definition is a phrasal verb you will recognise and never say.

### Two things you can hear

**The stress goes on the second half.** Say *he never turned UP*, not *he never TURNED up*. This is
audible and it's the single most common giveaway. Put the stress on the verb and the particle
shrinks to nothing, and the listener hears a verb they weren't expecting followed by a small noise.
Put it on the particle and the whole thing lands as one word, which is what it is.

> Something's come UP.
> I keep putting it OFF.
> We've run OUT of milk.

**Where the little word goes.** When the object is *it* or *them*, it goes in the middle. Always.
*Pick it up*, never *pick up it*. *Sort it out*, never *sort out it*. This one isn't a style choice
and it isn't optional — the wrong order stops the sentence dead, and you'll hear that tonight in the
deliberate failure. With a full noun you get a choice: *pick up the keys* and *pick the keys up* are
both fine. With *it*, there's no choice at all.

**Today's phrases** — nine whole sentences, not nine verbs. Say each one as a single unit. All of
these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Something's come up. | Cancelling without handing over the details |
| I keep putting it off. | Admitting you've been delaying something on purpose |
| I'll sort it out. | Taking a problem off somebody else |
| We've run out of … | The thing is gone, and you're the one reporting it |
| He never turned up. | Somebody didn't arrive, and you're still slightly annoyed |
| I'll pick it up on the way. | Collecting something in passing, as a favour |
| Who's looking after …? | Asking who's responsible for a person, an animal or a job |
| It didn't work out. | A plan or a relationship ended, and you're not going into it |
| Can you drop me off at …? | Asking for a lift as far as a point on somebody's route |

## Say it now

Each line three times: once with the stress deliberately in the wrong place, on the first half; once
with it on the second half, where it belongs; once at speed. You want the wrong version on your ear
once so you can recognise it later, and then buried.

1. *Something's come up — {{what you're cancelling}}.*
2. *I keep putting it off. {{Why you keep putting it off}}.*
3. *Don't worry about {{the problem}} — I'll sort it out.*
4. *We've run out of {{the thing}} again.*
5. *He never turned up. {{What you did instead}}.*
6. *I'll pick it up on the way — {{where you're passing}}.*
7. *Who's looking after {{the person, animal or job}}?*
8. *It didn't work out. {{One sentence, no more}}.*
9. *Can you drop me off at {{somewhere on their route}}?*

Then run all nine again with the sentences joined into pairs, no breath between them — one, then
straight into four; five, then straight into eight. Joining them removes the little setup pause your
mouth has been using to assemble the two halves, and that pause is the thing standing between you
and saying these at conversational speed.

## The deliberate failure

Two breaks, both short.

**First: put the sentence in a suit.** Take five of today's lines and say them with the single-word
verb instead. *Something has arisen. I keep postponing it. I will resolve it. He never arrived. It
was not successful.* Say them at normal speed, warmly, to somebody imaginary who is standing in a
kitchen.

**Second: put the little word in the wrong place.** *Pick up it. Sort out it. Drop off me at the
station.* Say them at speed, trying to sound natural.

**What breaking sounds like:** the first set is flawless. Not one error in any of them — you could
put every sentence in a formal letter and nobody would change a word. And they land like an
announcement. There's a distance in them that you can hear straight away on playback, and here's the
uncomfortable bit: this is the version many of us were taught was the better English. It's the one
that gets praised in a classroom. It is also the one that makes people slow down and start speaking
carefully back at you, because you've signalled a register and they're matching it. You get a more
formal conversation than the one you wanted, and you get it every time, and nobody ever tells you
why.

The second set breaks differently and much more loudly. *Pick up it* doesn't sound formal, it sounds
broken — your own mouth will stumble on it before the listener does. Notice that contrast on the
tape. One kind of wrong is invisible and costs you the room. The other is obvious and costs you
nothing, because you'd never actually make it twice.

## When it breaks in the wild

Somebody says: *can you drop it off on your way?* And you hear every word, and none of it means
anything, because the word you learned for that was *deliver* and nothing in the sentence matches.
You get a blank beat, and it's just long enough for them to notice it.

Don't ask them to repeat the whole sentence — you heard it fine. Ask about the part that carried the
meaning, and put it back in your mouth while you're at it. Saying the phrasal verb yourself, inside
a question, is how it goes in.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — drop it off where?
