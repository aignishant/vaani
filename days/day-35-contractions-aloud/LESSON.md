## §1 Where we are

You say *I am not sure* and the other person says *I'm not sure*, and the difference is not
politeness — it is beats. The speaking folder is about the four contractions you will say most
often, about putting them in the quiet part of a sentence so another word can be loud, and about
the small sound at the end of a negative that turns a sentence into its opposite when it goes
missing.

A sign goes up in a window with four apostrophes in the wrong places. The writing folder is about
what the mark actually stands for, about choosing the temperature of a piece of writing once and
keeping it, and about the error that everybody understands, nobody mentions, and every reader
sees.

Choose either folder first. Each stands on its own. Follow the parts in that folder's order.

## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** In [part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-sentence-that-flipped.md), have a real
person write down what they hear from both takes without knowing the sentences. GREEN is when at
least one negative in the careless take arrives as its opposite or as a question mark on their
page, and none does in the protected take. If nothing flips, you were still being careful — record
the careless take while walking or with noise, and run it again.

**Writing:** In [part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-open-on-sundays.md), put the deliberately wrong
sign where people really read it and ask two people what it says and what they would change. GREEN
is when both understand it completely and neither mentions the apostrophes unprompted — that is
the shape of this failure. Write down exactly which line anybody does notice.

Only record what you actually heard or saw. A deliberate exercise is labelled deliberate.

## §7 Traps

- Full forms everywhere, which sounds careful to you and distant to a listener.
- A two-syllable contraction: *I-yam*, *it-is*, *we-uv*.
- *Yes, I'm.* — a contraction at the end of a sentence, where English wants the full form.
- The beat landing on the contraction, so a plain sentence becomes a correction.
- The lost *t* in a negative, which turns the sentence into its opposite.
- The over-correction after that: *I DO NOT NEED IT TODAY*, which arrives as anger.
- *dont*, *cant*, *were* — the mark missing where letters really are.
- *do'nt*, *is'nt* — the mark between the words instead of where the letters went.
- *Its raining* and *the dog chased it's tail* — the pair the *it is* test settles in a second.
- *Open on Sunday's*, *fresh apple's* — an apostrophe in a plural, where nothing is missing.
- *Your welcome* for *you're welcome*.
- One message written at three temperatures, which reads as uncertainty.
