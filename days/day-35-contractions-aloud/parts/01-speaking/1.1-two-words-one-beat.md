## One-line answer

A contraction is two words squeezed into one beat — *I'm*, *don't*, *it's*, *we've* — and the
squeezed version is the normal one in speech, so saying the full form everywhere is what makes
careful English sound like a written page read out.

## The story

Somebody is telling you about their week and you say, carefully:

> I am sorry. I do not understand. It is not clear to me.

They answer:

> Oh, I'm sorry — I wasn't clear. It's just that we've been so busy.

Listen to the two sides. Yours has eleven words, all full, all the same size. Theirs has the same
content in fewer beats, and the important words — *sorry*, *clear*, *busy* — stand out because
everything around them got small.

Nothing you said was wrong. You had said the written version of English out loud.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **contraction** — two words joined into one shorter word: *I am* → *I'm*.
- **short form** — the same thing, said in plain words.
- **full form** — the two separate words: *I am*, *do not*.
- **beat** — one strong push of the voice.
- **apostrophe** — the mark that stands for the missing letters: ' — it has its own day on paper.

The four contractions in this part are the four you will say most often for the rest of your life:

| Full form | Contraction | What is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I am | I'm | the *a* of *am* |
| do not | don't | the *o* of *not* |
| it is / it has | it's | the *i* of *is*, or the *ha* of *has* |
| we have | we've | the *ha* of *have* |

In speech these are not casual or lazy. They are the ordinary form. The full form is what you use
when you are emphasising, correcting, or being very formal — which is perhaps one sentence in
fifty.

## Why Vaani needs it

Two reasons, and the second is bigger.

You will **hear** these constantly, and a beginner who has practised only full forms hears *don't*
as a word they do not know, because it is not two words said fast — it is one short word with its
own shape.

And when you **say** them, your sentences get a rhythm. The contraction is always unstressed, so it
pushes the stress onto the next word automatically, which is the rhythm practised on day 30
happening by itself.

| we've | [we've](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/we%27ve) | /wiːv/ for the UK and /wiv/ for the US, with a US audio file; "Contraction of *we* +‎ *have*"; a homophone of *weave* |

**The mouth work, one at a time.**

```text
   I'm     →  one syllable. The mouth closes on the m and stops. Not "I-yam".
   don't   →  the t at the end is small and often almost silent before another
              consonant: "I don't know" comes out "I dun-no" in fast speech.
   it's    →  ends in a clear ts, like the end of "cats". Not "it-is".
   we've   →  ends in a v with the teeth on the lip, exactly like day 4's v.
              Not "we-uv", and not "wif".
```

**The one that is two things.** *It's* is *it is* **and** *it has*:

> It's raining. (it is)
> It's been a long week. (it has)

You do not have to choose when speaking — the sound is the same. On paper it matters, and paper
has its own part today.

**Where the full form comes back, and this is a real rule:** at the end of a sentence, and when
you are correcting somebody.

> Are you coming? — I am.  ← never *I'm* on its own at the end
> I said I don't, not I do.  ← full stress for the correction

**And the negative ones carry the meaning**, so they are said slightly stronger than other
contractions. *Don't* is one of the few contractions that can take the beat:

> I DON'T want it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the full form everywhere**:

> I am going to the shop. I do not have money. It is very far.

Correct, and it sounds like a language exercise. A listener hears care, distance, and sometimes
anger — because in English, the full forms are what people reach for when they are being firm.

The second is the **two-syllable contraction**:

> I-yam · it-is · we-uv

The letters are gone, so the syllable has to go too. If your *I'm* has two beats in it, it is
still *I am*.

The third is the **dropped negative**, which is the dangerous one:

> I can go. / I can't go.

Under speed, the *t* of a negative contraction can disappear, and the sentence flips to its
opposite. English speakers protect this with vowel length and stress, not with the *t*. When it
matters, say the full form: *I cannot go.*

The fourth is the **contraction at the end of a sentence**:

> Yes, I'm.

Nobody says this. It is *yes, I am*.

The fifth is **contracting a name or a noun in speech and thinking it is wrong**. In fact, English
speakers do this constantly — *my brother's coming*, *the bus's late* — and beginners often avoid
it because it looks odd written down. In speech it is completely normal.

## In real life

Ordinary conversation is maybe one contraction every five or six words. A speaker who uses none at
all is immediately identifiable, not as a foreigner exactly, but as somebody being careful — which
changes how people speak back to you: more slowly, more simply, and more formally.

Under pressure — an official, an interview, an argument — native speakers *reduce* their
contractions deliberately, for firmness. That is worth knowing, because it means the full form is
not the polite form. It is the firm form.

What a native listener notices in a sentence with no contractions: distance. In a complaint that
is useful. In small talk it makes you sound annoyed when you are only being careful, and that
misunderstanding is the real cost of this lesson not being learned.
