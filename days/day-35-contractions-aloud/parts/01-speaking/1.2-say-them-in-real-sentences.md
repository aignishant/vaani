## One-line answer

A contraction only works inside a sentence, because its whole job is to get out of the way of the
word that matters — so the rep is not the word, it is the sentence with the beat in the right
place.

## The story

Somebody practised *I'm* twenty times until it was perfect. Then she used it:

> I'm ... going ... to the market ... now.

Every word its own size, the contraction included. She had learned to say the word and not to say
the sentence, so the *I'm* was perfect and did nothing, because the sentence around it was still
flat.

Then she said it the way she would say it in her own language — fast at the front, strong in the
middle:

> I'm going to the MARKET now.

One beat. Everything before *market* squeezed against it.

That is what the contraction is for. It is a tool for making something else louder.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **beat** — the strong push of the voice in a sentence.
- **squeeze** — to say something short and quiet.
- **sentence stress** — which word in the sentence is loudest.
- **chunk** — a group of words said as one piece.

A contraction is always in the quiet part of a sentence. It never carries the beat — with one
exception, the negatives, which can.

So every sentence with a contraction in it has a shape:

```text
   I'm going to the MARKET now.
   ──quiet──        BEAT
   We've finished the FIRST part.
   ──quiet──          BEAT
   It's not the RIGHT one.
   ──quiet──    BEAT
```

Practising the contraction alone teaches your mouth the sound. Practising it in a sentence teaches
your mouth its **job**.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every remaining speaking day in this course is a longer stretch of talking: the gate role-play, the
paragraphs in phases 3 and 4, the unscripted interview at the end. A long stretch of English is
held together by beats, and contractions are how you make room for them.

## The rule

**Practise in chunks, not in words.** The first three or four words of an English sentence usually
come out as one piece:

> I'm-gonna-be-late ... I'll-call-you-later ... It's-not-a-problem ... We've-already-eaten

Say each of those as one lump with a single small mouth movement, then add the rest of the
sentence with the beat where it belongs.

**Six sentence frames worth owning**, because they cover most of daily life:

| Frame | Example with the beat marked |
| --- | --- |
| I'm + -ing | I'm WAITING outside. |
| I'm not + adjective | I'm not SURE. |
| It's + adjective | It's TOO expensive. |
| It's + not + noun | It's not the RIGHT size. |
| We've + past participle | We've already PAID. |
| I don't + verb | I don't UNDERSTAND. |

**The negatives take the beat when you mean them.** This is the exception, and it is how English
marks a refusal:

> I DON'T want it.
> That's NOT what I said.

Said with the beat on the negative, that sentence is firm. Said with the beat elsewhere, it is
neutral. Both are useful, and choosing is the skill.

**Link the contraction to the word after it.** This is day 17's linking doing its job here: *it's
a* becomes *it-sa*; *I'm a* becomes *I-ma*; *we've already* becomes *we-valready*. Nothing is
dropped — the words are joined.

**And practise the four most common full sentences you will ever need**, until they come out as
single pieces:

> I'm sorry, I don't understand.
> It's not working.
> We've been waiting a long time.
> I'm not sure — could you say that again?

Those four sentences carry almost every beginner through almost every counter in the world, and
every one of them has a contraction in the quiet part and a beat on the important word.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **evenly-said sentence**:

> I'm going to the market now. (all words the same size)

The contraction is correct and the sentence is flat. The fix is not the contraction; it is
choosing which word gets the beat.

The second is the **beat on the contraction**:

> I'M going to the market.

This is a real English sentence, and it means *me, not somebody else*. If you did not mean that,
the listener now thinks a correction is happening.

The third is **breaking the chunk**:

> I'm ... not ... sure.

Three separate words with three sizes. Said as one piece — *I'm-not-SURE* — it is a normal English
sentence.

The fourth is the **missing negative under speed**, which has its own part today, because it is
the one that changes what you said.

The fifth is **using contractions everywhere including where English wants the full form**, which
is the other side of the same coin:

> Are you ready? — I'm.

## In real life

Fluent speech is chunks, not words: three or four small words joined and pushed against one strong
one. Listening improves when you expect that, because you stop trying to hear the small words
individually — you hear the chunk and the beat.

Under pressure — a fast conversation, a complaint, a room where you feel watched — speakers slow
down and separate their words, and every contraction becomes a full form. That is fine
occasionally; over five minutes it is what makes people repeat things to you more slowly than you
need.

What a native listener notices when you use contractions well: nothing, which is the aim. What
they notice when you never do: that you are being formal, and they match you, which is the exact
opposite of the practice you wanted.

And the useful truth for this whole day: nobody will ever correct you for saying *I am* instead of
*I'm*. This is one of the few things in the course you have to want for yourself, because the
world will not push you towards it.
