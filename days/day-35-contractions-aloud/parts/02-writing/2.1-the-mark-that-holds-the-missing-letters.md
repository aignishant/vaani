## One-line answer

In a contraction the apostrophe stands exactly where the missing letters were — *do not* loses the
*o* and becomes *don't* — so if you can say which letters are gone, you know where the mark goes.

## The story

A hand-written sign went up in a shop window:

> Sorry, were closed today. Well be back tomorrow.

Everybody understood it. One customer read it twice, because *were closed* is a real English
phrase about the past — *we were closed on Monday* — and for half a second the sign seemed to be
saying that yesterday had been the closed day.

Two marks were missing. Not decoration: two marks that each stood for a missing letter, and
without them one sentence briefly meant something else.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **apostrophe** — the mark ' used for missing letters and for showing who owns something.
- **contraction** — two words joined into one shorter word: *we are* → *we're*.
- **short form** — the same thing, in plain words.
- **stand for** — to take the place of.

The apostrophe has two jobs in English, and only one of them is today's.

| Job | Example | Which day |
| --- | --- | --- |
| showing who owns something | my brother's bag | day 14 |
| standing in for missing letters | don't, we're, it's | today |

Today's job has a rule you can check every single time, in your head, in a second:

> **Say the two full words. Find the letters that disappeared. Put the mark exactly there.**

```text
   do not      →  the o is gone      →  do n't     →  don't
   we are      →  the a is gone      →  we 're     →  we're
   I have      →  the ha is gone     →  I 've      →  I've
   it is       →  the i is gone      →  it 's      →  it's
```

The mark never goes between the two original words. It goes where the letters were.

## Why Vaani needs it

Short forms are the normal register for everything you write to people you know — messages,
friendly emails, notes, signs. A page written entirely in full forms reads as stiff or angry, and
a page with the apostrophes in the wrong places reads as careless, which is worse in anything
anybody judges you by.

## The rule

Everything below was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Capital letters and apostrophes](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/capital-letters-apostrophes),
which states: "We use an apostrophe to show where there are missing letters in contractions."

**The model lines from that page:**

> "It's raining."
> "Don't worry, it won't rain."
> "She can't drive because she's broken her leg."
> "I'd like a coffee, please."
> "You'll be fine."

**The contractions you will actually write**, grouped by what is missing:

| Missing | Contractions |
| --- | --- |
| *a* of *are* | you're, we're, they're |
| *i* of *is* | he's, she's, it's, that's, there's |
| *a* of *am* | I'm |
| *ha* of *have* | I've, we've, you've, they've |
| *ha* of *has* | he's, she's, it's |
| *wi* of *will* | I'll, we'll, you'll, he'll, it'll |
| *woul* of *would* | I'd, we'd, you'd, he'd |
| *o* of *not* | don't, doesn't, isn't, aren't, wasn't, haven't, didn't |

**The two odd ones**, which do not follow the pattern and have to be learned as themselves:

> will not → **won't**
> cannot → **can't**

**The pair everybody gets wrong**, and the page names it directly: "Note that **it's** is a
contraction of *it is* or *it has*. **its** is a possessive form of the pronoun *it*."

From the same page:

> "The dog is chasing its tail."
> "Are you sure it's OK for me to ring you so early?"
> "It's rained a lot this week."

The test is one second long and never fails: **say *it is* in place of it.** If the sentence still
works, write *it's*. If not, write *its*.

> It's raining. → *it is raining* ✓
> The dog is chasing its tail. → *the dog is chasing it is tail* ✗

**And the mark itself.** It is the high one — ' — not a comma sitting on the line, and not two
marks. Written by hand it sits in the air above the line, not on it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **missing apostrophe**, which is the story:

> were closed · well be back · dont worry · cant come

Every one is understood, and each one is a different word from the one you meant: *were*, *well*,
*cant* are all real English words with other meanings.

The second is the **mark in the wrong place**, which comes from putting it between the words
rather than where the letters went:

> do'nt · did'nt · is'nt

The letters missing are inside *not*, so the mark goes inside *not*: *don't*, *didn't*, *isn't*.

The third is ***its* / *it's* swapped**, in both directions:

> The dog is chasing it's tail. · Its raining.

This is the most common apostrophe error in English, made by everybody, and the *it is* test kills
it in a second.

The fourth is the **apostrophe in a plural**, which belongs to the other job and is not a
contraction at all:

> Open on Sunday's. · Fresh apple's.

Nothing is missing from those words and nobody owns anything. It is just a plural: *Sundays*,
*apples*.

The fifth is **your / you're**, the same trap in another shape:

> Your welcome. → *you are welcome* → **You're welcome.**

## In real life

Written short forms are normal everywhere except formal documents: notes, messages, emails to
people you know, signs, adverts. In a job application or an official letter, many writers still
use full forms, and that is a real and deliberate difference in temperature rather than a rule
about correctness.

Under pressure — a sign written in a hurry, a message typed with one hand — the apostrophes go
first, because they are the smallest thing on the page and the easiest to skip.

What a reader does with a missing apostrophe: understands, and notices. What a formal reader does
with *it's* for *its* in a letter that matters: reads the rest of the letter more carefully, for
other mistakes.

And a note worth having: phones often add apostrophes for you, and sometimes add the wrong one.
That is not a reason to stop checking; it is a reason to read your own sentence once before
sending anything that matters.
