## One-line answer

Short forms set the temperature of a piece of writing, so the decision is made once per piece —
warm and short for people you know, full forms for anything official — and mixing the two inside
one paragraph is what reads as uncertain.

## The story

The same message, written twice by the same person, to a neighbour whose parcel she had taken in.

> I have taken your parcel. It is in my flat. I will be at home after six. Please do not come
> before that.

and

> Hi — I've taken your parcel in, it's at my place. I'll be home after six, so any time after
> that's fine. Don't ring before, the baby's asleep!

The first is correct and sounds like a notice from a company. The second sounds like a neighbour.

The words are almost identical. The temperature is completely different, and the only difference
is the short forms and one blank line.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **register** — how formal or friendly a piece of writing is.
- **temperature** — the same thing, said in plain words: warm or cold.
- **consistent** — the same all the way through.
- **official** — from or to an office, a company, an authority.

Short forms are a dial, not a rule. They have three settings:

| Setting | Where | Short forms? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| warm | message to a friend, a neighbour, a note in the house | yes, throughout |
| neutral | email to a colleague, a shop, a school | some — *it's*, *I'll*, *don't* |
| formal | application, complaint, letter to an office, anything legal | no, full forms |

And one rule sits above all three: **be consistent inside one piece.** A message that starts
*I've* and ends *I have not* reads as somebody whose feelings changed halfway through — which, in
a complaint, is sometimes true and rarely useful.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate note at the end of this phase has to thank somebody and ask for something, which is
warm-to-neutral, and the applications and complaints in later phases are formal. Knowing which
dial setting you are writing at, before the first sentence, is what stops a friendly note from
sounding like a demand.

## The rule

**Decide the setting first, in one line, before writing:** *this is a note to a neighbour* or
*this is a letter to an office*. Then write the whole thing at that setting.

**The warm setting** — everything short, and this is the model checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Capital letters and apostrophes](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/capital-letters-apostrophes):

> "It's raining."
> "Don't worry, it won't rain."
> "I'd like a coffee, please."
> "You'll be fine."

**The neutral setting** — short forms with the verbs, full forms with the negatives that matter:

> I've attached the form. I'll send the rest on Monday. Please do not send it back by post.

That last full form is doing a job: it marks the one sentence you need the reader to notice.

**The formal setting** — no contractions anywhere:

> I am writing about the invoice dated 3 March. I have not received a reply to my earlier letter.

**What short forms do not fix.** They make writing warmer, not clearer. A friendly message with no
subject line, no ask and no date is still a message that will not be answered, however many
apostrophes are in it.

**Two more places the apostrophe appears in ordinary writing**, both correct and both worth
knowing:

```text
   times and quantities   →  a couple o'clock is rare; nine o'clock is everywhere
   spoken style in quotes →  She said, "I'm not going."
```

**And the one you should not use in anything anybody judges:** the very short spoken forms —
*gonna*, *wanna*, *gotta*, *innit*. They are real English and they are speech written down. In a
message to a friend they are fine; in an email to a school they mark you as somebody who cannot
tell the settings apart, which is a bigger mistake than any grammar point on this page.

**Punctuation to keep straight while you do this**, because it shares the same key on a phone: the
apostrophe is not a quotation mark. *Don't* uses one mark; "quotes" use two at each end.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **mixed piece**:

> Hi, I've taken your parcel. I will be at home after six. Don't ring before. I would be grateful
> if you could collect it before eight.

Four sentences, three temperatures. The last one has arrived from an office letter, and the reader
feels the change without being able to name it.

The second is the **formal letter with contractions**:

> I'm writing about the invoice. I haven't received a reply and I'd like to know what's happening.

Understandable and correct English; in a formal complaint it reads as less serious than you
intended, and serious is the entire point of that letter.

The third is the **warm note written cold**, which is the story, and it is the most common of all
in beginner writing. Full forms everywhere feel safe because they feel careful. To an English
reader they feel distant, and in a note to a neighbour distance reads as annoyance.

The fourth is **spoken forms in the wrong place**:

> I wanna ask about the class fees.

The fifth is **the apostrophe typed as a quotation mark**, which happens on some keyboards and
looks like a mistake even when it is a setting:

> I "m coming.

## In real life

Readers do not analyse your register; they feel it, in the first line, and answer in the same
temperature. Write warm and you get warm back. Write cold to a neighbour and you will get a very
correct reply and a slightly cooler corridor.

Under pressure — a complaint, a disagreement, money — writers often go formal as a way of being
firm, and that is a real, deliberate use of full forms. What it must not be is accidental: if your
note to a friend went formal because you were concentrating on the grammar, they will read it as
you being upset.

What a formal reader does with *gonna* in an application: nothing they will tell you about. What a
friend does with a note full of *I would like to inform you*: asks somebody else whether you are
angry.

And the useful habit: read the first line aloud before you send. If it does not sound like
something you would say to that particular person, the setting is wrong, and the short forms are
the fastest thing to change.
