## One-line answer

Write a real sign with the apostrophes deliberately wrong, put it where people actually read it,
and discover the exact shape of this failure: everybody understands it, nobody mentions it, and
the sign still looks like it was written by somebody guessing.

## The story

A hand-written sign in a window:

> OPEN ON SUNDAY'S
> FRESH APPLE'S AND ORANGE'S
> WE DONT TAKE CARD'S

Four apostrophes. Every one of them in a plural, where nothing is missing and nobody owns
anything. And one missing, in *dont*, where a letter really is gone.

The shop was busy. Nobody said a word about the sign. Every single person who read it understood
it perfectly, and a good number of them noticed.

That is the whole shape of this failure: **fully understood, quietly marked, never mentioned.**

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. The apostrophe in a plural
is one of the most common written errors in English — made by native speakers too — which is why
it is today's deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **plural** — more than one: *apples*, *cards*, *Sundays*.
- **possessive** — showing who owns something: *the shop's window*.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

Three different things use or do not use an apostrophe, and they look alike on the page:

| Kind | Example | Apostrophe? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| plural — more than one | We sell apples. | no |
| possessive — who owns it | the shop's window | yes |
| contraction — letters missing | We don't take cards. | yes |

The test, and it is one question: **is a letter missing, or does somebody own something?** If
neither, no apostrophe.

## Why Vaani needs it

Signs, notices, messages and forms are the writing most people will actually see from you. This is
also the last part of phase 2 that teaches a mark rather than a shape, and the fossil hunt in it —
checking every apostrophe you write — is a habit to carry into the gate note and every phase after
it.

## The rule

**Step 1 — the GREEN sign.** Write a real sign or notice you could actually put up — for a shop, a
door, a shared kitchen, a noticeboard, a gate. Four or five lines. Use at least two contractions
and at least two plurals, with every apostrophe checked by the question above.

> Open on Sundays, 9 to 6.
> We don't take cards — cash only, sorry.
> Fresh apples and oranges every morning.
> The bell doesn't work. Please knock.

**Step 2 — the RED sign, deliberately.** The same sign with exactly these changes:

- an apostrophe added to every plural,
- the apostrophe removed from every contraction,
- one *its* or *it's* swapped.

> Open on Sunday's, 9 to 6.
> We dont take card's — cash only, sorry.
> Fresh apple's and orange's every morning.
> The bell doesnt work. Its broken.

Write **deliberate** on the back of it, not on the front.

**Step 3 — the reading test.** Put the RED sign where people really read things — a door in your
home, a noticeboard, a group message, a family chat. Leave it there. Then, separately, ask two
people two questions:

1. *What does this sign say?*
2. *Is there anything you would change about it?*

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not a sign with four wrong marks. It is a careful piece of writing with
exactly one:

> Thanks for sending the form's. I'll bring them on Monday.

One word. The rest of the message is warm, correct and well shaped, and a reader who was deciding
whether you are careful has just been given an answer.

The second real wrong version is ***it's* used for the possessive** in a formal piece, which is
the one that gets noticed most:

> The company and it's staff.

Everybody in an office knows this one, which is exactly why it is expensive: it is the single
apostrophe error that readers are most confident about spotting.

## In real life

Nobody corrects a sign, a message or an email. Correcting somebody's apostrophes unasked is rude
almost everywhere, so this mistake has no natural predator — it survives for decades in people
who write well otherwise.

Under pressure — a sign written fast with a marker pen, a message typed while walking — the marks
go wrong in both directions at once: added to plurals, dropped from contractions.

What a reader does with *Fresh apple's*: buys apples. What a reader deciding something about you
does: notes it, silently, and it joins whatever else they have decided.

And the reason this part exists at the end of a phase: you now write things people read. The
sweep in step four takes a few minutes and finds every one of these in your own work, and it is
the only method in this document that keeps working after today.
