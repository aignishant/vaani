# What's in the box

`FN-13` · Day 35

## The one-line answer

An anecdote is something you hand to another person for a reason, and the reason — the point — has to
be decided before you open your mouth, because the one thing you cannot do is find it on the way.

## The model

When somebody hands you a parcel, they know what's in it. That's the whole difference between a
present and an inconvenience. A short anecdote works exactly the same way: you're handing something
over, the person takes it, and at the end there has to be something in the box.

So the rule that runs this entire day: **know your last sentence before you say your first one.**
Not the wording. The point. What this story is for, in six or seven words, in your head, before you
begin. If you can't say what it's for, you haven't got an anecdote — you've got a sequence of events
you were present at, which is a different and much less welcome thing.

That's uncomfortable, because it sounds like planning and conversation doesn't allow for planning.
It allows for more than you think. The moment somebody says the thing that reminds you of your
story, you have the length of their next sentence to decide whether there's a point in the box. If
there isn't, don't tell it. That's a real option and fluent speakers use it constantly.

### Three parts, and only one of them is the story

**The way in.** An anecdote in real conversation is never announced. It's hooked onto whatever the
other person just said, and the hook is what makes it welcome instead of an interruption. Two moves:
the ones that hook straight on, and the ones that ask for the floor first.

> Funny you should say that —

> That reminds me — did I tell you about the thing with the car?

The second one is doing something clever. It asks permission, so if they've heard it, or if they
were in the middle of their own thing, nobody's stuck. It costs you one clause and it removes the
worst outcome in this entire skill, which is telling a long story to somebody who was still talking.

**The middle, cut to fit the point.** Here is the rule and you can hear it working: **every detail
that isn't load-bearing for the point comes out.** Not shortened — out. The day of the week. The
name of the road. Who else was in the room. How you got there. Exactly what was said before the bit
that matters. Each of those, if the point doesn't need it, is a small tax you're charging the
listener, and they're paying it while waiting to find out why.

*This was back when I was …* does the whole job of placing the story. No dates, no names, no
sequence of how you came to be there. One clause and you're in.

And you can hear when the middle is too long. The listener starts nodding faster. That's the sound
of somebody helping you get to the end.

**The landing.** This is the part being tested tonight. When you reach the point, say it, plainly,
in one sentence — and *flag* it, so the listener knows it's arrived:

> And the bit that got me was, she'd been there the whole time.

> Which is exactly why I now check before I leave.

The flag matters because a point that isn't marked gets missed. Then, immediately, hand the floor
back. *Has that ever happened to you?* An anecdote that ends and just sits there turns the
conversation into a performance you gave. Give it back in the same breath and it stays a
conversation.

**Today's phrases** — two for the way in, one for the middle, four for landing the point, two for
getting out. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Funny you should say that — | Hooking your story straight onto what they just said |
| That reminds me — did I tell you about …? | Asking for the floor before you take it |
| This was back when I was … | Placing a story without dates, names or a run-up |
| The bit that got me was … | Flagging that the point is arriving now |
| Which is exactly why I now … | Landing the point as a change in what you do |
| So that's why I never … | Landing the point as a rule you've kept since |
| I still think about it, actually. | Landing softly, when there's no lesson, only the thing itself |
| Has that ever happened to you? | Handing the floor back the moment you've finished |
| Anyway — that's the reason I … | Pulling out of a story that has run longer than it should |

## Say it now

These are drilled backwards, because that's the order the skill is actually built in. Point first,
way in second, middle last.

1. *The bit that got me was {{the point}}.*
2. *Which is exactly why I now {{what you do differently}}.*
3. *So that's why I never {{the rule you keep}}.*
4. *I still think about it, actually.*
5. *Funny you should say that — {{the hook}}.*
6. *That reminds me — did I tell you about {{the thing}}?*
7. *This was back when I was {{a period of your life, no dates}}.*
8. *Anyway — that's the reason I {{the point, delivered short}}.*
9. *Has that ever happened to you?*

Now run them as three complete arcs — line 5 or 6, then 7, then a middle of no more than three
sentences, then one of lines 1 to 4, then line 9 — using three different things that have actually
happened to you. Three sentences in the middle. Count them on your fingers if you have to. When you
run out of fingers, land the point.

- **With a partner:** they say a random opening sentence and you hook onto it. At the end of each
  one they tell you the point back in their own words. If it doesn't match what you announced at the
  start, that anecdote didn't land, and you don't argue with them about it.

## The deliberate failure

Tell a fourth anecdote and skip the first step. Don't decide the point. Start with something that
happened, tell it properly, and trust yourself to find the ending when you get there.

**What breaking sounds like:** you'll hear yourself get to the place where the point should be, and
what comes out instead is a small handful of noises. *So, yeah.* *Anyway.* A little laugh with
nothing under it. And the pitch of your voice goes up at the very end, which is what people do when
they're asking the listener to supply the meaning for them.

That upward *so, yeah* is the finding, and once you've heard it on your own tape you'll hear it
everywhere, including in people whose English is perfect. It is not a filler. It is the exact
audible signature of a story that had no point in the box — and it's on a large share of unplanned
stories, including several you've told this month.

Now do the honest bit. Play back the three good anecdotes and check them for the same sound. If one
of those ends in *so, yeah* too, then that one had no point either, and you announced one at the
start, which means you lost it somewhere in the middle. That's a different fault and it's a more
useful one to have found.

## When it breaks in the wild

You're two-thirds through, and the room turns — somebody arrives, a phone goes, two other people
start their own conversation. Your story is now longer than the attention available for it, and the
temptation is to speed up and deliver the whole thing faster.

Don't. Abandon the middle. Everything between where you are and the point can go, and nobody who
wasn't listening will ever know it was missing. Cut straight to what's in the box.

**The repair:**

> Anyway — the point was, I've never left it in the car since.
