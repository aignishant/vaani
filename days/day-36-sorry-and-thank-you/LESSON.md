## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** In [part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-sorry-that-asked-for-comfort.md), play both
takes to a real person and ask what they would say back. GREEN is when the overdone take produces
reassurance — *don't worry, it's not your fault* — and the four-part take produces acceptance and
a closed matter. If both get the same answer, there are still feelings about yourself in the short
version; take them out and run it again.

**Writing:** In [part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-the-sorry-with-a-but.md), give both notes to two
readers and ask what the apology is for, whose fault they think it was, and when it will be fixed.
GREEN is when the defensive note leaves them thinking somebody else is being blamed, or unable to
say when it will be fixed, and the good note answers all three.

Only record what you actually heard or saw. A deliberate exercise is labelled deliberate.

## §7 Traps

- A big apology for a small thing, which makes the other person manage your feelings.
- Saying nothing when somebody apologises to you, which reads as not forgiving them.
- Silence when somebody thanks you; *you're welcome* is not optional in English.
- Four *sorry*s in a row, which stops meaning sorry and starts meaning nervous.
- The reason given instead of the apology: *sorry, the traffic was terrible*.
- An apology with no sentence about what it cost the other person.
- Telling somebody how they felt instead of guessing.
- A promise too big to keep: *I'll never be late again*.
- A thank-you with no specific thing in it, which could have been sent by anybody.
- An apology note with no date in the repair.
- A thank-you with a request attached to the end of it.
- *But*, *however*, *actually*, *as you know* — every one of them a small defence.
