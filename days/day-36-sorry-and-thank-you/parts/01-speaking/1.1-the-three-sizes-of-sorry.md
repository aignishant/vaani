## One-line answer

English has three sizes of *sorry* and three sizes of *thank you*, and using the big one for a
small thing is as strange as using the small one for something that mattered.

## The story

You step backwards in a shop and your bag touches somebody's arm. You turn round and say:

> I am extremely sorry. Please forgive me. It was very bad of me.

The other person, who had barely noticed, now has to do something with all of that. They say *no,
no, it's fine, really* twice, and you both feel worse than before you spoke.

The English for that moment is one word, said lightly, while still moving:

> Sorry.

And the same in the other direction. Somebody holds a door and you say nothing, because *thank you
very much for your kindness* feels too big — so nothing comes out at all, and a small warm moment
turns into a cold one.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **apologise** — to say sorry.
- **accept** — to receive an apology or thanks and close the matter.
- **weight** — how big or serious something is.
- **light** — small, quick, not serious.

Sorry and thank you both come in three sizes, and the size has to match the thing. Too big is as
wrong as too small, and it is the mistake learners make more often, because a big apology feels
safer.

| Size | Sorry | Thank you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| small | Sorry. · Oops, sorry. | Thanks. · Cheers. |
| medium | I'm sorry about that. · Sorry, my fault. | Thank you — that's really kind. |
| big | I'm really sorry. I should have called you. | I don't know how to thank you. Really. |

And both have a third piece almost nobody teaches: **the answer.** Somebody apologises to you;
somebody thanks you. English has fixed, short replies for both, and silence is not one of them.

## Why Vaani needs it

These are the two most frequent social sentences in the language, and they appear in every
remaining day of this course: the gate role-play, the notes and emails of this phase, and the
interview at the end. They also do something none of the other lessons do — they decide whether
people enjoy dealing with you.

## The rule

**Apologising.** The phrases were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Apologising](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/apologising):

> "I'm so sorry!"
> "I'm really sorry!"
> "It was my fault."
> "I'm sorry too."

**Accepting an apology**, from the same page — and this is the half that is usually missing:

> "That's all right."
> "Honestly, it's fine."
> "Don't worry about it."

**Thanking**, at three sizes:

```text
   small    Thanks. · Thanks a lot. · Cheers.
   medium   Thank you — that's really kind of you.
   big      Thank you so much. Honestly, you saved me.
```

**Answering thanks**, which learners often leave silent:

> You're welcome. · No problem. · Any time. · Don't mention it. · That's all right.

**Match the size to the thing.** Three tests, and they are quick:

| Did it cost them anything? | Did it change your day? | Then |
| --- | --- | --- |
| no | no | small |
| a little | a little | medium |
| yes | yes | big |

**Say it while it is happening, not later.** Both of these are said *in* the moment; an apology
that arrives after a pause sounds like one you had to be persuaded into.

**And the two-way version, which is the most useful sentence in this part.** When both of you were
partly responsible:

> Sorry — my fault. — No, I wasn't looking either. Sorry.

From the same page, in the model conversation, exactly this happens: after "I am so sorry! Are you
OK?" the other person answers "That's all right. I shouldn't have been running."

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **oversized apology**, which is the story:

> I am extremely sorry. Please forgive me.

Correct English, wrong weight, and the other person now has to manage your feelings. The fix is
one word, said lightly, while still moving.

The second is the **missing answer**. Somebody says sorry to you and you say nothing, or you smile
and turn away. In English that reads as *I have not forgiven you*, which is rarely what you meant.
Four words fix it: *don't worry about it*.

The third is **thanks with no answer from you** when somebody thanks *you*:

> Thank you so much! — (silence)

*You're welcome* is not optional in English the way it is in some languages.

The fourth is the **repeated sorry**, which is a habit rather than a mistake:

> Sorry, sorry, sorry — sorry.

Twice is the maximum for anything. Beyond that it stops meaning sorry and starts meaning nervous,
and listeners start reassuring you instead of accepting the apology.

The fifth is **thanking for the wrong thing**:

> Thank you for your help. (said to somebody who did the whole thing themselves)

*Thank you for doing it* is what they earned. Small precision, large difference.

## In real life

English speakers say *sorry* constantly, in situations with no fault at all — walking past
somebody, reaching across a table, not hearing a sentence. It is a lubricant, not a confession,
and copying that use is one of the fastest ways to sound ordinary in English.

Under pressure — somebody annoyed, something that cost money, a mistake at work — learners either
oversize the apology, which reads as panic, or avoid it entirely, which reads as not caring.

What a native listener notices most: whether the answer came. An apology or a thank-you that lands
in silence is the awkward moment people remember, and it is nearly always caused by a learner
searching for a big enough sentence rather than by anything they said.

And the real difference worth knowing: in many languages, a light apology for a small thing sounds
insincere, so learners avoid it. In English it is the opposite — the light one is correct, and the
heavy one is what sounds odd.
