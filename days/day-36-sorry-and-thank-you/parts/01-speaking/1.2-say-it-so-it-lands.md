## One-line answer

A real apology has four parts — say it, name what you did, say what it cost them, say what you
will do — and a real thank-you has three, and the part everybody skips is the one that names the
effect on the other person.

## The story

Somebody was an hour late to a dinner and said, at the door:

> Sorry! The traffic was terrible.

True, and it landed as nothing, because it was about traffic. Everything the host had actually
experienced — food going cold, guests waiting, wondering whether to start — was missing from it.

What would have landed:

> I'm really sorry. I should have called you when I was stuck. You must have been wondering
> whether to start without me — next time I'll ring as soon as I know.

The words *I'm sorry* are in both. The difference is that the second one is about the person who
was waiting.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **effect** — what happened to the other person because of it.
- **excuse** — a reason given to move the fault away from you.
- **specific** — naming the exact thing.
- **repair** — what you will do so that it does not happen again.

An apology has four parts, and they answer four different questions in the other person's head:

| Part | The question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| I'm sorry. | Does he know it was wrong? |
| I should have called. | Does he know what he did? |
| You must have been waiting. | Does he understand what it did to me? |
| Next time I'll ring. | Will it happen again? |

Part three is the one that makes an apology land. Parts one, two and four can all be said sincerely
and still leave the other person unsatisfied, because nothing in them says the speaker noticed
what it cost.

A thank-you has the same shape, one part shorter:

| Part | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Thank you. | Thanks so much. |
| for the specific thing | for taking the parcel in |
| and what it did for me | I'd have missed it completely. |

## Why Vaani needs it

Two of the writing days in this phase end in notes that do exactly this, and the phase 2 gate note
has to thank somebody in a hundred words. Out loud, this is the conversation that decides whether
a mistake at work, with a landlord, or with a friend becomes a problem or an ordinary moment.

## The rule

**The four parts, said as one short run.** Long is not sincere; complete is sincere.

> I'm really sorry. I should have called you when I got stuck. You must have been wondering
> whether to start — next time I'll ring as soon as I know.

**Part one — say it plainly.** The phrases were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[Apologising](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/apologising):

> "I'm so sorry!"
> "I'm really sorry!"
> "It was my fault."

**Part two — name the thing, not the category.**

| Vague | Named |
| --- | --- |
| Sorry about everything. | Sorry I didn't call. |
| Sorry for the trouble. | Sorry I gave you the wrong date. |

**Part three — the effect, and say it as a guess, not as a fact:**

> You must have been waiting.
> That probably held up the whole morning.
> I think you had to do it twice because of me.

Guessing is right here. Telling somebody how they felt is a different mistake.

**Part four — the repair, small and specific:**

> Next time I'll ring as soon as I know.
> I'll send the right one today.

**The reason, if you give one at all, goes at the end and stays short.** A reason at the front
becomes an excuse:

> Sorry I didn't call — the traffic was unbelievable. ✓ (reason, small, last)
> The traffic was terrible, so I'm late. ✗ (the traffic apologised, not you)

**And the thank-you, in its three parts:**

> Thanks so much for taking the parcel in. I'd have missed it completely, and I was out all day.

**Then stop.** Both of these end. The commonest error after this point is adding more, which is
the subject of the next part.

**And accept the answer.** From the same page, checked the same day, this is what comes back:

> "That's all right." · "Honestly, it's fine." · "Don't worry about it."

When you hear that, the matter is closed. Say *thanks* and move on to something else; going back
to the apology reopens something the other person has just shut.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **reason instead of an apology**, which is the story:

> Sorry! The traffic was terrible.

The other person hears a fact about roads. The fix is not more sorry — it is part three.

The second is the **apology with no named thing**:

> I'm sorry about everything.

The listener does not know what you have understood, so they cannot be sure it will not happen
again.

The third is the **effect told rather than guessed**:

> I know you weren't really upset.

You have just decided how they felt, inside an apology. If you must say something, guess and leave
room: *you must have been annoyed.*

The fourth is the **promise too large to keep**:

> I'll never be late again.

Nobody believes it, including you. A small specific repair is worth more than a large promise.

The fifth is **the thank-you with no effect**:

> Thanks for your help.

Fine, and it disappears. *I'd have missed it completely* is what makes somebody glad they helped.

## In real life

A complete apology takes about three sentences, and it usually ends the matter immediately. An
incomplete one produces a conversation — questions, explanations, a second attempt — which is
longer and worse for both of you.

Under pressure — money, work, somebody genuinely angry — people either explain (which sounds like
excuses) or over-apologise (which sounds like panic). The four parts are worth knowing by heart
precisely because they are hard to find in the moment.

What a native listener notices: whether you named what it cost them. That single sentence is what
people mean when they say somebody apologised *properly*.

And a real difference to know: in English, apologising quickly for a small workplace mistake — and
then saying what you will do — is normal and expected, and it usually ends the matter. Waiting to
be asked about it makes a small thing into a bigger one.
