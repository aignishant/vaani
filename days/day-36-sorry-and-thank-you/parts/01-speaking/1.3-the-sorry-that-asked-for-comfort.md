## One-line answer

Record the long, unhappy apology — the one with five sorries and a bad feeling in it — and listen
to what the other person has to say back: they end up comforting you about your own mistake.

## The story

Somebody forgot to bring a document a colleague needed. She apologised like this:

> Oh no, I'm so sorry, I'm really sorry, I can't believe I did that, I'm so stupid, I've been
> forgetting everything this week, I'm really really sorry, I feel terrible.

Her colleague said: *It's fine! Don't worry! It's really not a problem, honestly.*

Then he brought her a cup of tea.

Look at what happened. He had the problem. He did the reassuring. He spent his morning making her
feel better about a document he still did not have — and nobody ever said when it would arrive.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. The long apology that turns
into a request for comfort is the most common failure in this part of English, and it comes from
genuine feeling, which is why it is today's deliberate break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **comfort** — making somebody feel better.
- **self-blame** — words about how bad you are: *I'm so stupid*.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you heard it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

An apology is for the other person. The moment it contains your feelings about yourself, the roles
swap: they become the helper and you become the one being helped, over a problem you caused.

| What you say | What it asks the listener to do |
| --- | --- |
| I'm sorry I forgot. I'll bring it this afternoon. | accept, and move on |
| I'm so stupid, I feel terrible, I can't believe I did this. | reassure you |

Both are sincere. Only one of them is an apology.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every phase after this one has work in it — asking, explaining, being late, getting something
wrong. The apology that asks for comfort is the one that quietly makes people stop bringing you
things to do, because dealing with your mistake costs them twice.

## The rule

**Take one — the comfort-seeking apology, deliberately.** Record a real mistake of yours, said
with:

- at least four *sorry*s,
- two sentences of self-blame — *I'm so stupid*, *I always do this*,
- nothing about what it cost the other person,
- no repair and no date.

Say "this take is deliberately overdone" first, out loud.

**Take two — the four-part apology**, from [part 1.2](1.2-say-it-so-it-lands.md): say it, name it,
say the effect, say the repair. Short.

**The check. Play both to a real person and ask them one question:** *what would you say back to
me?*

Write their answers down word for word.

**It is GREEN when take one produces reassurance — *don't worry, it's fine, it's not your fault* —
and take two produces acceptance and a closed matter.** The two sound similar and they are
completely different: one is them looking after you, the other is the thing being over.

**It is RED when both get the same answer.** That usually means take two still has feelings about
yourself in it. Take them out and run it again.

**And count.** Listen to take one and count two numbers: how many times you said *sorry*, and how
many sentences were about you rather than about them. Write both numbers down.

**Then repair.** Record five pairs, long then short:

> I'm so sorry, I'm terrible at this, I always forget, sorry. — Sorry, I forgot it. I'll bring it
> this afternoon.
> I feel awful, I can't believe I did that. — That must have held you up. I'll fix it now.

The short versions are not colder. They are the ones that leave the other person free.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not always long. It is the apology with ***but*** in it, which is
shorter and does more damage:

> I'm sorry I was late, but the traffic was terrible and nobody told me the road was closed.

Everything before *but* is deleted by everything after it. The listener hears a defence, and the
apology is spent.

The second real wrong version is the **apology repeated later**:

> — Don't worry about it, honestly.
> — No, but really, I'm so sorry.

They have closed the matter. Reopening it makes them do the work again, and the second time they
are less warm, and both of you notice.

## In real life

Colleagues, friends and family accept short complete apologies instantly and are relieved by them.
What they cannot do quickly is manage somebody else's distress about a small mistake, and after
two or three of those they start protecting themselves — by not telling you when something went
wrong, or by fixing it silently instead.

Under pressure — a real mistake, somebody visibly annoyed, money involved — the self-blame version
arrives on its own, because it is what the feeling produces. The four-part version has to be
practised in advance to be available then.

What a native listener does with the comfort-seeking apology: comforts you, kindly, and quietly
notes that the problem has not been solved. What they do with the four-part one: say *that's all
right* and move on, which is what you wanted in the first place.

And the honest note: feeling bad about a mistake is normal and right. The lesson today is only
about where those feelings go. They do not belong in the apology, because the apology is not
yours — it belongs to the person you apologised to.
