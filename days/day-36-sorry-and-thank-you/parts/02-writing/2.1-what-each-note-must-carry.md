## One-line answer

A thank-you note names the exact thing and says what it did for you; an apology note names the
exact thing, says what it cost them, and says what happens next — and both are short.

## The story

Somebody stayed with a family for a week and afterwards sent:

> Thank you for everything. It was very nice. Thanks again.

It is warm and it is also a note that could have been sent by any guest to any host in the world.
Nothing in it says that this family did anything in particular, so nothing in it can make them
glad they did it.

The version that would:

> Thank you for everything last week — especially the lift to the station at five in the morning.
> I'd never have made that train, and I'd probably have spent the whole day worrying about it.

One specific thing. One effect. It is shorter than a page of general warmth and it is the one that
gets kept.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **note** — a short written message, sent or left for somebody.
- **specific** — naming the exact thing.
- **effect** — what it did for you, or what it cost them.
- **excuse** — a reason given to move the fault away from you.

Two notes, two shapes. They are mirror images, and each has one part that carries the whole thing.

| Thank-you note | Apology note |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Thank you | 1. I'm sorry |
| 2. for exactly this | 2. for exactly this |
| **3. and here is what it did for me** | **3. and here is what it cost you** |
| 4. something forward: see you, come and stay | 4. what I am doing about it, and by when |

Part three is the one that gets left out of both, and it is the one a reader feels.

## Why Vaani needs it

These two notes are the end of phase 2's writing: the gate note asks for something, thanks
somebody and gives a direction, in a hundred words. They are also the two written things people
keep, and the two that repair relationships when speaking is too hard or too late.

## The rule

**The thank-you note.** The model and tips were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[A thank-you email](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/thank-you-email):

> "Hi Carolina, I just wanted to write and say thank you for everything. You and your family were
> amazing hosts."
> "If you ever come to Sri Lanka, I hope you will stay with me"
> "Keep in touch!"

And its guidance: open with *Hi* for something informal; a good thank-you message has three parts
— say your thanks, give specific examples, and offer a future connection; and close with something
like *See you soon*, *Write soon* or *Take care*.

So, with part three added, which is where your own work goes:

```text
   Hi <name>,

   I just wanted to say thank you for <exactly what they did>.
   <What it did for you — the sentence only you can write.>
   <Something forward.>

   <Close>
   <Your name>
```

**The apology note.** The structure was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[An email explaining a problem](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/email-explaining-problem),
whose tips say: "Say what the problem is first. Then, give more details. Finally, offer a solution
or say what you hope will happen next." and "Make it short and clear. Just include the most
important information."

Its model opens by saying the problem plainly:

> "I'm writing to let you know that I can't attend class for two weeks as I've had an accident and
> hurt my back."

and closes with:

> "Thank you for your understanding."

So the apology note is:

```text
   Dear <name>,      or     Hi <name>,

   I'm sorry about <exactly what happened>.
   <What it cost them — one sentence, guessed, not claimed.>
   <What you are doing about it, and by when.>
   <A short reason, if there is one — one clause, at the end.>

   Thank you for your understanding.
   <Your name>
```

**Length: five or six lines for either.** A long apology reads as anxiety; a long thank-you reads
as a form.

**No *but*, anywhere in an apology note.** Everything before *but* is deleted by everything after
it. If a reason is needed, it goes last, in its own short sentence.

**And the register**, which you set once: warm short forms for a friend or a neighbour, full forms
for an office, a landlord or a school.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **general thank-you**, which is the story:

> Thank you for everything. It was very nice.

Nothing specific, so nothing lands. The fix is one concrete thing: the lift, the soup, the phone
call at nine at night.

The second is the **apology that is mostly explanation**:

> I'm sorry, but the traffic was terrible, and then the office was closed, and my phone had no
> battery, and nobody told me the date had changed …

By line three the reader is being asked to agree that it was not really your fault.

The third is the **missing repair**. The apology is warm, complete and the reader still does not
know when they will get the document, the money or the key.

The fourth is the **thank-you that asks for something**:

> Thanks so much for the lift! By the way, could you also pick up my parcel next week?

The thanks has become a doorway to a request, and the reader feels it. Ask in a separate message.

The fifth is the **apology sent too late to be an apology**, which turns into an explanation of
why you did not write sooner. If it is late, say so in four words — *sorry for the slow reply* —
and then write the apology as normal.

## In real life

Written apologies and thank-yous are kept. People read them twice, sometimes show them to
somebody, and remember them long after the event. That is what makes the specific sentence worth
the extra minute.

Under pressure — a real mistake, money, something that upset somebody — writers explain, because
explaining feels like taking responsibility and is actually the opposite.

What a reader does with a general thank-you: feels mildly pleased, and forgets it. What they do
with a specific one: remembers that you noticed. What a formal reader does with an apology
containing four reasons: forwards it to somebody, with the reasons as the story.

And a real-life difference: a thank-you can be as small as one line and still be right. An apology
for something that cost somebody real time or money cannot; it needs all four parts, and it
usually needs to be written the same day.
