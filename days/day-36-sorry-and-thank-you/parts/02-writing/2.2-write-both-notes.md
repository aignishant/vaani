## One-line answer

Write both notes to real people and send them — the thank-you in five lines with one specific
sentence, the apology in five lines with a repair and a date — because a note that is not sent has
not been written.

## The story

Two notes, both real, both short.

> Hi Meena,
>
> I just wanted to say thank you for Tuesday — for staying with my mother until I got back. She
> told me you sat with her the whole time and talked about the garden.
>
> I was stuck on the other side of the city and I'd been worrying the entire afternoon. Knowing
> you were there was the only reason I got through the meeting.
>
> Come for lunch on Sunday if you're free.
>
> Take care,
> Anita

> Dear Mr Rao,
>
> I'm sorry about the mistake in last month's order — I sent the 5 kg bags instead of the 10 kg
> ones.
>
> I know that meant your Saturday delivery was short, and you had to explain it to your own
> customers.
>
> I've sent the correct bags this morning and they'll reach you by Thursday. I'm not charging for
> this delivery.
>
> Thank you for your understanding.
>
> Anita

Different temperatures, same skeleton. Each one names a specific thing, says what it meant for the
other person, and points forward.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **draft** — a first version.
- **send** — to actually give it to the person.
- **specific sentence** — the one line only you could have written.
- **repair** — what you will do about it, with a date.

Both notes are short, and short is the hard part. The way to get there is to write the four parts
first — which you did in [part 2.1](2.1-what-each-note-must-carry.md) — and then join and cut,
rather than starting at *Dear* and writing until you run out.

The order of work:

1. The four labelled parts.
2. Join them into five or six lines, with blank lines between blocks.
3. Cut everything that is not one of the four parts.
4. Read it once as the person receiving it.
5. Send it.

Step five is not decoration. An unsent note is an exercise; a sent one changes something between
two people, and that is what this phase has been building towards.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate is a hundred-word note that asks for something, thanks somebody and gives a
direction. That note is these two notes plus a request, written in one piece. If you can write
both of these today and send them, the gate is a matter of joining, not of learning.

## The rule

**The thank-you, line by line.** The shape and closings were checked on 2026-09-22 at
[A thank-you email](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a1/thank-you-email):
its model opens "Hi Carolina, I just wanted to write and say thank you for everything", offers a
future connection — "If you ever come to Sri Lanka, I hope you will stay with me" — and closes
"Keep in touch!"; its tips give *See you soon*, *Write soon* and *Take care* as closings.

```text
   Hi <name>,
   <blank>
   I just wanted to say thank you for <the exact thing>.
   <blank>
   <What it did for me — the specific sentence.>
   <blank>
   <Something forward.>
   <blank>
   <Close>
   <Name>
```

**The apology, line by line.** The order was checked on 2026-09-22 at
[An email explaining a problem](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/email-explaining-problem),
whose tips say: "Say what the problem is first. Then, give more details. Finally, offer a solution
or say what you hope will happen next." and "Make it short and clear. Just include the most
important information." — and whose model closes with "Thank you for your understanding."

```text
   Dear <name>,
   <blank>
   I'm sorry about <the exact thing>.
   <blank>
   <What it cost them — guessed, one sentence.>
   <blank>
   <What I am doing, and the date it lands.>
   <blank>
   Thank you for your understanding.
   <Name>
```

**Put a date in the repair.** *Soon* in an apology is the same failure as *as soon as possible* in
a request: it reads as *whenever*.

**Keep the register consistent** — warm short forms for the neighbour, full forms for the customer
— and decide it before the first line.

**Punctuation that this piece is judged on**, because both notes are short enough that every mark
is visible: a comma after the greeting, a full stop at the end of every sentence, an apostrophe
only where letters are missing or somebody owns something, and no exclamation marks in the
apology.

**And read it once in the other person's voice before sending.** The two questions to ask:

> Does this name something only I could have noticed?
> Does this tell them what happens next?

If either answer is no, the note is not finished.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is the **unsent draft**. It is written, it is good, and it sits in a
notebook, and the relationship it was for never hears it. Everything in this part is undone by
that one habit.

The second is the **apology with no date**:

> I'll fix it as soon as I can.

The reader has to decide what that means, and they will assume the worst version of it.

The third is the **thank-you that is all about you**:

> I had such a wonderful time. I really enjoyed the food. I was so happy to be there.

Three sentences about your experience and none about what they did. Part three must be about what
*they* did *for you*, not about how you felt in general.

The fourth is the **note that has grown into a letter**. Twelve lines of warmth, and the specific
sentence is on line nine where it will be skimmed past.

The fifth is the **mixed note** — an apology with a request attached, or a thank-you with a
question. Each note does one job. The request goes in a separate message tomorrow.

## In real life

Short notes get answered and long ones get postponed, exactly like every other piece of writing in
this phase. And unlike requests, these two get kept: a specific thank-you is remembered for years
by the person who received it, which is a strange, real and cheap thing to know about people.

Under pressure — a real mistake, a real debt of kindness — writers delay, because the note feels
like it should be big. The delay then becomes another thing to apologise for, and the note gets
harder every day it is not sent.

What a reader does with a note that names what it cost them: relaxes. The thing they were braced
to explain has already been said, so nothing needs defending. That is why part three does more
work than any amount of *sorry*.

And the real difference from speaking: in speech you can watch their face and add another
sentence. On paper you get one attempt, so the specific sentence has to be there the first time.
