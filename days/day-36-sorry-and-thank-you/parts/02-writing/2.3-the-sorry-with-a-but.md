## One-line answer

Write the apology with *but* in it and the reasons at the front, show both versions to a reader,
and ask one question — *does this person think it was their fault?* — because that is what the
word *but* actually does to an apology.

## The story

Two versions of the same note, about the same missed delivery.

> Dear Mr Rao,
>
> I'm sorry about the order, but the driver was new and nobody had told him about the second
> gate, and your office number on our file was wrong, so we couldn't call. It was a very difficult
> week here.

and

> Dear Mr Rao,
>
> I'm sorry the order didn't arrive on Friday.
>
> I know that left you short for the weekend, and you had to tell your customers something you
> shouldn't have had to.
>
> The bags went out this morning and will reach you on Thursday, and there's no charge for this
> delivery.
>
> Thank you for your understanding.

The first one is honest. Every fact in it is true, including the wrong phone number, which really
was his office's mistake.

And that is the problem: by the end of it, the customer has been told that some of this was his
fault, inside a note that opened with *sorry*.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make. Writing the reasons before
the repair is the most common failure in written apologies, which is why it is today's deliberate
break.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **but** — the word that marks what follows as more important than what came before.
- **defence** — words that protect you from blame.
- **RED** — the check failed, and you saw it fail.
- **GREEN** — the check passed.

*But* is not a neutral joining word. It tells the reader: *the real information is on this side*.

```text
   I'm sorry I was late,  BUT  the traffic was terrible.
   ↑ politeness                ↑ what I actually want you to believe
```

Every reader in every language feels this. It is why an apology with *but* in it does not land, and
why it often makes things worse than not apologising at all.

Today you write it, deliberately, and ask a reader what they make of it.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 2 gate note has to thank somebody and ask for something — both of which die the same way
if the reasons come first. Beyond this course, the apology note is the piece of writing most
likely to be read by somebody who is already annoyed, and the one where a single word decides
whether the matter ends.

## The rule

**Step 1 — the GREEN note.** Your apology from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-both-notes.md): what happened, what it cost them, the repair with a date, a
short reason last if any.

**Step 2 — the RED note, deliberately.** Same facts, three changes:

- put every reason before the repair,
- join the apology to the first reason with *but*,
- add one sentence that suggests part of it was their fault.

> I'm sorry about the order, but the driver was new and your number on our file was wrong, so
> nobody could call you.

Write **deliberate** on your copy.

**Step 3 — the reader test.** Give both notes to two real readers, separately, and ask each of
them three questions:

1. *What is this person apologising for?*
2. *Does the writer think it was their fault?*
3. *When will the problem be fixed?*

**It is GREEN when readers of the RED note say the writer is blaming somebody else, or cannot say
when it will be fixed — and readers of the GREEN note can answer all three questions.** That is
the break working.

**It is RED when both notes read the same.** Look at your GREEN note again: there is probably a
reason in it that has drifted forward, or the repair has no date.

**Step 4 — the word hunt.** In both notes, circle every one of these:

```text
   but · however · although · actually · to be fair
   unfortunately · as you know · I did tell you
```

Each one is a small defence. In a thank-you note they are harmless. In an apology, every single
one moves the note a step away from being an apology, and *as you know* and *I did tell you* are
the two that end relationships.

**Step 5 — repair.** Rewrite the apology once more with:

- no word from the hunt list,
- the reason moved to the end as its own short sentence, or deleted,
- the repair with a date,
- one sentence saying what it cost them.

Then send it to the person it is actually for.

## When it breaks

The real wrong version is not a note full of excuses. It is a good apology with one *but* in it:

> I'm really sorry the order was late. I know it left you short over the weekend, but the phone
> number on our file was wrong.

Two excellent sentences and then the door closes. The reader reaches the end holding the phone
number, not the apology, and their next message will be about the phone number.

The second real wrong version is the ***sorry if***:

> I'm sorry if you were upset by this.

This is not an apology. It says that the upset is in question, and every reader knows it. The fix
is to say what happened and what it cost, with no *if*: *I know this upset you.*

## In real life

Annoyed readers read apologies looking for one thing: whether you understand what happened to
them. A reason at the front answers a different question — whether it was your fault — which they
had not asked yet.

Under pressure — a customer, a landlord, a colleague, a friend who was hurt — writers explain,
because being blamed for something that was not entirely your fault is genuinely hard, and the
truth feels like it belongs in the note. Often it does belong. It belongs *later*, in a different
message, after the matter is closed.

What a reader does with an apology containing *but*: replies to what came after the *but*. What
they do with a complete one: says *that's fine, thanks for sorting it out*, and the exchange ends.

And the practical rule to carry out of this phase: **if a reason must be in the note, it goes
last, in its own sentence, with no *but* in front of it.** One short reason at the end reads as
information. The same reason at the front reads as a defence.
