## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** In [part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-nod-that-cost-you-the-address.md), do the
silent-nod take with a real person, then say back what you think you agreed to before they tell
you. GREEN is when the second take gets every detail right and carries at least three repairs
that land inside their turn. RED is when the details you got wrong in the first take were ones a
single repair would have caught. To make it go red on purpose, ask them to speak at their normal
speed about something with four separate details in it, and do not repair once.

**Writing:** In [part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-i-told-he-yesterday.md), give a real reader three
versions of the same note and ask only *who did what?* GREEN is when the repaired note is
understood first time with no question. RED is when the note with correct words but lost pointing
costs them more than the note with broken words — a re-read, a question, or a wrong answer.

Only record what you actually heard or saw. A deliberate exercise is labelled deliberate.

## §7 Traps

- *Sorry?* three times in a row, which only ever gets you the same sentence louder.
- Saying *my English is bad* instead of naming what broke.
- Waiting until the end of a long turn to say you missed the middle of it.
- Nodding at a number, a date or a price — the three places a nod is never free.
- A repair with silence after it, which reads as *still lost*.
- *Right, right, right* — the silent nod wearing a hat.
- *Thank he*, *to I*, *with she* — the subject word standing in the object slot.
- *Me and him went* — the object word standing in the subject slot.
- Three *he*s and two men in the same paragraph.
- A pronoun written before the name it points at.
- The same name in every sentence, which reads as a form rather than a note.
- *It* pointing at a whole sentence when two things are in play.
