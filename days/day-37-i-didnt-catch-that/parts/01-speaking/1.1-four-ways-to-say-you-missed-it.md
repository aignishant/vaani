## One-line answer

When you miss what somebody said, you have four different repairs — *say it again*, *say it more
slowly*, *say that one word again*, *did you mean this?* — and choosing the right one tells the
other person exactly what to fix, so the conversation carries on instead of starting over.

## The story

A delivery driver is at your gate with a parcel. He says something fast, looks at you, and waits.

You did not catch it. So you say:

> Sorry?

He says it again — same speed, same words. You still did not catch it. You say *sorry?* again. Now
he says it louder, because *sorry?* only tells him **that** you missed it. It never tells him
**what** you missed.

The third time, you try something different:

> Sorry — could you say that more slowly?

He slows down, and this time you get it. He wants your flat number.

Nothing about your English changed between the second try and the third. What changed is that the
third repair told him what to do.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **repair** — a short sentence that fixes a small break in a conversation.
- **turn** — one person's go at speaking, before the other person speaks.
- **volume** — how loud a voice is.
- **speed** — how fast somebody speaks.
- **confirm** — to check that what you think you heard is right.

A conversation breaks in one of four ways, and each way has its own repair.

| What went wrong | What you say | What the other person then does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You heard nothing at all — noise, a bad line | *Sorry, I can't hear you very well.* | speaks up, or moves |
| You heard it, but it went past too fast | *Could you say that more slowly, please?* | slows down |
| You got all of it except one word | *Sorry — the last word?* | repeats that word only |
| You think you got it, but you are not sure | *Do you mean the blue one?* | says yes, or corrects you |

The fourth one is the strongest, and beginners use it least. It gives the other person something
to say *yes* to, and *yes* is the easiest thing anybody can say.

## Why Vaani needs it

Day 40 is a recorded role-play in a shop or a café, both voices yours, and twelve turns is the
shortest it can be. Real shop turns break all the time — a till beeps, somebody asks a question
from behind you — and a role-play where nothing ever breaks is not a shop. It is a script read
aloud.

And this is the skill that keeps every other skill usable. You can know a word perfectly and still
lose it in fast speech. What you lose then is not the word. It is the rest of the conversation,
because you stop listening while you worry about the part you missed.

## The rule

**These repairs come from a page opened and checked on 2026-09-23:**
[Checking understanding](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a1/checking-understanding).
It carries a video and a phrase list, and it gives them in this form:

> I'm sorry?
> I can't hear you very well.
> Could you say that again, please?

and, for checking what you think you heard:

> Do you mean the hammer? Is this it?

**Say the repair, then stop.** All of them are short. A long repair is one more sentence for the
other person to miss.

> Sorry, could you say that again?   ← good
> Sorry, I am very sorry, my English is not good, I did not understand what you said just now,
> could you please say it once more?   ← a long apology with no repair inside it

**Where the voice goes.** Each of these ends with the voice going **up**, because it is a
question. If your voice goes down, the request stops being a request:

```text
   Could you say that again?  ↗     asking
   Could you say that again.  ↘     complaining
```

**The naming repair — the one that does the most work.** Say back the part you *did* get, and stop
at the hole:

> You said the office closes at …?
> Take the second left, and then …?

The other person fills the hole and repeats nothing else. You do not have to hear the whole
sentence twice and miss it twice.

**The confirming repair.** Say what you think you heard, in your own words:

> So I pay here and collect it there?
> You mean Tuesday, not today?

If you are right, they say *yes*, and you have lost nothing. If you are wrong, they fix one thing,
and the mistake dies where it was born instead of three sentences later.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the silent nod**:

> Yes. Yes, OK.

You did not understand, and you said yes anyway. This is the most expensive habit a beginner has.
The other person takes your *yes* as an answer and carries on, and five sentences later you have
agreed to a day you cannot come, a price you did not hear, or a form you were never told to bring.

The second is **the same repair three times**:

> Sorry? … Sorry? … Sorry?

You are giving the same instruction to somebody who has already followed it. They cannot guess
which part broke, so they get louder — and loudness was never the problem.

The third is **apologising for yourself instead of naming the break**:

> Sorry, my English is very bad.

A listener hears this as *stop talking to me*. They simplify everything, speak to you like a small
child, or turn to the person beside you. You asked for a repair and you got pity.

The fourth is **the repair that arrives too late**. You wait until the end of a long turn and then
say you missed it, so they say all of it again and you miss a different part. Break in at the
hole, not after it.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of these is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers repair constantly. Listen to two people who share a first language: *sorry, what?*,
*the what?*, *wait, whose sister?* It happens several times in an ordinary conversation and nobody
treats it as a failure. Only learners think a repair is an admission of something.

Under pressure a repair gets shorter, not longer. In a noisy street a native speaker says *the
what?* and nothing else. In an office, *sorry, say again?* At a counter with a queue behind you,
the naming repair is the polite one, because it takes the least of everybody's time.

What a native listener notices is a learner who never repairs. It reads as either perfect
understanding or none at all, and they will test which one it is. What they forgive instantly is a
repair with a question in the voice. What they do not forgive twice is a *yes* that turns out to
have meant *no*.

  counter, on a phone call, at work, at home. For each one, record all four repairs about that
