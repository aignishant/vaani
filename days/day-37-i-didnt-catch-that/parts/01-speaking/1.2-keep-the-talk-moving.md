## One-line answer

A repair works when it is short, when it lands inside the other person's turn instead of after it,
and when you give something back straight away — so the conversation keeps its direction and you
are not the person who made everybody start again.

## The story

You are at the counter of a small workshop, getting a phone screen replaced. The man behind the
counter is filling a slip and talking at the same time.

> Right, so it'll be ready Thursday after four, and bring the slip, and we don't take cards
> under two hundred, so …

You missed the middle. You wait politely until he finishes, and then you say:

> Sorry, I didn't understand.

So he says all of it again, from *Thursday*. And again it goes fast, and again you get the
beginning and lose the middle, and now there are two people behind you.

The repair you needed was four words long and belonged fifteen words earlier:

> Sorry — bring the what?

One word comes back — *the slip* — and he carries on from where he was. Nobody started again.
Nobody queued.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **interrupt** — to speak while the other person is still speaking.
- **hold your turn** — to keep your go at speaking, instead of giving it away.
- **land** — to arrive at the right moment.
- **direction** — where a conversation is going, and what it is about.

A repair has three jobs, and most learners do only the first.

1. **Say what broke.** One of the four repairs from
   [part 1.1](1.1-four-ways-to-say-you-missed-it.md).
2. **Put it in the right place.** As near to the hole as you can get, not at the end of the turn.
3. **Give something back.** After you understand, say the small word that shows it landed —
   *right*, *got it*, *Thursday, OK* — and then add your own sentence so the other person is not
   left holding the whole conversation.

The third job is the one that keeps the talk moving. A repair with no answer after it feels to the
other person like a test they keep failing.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 role-play is at least twelve turns and both voices are yours. Turns are the thing being
counted, and a repair that makes the other person restart eats three turns and produces nothing.
A repair that lands inside the turn costs one turn and produces the missing word.

It also decides who does the work. If your repair gives nothing back, the other person has to keep
inventing sentences. People get tired of that quickly, and the conversation you wanted to practise
ends early.

## The rule

**Interrupting to repair is allowed, and it is normal.** English speakers do it with a small
lifting word first, so the interruption does not sound like a correction:

> Sorry — the what?
> Hang on — Thursday?
> Wait — two hundred?

The lifting word — *sorry*, *hang on*, *wait* — is half a beat. Then the hole, said as a question.
Then silence, and you let them answer.

**Repeat the last thing you understood, with the voice going up.** This is the shortest possible
repair and the other person does not have to guess where you stopped:

> … and bring the slip, and we don't take cards …
> Bring the …?

**Then close the loop.** Say one word that means *I have it now*:

> Right. Got it. OK, Thursday after four.

Saying the fact back in your own words is the strongest version, because the other person hears
whether you got it right and fixes it if you did not.

**Then give something back.** A question of your own, or a fact. It hands the turn over cleanly:

> Right, Thursday after four. And where do I pay?

**The shape of the whole thing**, in one picture:

```text
   they speak  ───────────┐
                          │  sorry — the what?        (short, at the hole)
                          ▼
   they answer  ──────────┐
                          │  right, the slip          (close the loop)
                          ▼
   you speak   ───────────┐
                          │  and where do I pay?      (give something back)
                          ▼
   the talk keeps its direction
```

**When you are on a phone and there is no face to read**, say the repair earlier. You cannot see
somebody finishing a sentence on a phone, so waiting for a gap means waiting too long.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the polite wait**. You know the repair; you save it for the end
of the turn. What the other person gets is a request to say four sentences again, and what you get
is the same four sentences at the same speed.

The second is the **repair with nothing after it**:

> Sorry, could you say that again?
> … Thursday after four.
> *(silence)*

You understood, but you gave no sign, so they do not know whether to say it a third time. Two
seconds of silence after a repair reads as *still lost*.

The third is **the false close**:

> Right, right, right.

Said quickly, three times, this is the silent nod wearing a hat. It tells the other person to
carry on, which is exactly what you did not want.

The fourth is **repairing the wrong thing**. You missed the price, but you ask about the day,
because the day was the last word you heard clearly. Then you walk out knowing the day and not
the price. Ask about the hole, even when the hole was four words back.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

In a fast conversation between two fluent speakers, repairs are almost invisible. They overlap:
one person says *the what?* while the other is still speaking, the missing word comes back, and
neither of them stops. Nobody apologises and nobody restarts.

Under pressure — a counter with a queue, a call on a bad line, somebody who is late — the polite
wait is the expensive choice and the interruption is the kind one. An official who has said the
same thing forty times today would much rather say one word than five sentences.

What a native listener notices: whether your *right* comes with the fact attached. *Right,
Thursday after four* ends the matter. *Right* on its own gets tested later — they will ask you
what day it is before you leave, because they are not sure you have it.
