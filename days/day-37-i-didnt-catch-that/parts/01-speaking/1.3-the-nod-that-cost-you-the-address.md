## One-line answer

Say *yes* to something you did not understand, on purpose, in front of a real person, and then ask
them what you just agreed to — because the cost of the silent nod is invisible until somebody
tells you what you signed up for.

## The story

Somebody from your building stops you on the stairs and gives you directions to a wedding hall.
They talk for a while. You catch *near the temple* and *after the flyover*, and you lose everything
between.

You nod. You say:

> Yes, yes. OK.

Two days later you are standing at a flyover with a phone at four per cent, and there are two
temples, and the one you can see is the wrong one.

Nothing in that conversation felt like a failure while it was happening. That is the whole
problem with the nod: it is comfortable in the moment and expensive later, and the two are far
enough apart that most people never connect them.

Today you connect them on purpose, in a room, where being lost costs nothing.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **agree** — to say yes to something.
- **consequence** — what happens afterwards because of what you did.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You are going to run the same conversation twice with a real person.

- **The RED take.** They tell you something with real detail in it. You understand about half. You
  nod, you say *yes*, you never repair. At the end you ask them to write down what you agreed to.
- **The GREEN take.** Same conversation, same person, same kind of detail. This time you repair
  every time something goes past you, using
  [part 1.1](1.1-four-ways-to-say-you-missed-it.md) and
  [part 1.2](1.2-keep-the-talk-moving.md). At the end, you say the whole thing back.

Then you compare what you agreed to with what they actually said.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 role-play is judged on whether a stranger can follow it, and a role-play with a silent
nod in it sounds fine to you and wrong to them. You cannot hear your own nod. Somebody else has to
show it to you, once, in a way you remember.

And the ledger needs this. `docs/ERRORS.md` is the most valuable file in this repository, and the
rows worth having are the ones with a real consequence attached: not *I said yes when I was lost*,
but *I said yes and the four things I agreed to were these, and two of them were wrong*.

## The rule

**Set the RED take up so it can actually fail.** Ask a real person for something with parts in it:
directions with three turns, a recipe with five steps, or how to do something at an office —
what to bring, where to go, who to ask, when it closes. Tell them to speak at their normal speed
and not to help you.

**Then do the wrong thing on purpose.** Nod. Say *yes*. Say *OK, OK*. Do not repair once, however
much you want to.

**Then make the damage visible.** Straight after, say to them:

> Don't tell me yet. I'll say what I think you said.

Say your version out loud with the recorder on. Then let them correct every part, and write both
versions down.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the wrong details in your table are
details you would have got with one repair — a number, a name, a day. It is **GREEN** when the
second take gets every detail right *and* the recording has at least three repairs in it that land
inside the other person's turn.

A RED here is the good outcome the first time. A RED you do not write down is a wasted evening.

**Then run the GREEN take** and count, out loud at the end of the recording: how many repairs, and
which of the four kinds each one was.

**The last piece — the repair for a nod you already gave.** This is the one nobody teaches, and
you will need it in real life, because sometimes you nod before you decide to stop nodding:

> Sorry — I said yes, but I'm not sure I got the last part. Thursday, or Tuesday?
> Can I say it back to you, to check?

Say it late rather than never. A correction two minutes later costs a sentence; a correction two
days later costs the afternoon.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version in the exercise itself is **a RED take that quietly repairs**. You
cannot help it — you ask one small question, and the take comes out fine, and you learn nothing.
If your RED take produces four correct details, it was not a RED take. Run it again with somebody
who talks faster.

The second is **a RED take with no consequence attached**. You nod, you notice you are lost, and
you stop there. Nothing is written down, so nothing is learned. The table above is the exercise.

The third, in real life, is **the nod that hardens**:

> Yes. … Yes. … Yes.

Three nods in, you cannot repair any more without admitting the first two were empty. That is why
the repair belongs at the first hole.

The fourth is **agreeing to a time you did not hear**, which is the most common expensive one:

> So I'll see you then?
> Yes!

*Then* is not a time. If the time was never said clearly, *yes* is a promise you cannot keep.

## In real life

Native speakers nod at things they did not catch too — but they nod at *words*, not at
*decisions*. A fluent listener will let a strange word go past and ask about it later, and will
stop dead at a number, a date, a place or a price. That is the line: the nod is free on the parts
that carry no consequence, and never free on the parts that do.

Under pressure the line moves in the wrong direction. At a hospital desk, at a police checkpoint,
in front of somebody in a hurry, the wish to be agreeable is strongest exactly where the
consequence is largest. That is worth knowing in advance, because you will feel it and you will
have to say *sorry, can I check that?* anyway.

What a native listener notices: a *yes* that comes too fast. It arrives before the sentence has
finished, and a listener who is paying attention will stop and test it — *so what time are you
coming?* If you have ever been asked that straight after agreeing, somebody had already spotted
your nod.
