## One-line answer

English has two sets of words for people — one set for the person **doing** the action (*I, he,
she, they*) and one set for the person the action **lands on** (*me, him, her, them*) — and the
second set goes after the verb and after small words like *to*, *for* and *with*.

## The story

You are writing a note for the family upstairs, because you borrowed their ladder.

You write:

> I gave the ladder to your son. He took it up. Please thank he for me.

Your neighbour reads it, understands it perfectly, and smiles at the last three words. Not because
the meaning was lost — it was not — but because *thank he* is the sort of thing a sentence cannot
do in English, in the way a shoe cannot go on the wrong foot and still feel right.

The fix is one letter and one sound:

> Please thank him for me.

Same person, same sentence, different slot. English asks you to change the word when the person
moves from the front of the sentence to the back.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **pronoun** — a short word that stands in place of a name: *I*, *she*, *them*, *it*.
- **verb** — the doing word in a sentence: *give*, *call*, *thank*, *see*.
- **subject** — the person or thing that does the verb. It usually comes before the verb.
- **object** — the person or thing the verb lands on. It usually comes after the verb.
- **preposition** — a small word that goes before a person or a thing: *to*, *for*, *with*, *from*.

English gives you the same person twice, in two shapes.

| Doing the action (subject) | Receiving the action (object) |
| --- | --- |
| I | me |
| you | you |
| he | him |
| she | her |
| it | it |
| we | us |
| they | them |

Two of them never change: *you* and *it*. The other five do, and the five that change are the five
you will write every day.

The rule for which one to use is about **position**, not about the person:

> **She** called **me**.
> **I** called **her**.

Same two people, both times. The word changes because the slot changes.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 gate is a note of about a hundred words that has to do three jobs — ask for something,
thank somebody, and give a direction. Every one of those three jobs points at a person who is not
the writer:

> Could you send **it** to **me**?
> Thank you for helping **us**.
> Give **them** the key when **they** arrive.

A hundred-word note with names repeated in every sentence reads like a form. A note with the wrong
object pronoun reads like a beginner. This is the rule that makes the difference between them.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Personal pronouns](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/personal-pronouns).
It sets out both sets and says object pronouns are used as the object of a verb or after a
preposition, with these examples:

> Can you help me, please?
> She is waiting for me.

**Slot one: straight after the verb.** Ask *who did the verb land on?*

> I called **him** yesterday.
> The shop keeps **them** at the back.
> Tell **her** the price.

**Slot two: after a small word — to, for, with, from, about, at.**

> Give the key to **me**.
> I bought it for **us**.
> She came with **them**.
> I heard about **you** from **him**.

Every word after those small words comes from the second column. There are no exceptions to learn.

**The shape on the page**, so you can see the two slots:

```text
      subject        verb        object
        She         called        me.
         I          called       her.
         We          gave        them      the keys.
                                  ▲
                      after the verb  ──────┘

      ... and after a small word:

        to me   ·   for us   ·   with him   ·   from her   ·   about them
```

**Two people in a row.** When a sentence has a person *and* a thing, the person usually comes
first and both keep their own shape:

> Send **me** the photo.
> I gave **her** the money.

Or you turn it round and use the small word:

> Send the photo to **me**.
> I gave the money to **her**.

Both are correct. The second is slightly more formal and is safer when the sentence is long.

**Two people joined by *and*** is where careful writers slip:

> He sent the form to my brother and **me**.

Test it by dropping the other person: *He sent the form to me* — correct. *He sent the form to I*
— not English. So *me* is right, and *my brother and I* would be wrong in that slot.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the subject word in the object slot**:

> Please thank he for me.
> I saw she at the market.
> Give the book to I.

A reader understands all three and hears a beginner in all three. The fix is one word each time:
*him*, *her*, *me*.

The second is **the object word in the subject slot**, which is rarer but louder:

> Me and him went to the shop.

In writing this is read as careless rather than foreign. The subject slot takes *He and I went to
the shop*.

The third is **repeating the name instead of using any pronoun at all**:

> I met Ravi on Tuesday. I gave Ravi the receipt. Ravi said Ravi would call me.

Nothing here is wrong and nothing here is English prose. Names repeated in every sentence read as
a police report.

The fourth is **dropping the object altogether**, which happens when a writer's first language
allows it:

> I bought yesterday. Did you see?

An English sentence with *bought* wants to know **what**, and one with *see* wants to know **what**
or **who**. Write *I bought it yesterday. Did you see it?*

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A fluent writer chooses between the name and the pronoun by counting how far back the name was.
Once a name is two or three sentences old, a bare *him* or *them* starts to feel unsafe and the
name comes back — not for grammar, but so the reader never has to stop and work out who is
meant.

Under pressure this is the rule that survives. In a hurried message, people drop words, shorten
sentences and use no punctuation at all, and they still write *tell her*, never *tell she*.

A formal reader — an office, a landlord, a form — notices the third failure above more than the
first. A pronoun mistake reads as a language learner, which is forgiven. A note with the same name
in six sentences reads as a machine, which is answered more slowly.

- **Write:** `TODO(me)` Write fourteen true sentences about real people in your life. Seven with
  the person straight after the verb, and seven with the person after *to*, *for*, *with* or
  *from*. Use each of *me*, *him*, *her*, *us* and *them* at least twice. Then take three of your
  sentences and write each one a second time with the people swapped round, so the subject word
