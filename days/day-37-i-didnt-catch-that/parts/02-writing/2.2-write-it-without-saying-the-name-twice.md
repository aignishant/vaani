## One-line answer

Write the name once, then use *him*, *her*, *them* or *it* for the rest of the paragraph — and
bring the name back the moment a reader could point at two different people and not know which
one you mean.

## The story

Two families share a water connection. One of them writes a note to the other about the pump.

> Sunita called the plumber. The plumber told Sunita the pump is old. Sunita asked the plumber to
> come on Saturday, and the plumber said the plumber will bring a new part.

Every sentence is correct. Nobody would have to read it twice to understand it. And nobody would
read it twice for pleasure either — it is four sentences wearing the same two names like a
uniform.

Now with the second set of words in place:

> Sunita called the plumber. He told her the pump is old. She asked him to come on Saturday, and
> he said he'll bring a new part.

Same facts, same length, and the second version has something the first does not: it moves.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph** — a group of sentences about one thing, with a blank line before and after it.
- **refer** — to point at somebody or something with a word.
- **ambiguous** — able to mean two things, so the reader has to guess.
- **antecedent** — the name that a pronoun points back to. If you remember the idea, the word does
  not matter.

There is a simple pattern underneath good English writing, and it has two halves.

**The first half: name once, then point.** You write the name the first time, and after that you
point at the person with a short word, because the reader is still holding the name in their head.

**The second half: bring the name back when pointing stops being safe.** Pointing stops being safe
in three places:

1. when a second person of the same kind arrives — two men, two women, two offices;
2. when the name is several sentences back and the reader may have let go of it;
3. at the start of a new paragraph, which is a new breath for the reader.

That is the whole skill. The rest is noticing.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 note is about a hundred words and it carries three jobs. A hundred words is short enough
that every repeated name is visible and long enough that the reader can lose track of who is who.
Both failures fit inside one note.

And a note that repeats names does more damage than it looks. It makes a request sound colder, and
this note has a thank-you in it.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Personal pronouns](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/personal-pronouns),
which gives the object set as *me, you, him, her, it, us, them* and shows them after verbs and
after prepositions, as in:

> Can you help me, please?

**Step one: write it with every name in place.** Names everywhere. Ugly and correct. This is a
draft, not a note.

**Step two: read it and mark the second appearance of every name.** Those are your candidates.

**Step three: replace the ones that cannot be misread.** Keep the ones that can.

> Ravi met the landlord on Tuesday. **He** gave **him** the rent.

Who gave the rent to whom? A reader cannot tell — two men, two *he*s. This one keeps a name:

> Ravi met the landlord on Tuesday. **Ravi** gave **him** the rent.

**Step four: check the first sentence of each paragraph.** If it opens with *He* or *They*, and the
name was in the paragraph before, put the name back. A paragraph is where readers look up from the
page.

**The test that decides every case.** Ask: *could a tired reader point at the wrong person here?*
If yes, write the name. If no, point.

**What *it* does.** *It* points at things, not people, and it is the one that goes silently wrong,
because a thing can be a whole sentence:

> The pump is leaking and the tank is empty. I told the owner about **it**.

About which? Use the noun when two things are in play: *about the pump*.

**The shape of a well-pointed paragraph:**

```text
   sentence 1    Sunita called the plumber.        ← both names, once
   sentence 2    He told her the pump is old.      ← point, point
   sentence 3    She asked him to come Saturday.   ← point, point
   sentence 4    Sunita will be at home.           ← name back: new job, new focus
```

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the paragraph with a lost he**:

> My brother went to the office with his friend. He said he would wait outside, but he did not.

Three *he*s and two men. A reader guesses, and about half of them guess wrong. The fix is one
name: *My brother said he would wait outside*.

The second is **a pronoun before its name**:

> He was very helpful, so I am writing about the man at the counter.

The reader meets *he* with nobody to attach it to and has to hold an empty box until the end of
the sentence. Put the name first.

The third is **over-correcting back to names** after somebody tells you your writing is unclear:

> Sunita called the plumber. Sunita told the plumber. The plumber told Sunita.

You have solved ambiguity by removing all the pointing. Now nothing is unclear and nothing is
readable.

The fourth is the one that mixes today's two halves together:

> Sunita called the plumber. **Him** said the pump is old.

The person doing the verb takes *he*. The fix is in
[part 2.1](2.1-the-word-that-comes-after-the-verb.md): the slot decides the word.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A fluent writer does this without noticing, and the proof is that they re-read a paragraph and
change one *he* back into a name without being able to say why. The why is the tired reader.

Under pressure — a message typed on a phone while walking — people point more and name less, and
the result is the everyday message that has to be answered with *who?* If you have ever had to ask
*who do you mean?*, somebody pointed once too often.

A formal reader is stricter than a friend. A landlord, an office or a bank reads a note once, acts
on it, and does not come back to ask which person you meant. In that setting, writing the name one
extra time is not clumsy. It is what careful people do.

## Check yourself

- **Write:** `TODO(me)` Write a real note of eight to ten sentences about something that actually
  happened between two people you know — a repair, a delivery, a favour, a misunderstanding. Write
  it twice. The first version uses every name in every sentence. The second version keeps the names
  only where a reader could point at the wrong person. Then mark, in the second version, every
  place you kept a name and write one word beside it saying why: *two people*, *far back*, or *new
  paragraph*.
- **Say out loud:** Read the second version aloud to somebody who does not know the story, then ask
  them who did what. Say which sentence they got wrong, if any.
