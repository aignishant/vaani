## One-line answer

Write the note with the wrong pronouns on purpose, give it to a real reader, and ask them one
question — *who did what?* — because the two pronoun failures cost completely different amounts,
and until a reader shows you that, you will spend your worry on the cheap one.

## The story

Two notes go out to the same neighbour on the same day.

The first one says:

> I told he yesterday. Please give the key to I when you come.

The neighbour reads it, knows exactly what to do, brings the key, and thinks nothing more about
it.

The second one says:

> Anil met the electrician at the meter. He said he would come back on Friday, and he asked him to
> wait downstairs.

The neighbour reads it three times. Then they call, because they cannot work out who is coming on
Friday and who is waiting downstairs.

The first note is wrong in the way that is easy to see and costs nothing. The second note is
*correct in every word* and costs a phone call. Today's job is to feel that difference, once, with
your own writing and a real reader.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **reader test** — giving your writing to a real person and asking a question to find out what
  they understood.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.
- **cost** — what the mistake makes somebody do: nothing, a re-read, a phone call, a wrong action.

You will write three short notes about one real thing that happened between two people you know.

- **Note A — the broken-word note.** Deliberately use subject words where object words belong:
  *thank he*, *to I*, *with she*. Otherwise make it clear.
- **Note B — the lost-pointing note.** Every word correct, but written so the pointing is
  ambiguous: two people of the same kind, and *he* or *she* doing all the work.
- **Note C — the repaired note.** Correct words, and names put back wherever a reader could point
  at the wrong person.

Then a real reader answers one question about each: **who did what?**

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 gate note has a person in almost every sentence — somebody to ask, somebody to thank,
somebody to be directed. That is exactly the shape that produces note B by accident, and note B is
the one that gets a note rewritten or ignored.

And the ledger wants both rows. Two different failures, two different costs, written down as what
a real person actually did with the page.

## The rule

**Write all three notes about the same real event**, five to seven sentences each. Same facts, same
order. Only the pronouns change.

**Then run the reader test.** Give the reader one note at a time and ask exactly this:

> Who did what? Tell me in your own words.

Write down their answer word for word before you say anything. If you explain first, the test is
over.

**The cost column is the point.** Write what actually happened, not what you expected: *read it
once and understood*, *read it twice*, *asked me a question*, *got it wrong*.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when note B costs the reader more than
note A — a re-read, a question, or a wrong answer — while note A, with its broken words, is
understood first time. It is **GREEN** when note C is understood first time with no question, and
you can say in one sentence which repair in it did the work.

If note B is understood perfectly, it was not ambiguous enough. Put a second person of the same
kind in it and run it again.

**Then do the hunt.** Take the last three real messages you sent to anybody. Find every *he*,
*she*, *it* and *they* in them, and for each one write the name it was pointing at. Any pronoun
where you have to stop and think is a note that nearly cost somebody a phone call.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a note A that nobody notices**. You write *give
the key to I*, and the reader reads straight past it, because readers repair small word errors
without knowing they did. That is not a failed exercise — it is the finding. Write it in the cost
column: *nothing*.

The second is **a note C that has gone back to names everywhere**. If your repaired note reads
like the uniform paragraph in
[part 2.2](2.2-write-it-without-saying-the-name-twice.md), you have not repaired it; you have
removed the skill.

The third, in real writing, is **fixing the cheap error and never seeing the expensive one**. A
learner who has been corrected for *thank he* starts checking every pronoun word and still writes
paragraphs where nobody can tell which man is coming on Friday.

The fourth is the mixed one, which is the worst of both:

> Anil met the electrician. Him said he will call he later.

Broken words *and* lost pointing. The reader gets neither the grammar nor the story.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Editors and teachers correct note A because it is easy to correct. Readers punish note B, because
it is the one that makes them do work. Both matter, and they are not the same size: one marks you
as a learner, the other stops your message working.

Under pressure, note B is what a hurried writer produces. Typing fast on a phone, people point
more than they name, because the person and the story are perfectly clear in their own head. The
reader has no head to look into.

A formal reader — a landlord, an office, a bank — will act on the note without asking. That is the
real risk of note B: it does not always come back as a question. Sometimes it comes back as the
wrong person waiting downstairs on Friday.
