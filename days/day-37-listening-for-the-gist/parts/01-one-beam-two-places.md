# One beam, and you keep pointing it at yourself

`IN-04` · Day 37

## The one-line answer

You cannot listen to somebody and build your reply at the same time, so the fix is not to listen
harder — it is to stop building.

## The model

Picture a torch with one beam. You can point it at the person talking, or you can point it at the
half-finished sentence you are assembling in your own head. Wherever the beam is not, it is dark.

Here is what actually happens. Somebody starts talking. Four or five words in, you catch the
topic — and the moment you catch it, the beam swings inward, because now you have something to
prepare. You start drafting. You look for the word. You check the tense. You rehearse the opening.

And they are still talking. They have gone on to the second half of it, the bit with the actual
question in it, and that half arrives in the dark. When they stop, you deliver the sentence you
built — and it answers something they were saying at the start, or something they never asked at
all. They look slightly puzzled. They say *no, I meant —* and start again, and now you are behind
by a whole turn.

This is the single most common reason a conversation in a second language goes wrong, and it has
nothing to do with your vocabulary. Your ears are fine. You just took the beam off them.

**Why listening harder does not work.** Because that is not what is broken. You were listening
hard — inwardly, at your own draft, with enormous effort. Telling yourself to concentrate more
just makes you concentrate more on the draft. **The thing you have to give up is the drafting.**

That sounds impossible, and it is the whole day, so let us look at why it is affordable.

**You do not need a head start.** English hands you openings that carry no content at all and buy
you the entire construction time. That was Day 9 — *bear with me*, *give me a second, I'll get
there* — plus the ordinary business of taking a breath and saying *right* before you begin. A
reply started after they finish, with a filler on the front, arrives sooner than a reply you built
during, because the built one has to be scrapped and rebuilt the moment you discover what they
actually asked.

**You do not need every word.** You need three things, and only three. Point the beam and collect:

- **What it is about.** The subject. Usually in their first handful of words.
- **Where they stand on it.** Are they pleased, annoyed, unsure, joking? This is carried mostly by
  the voice, which is why you can often get it even when the words go past too fast. Day 26 taught
  you the fall and the rise; this is what that was for.
- **What they want from you.** An answer, an opinion, agreement, information, or just somebody to
  hear it. This is the one that gets lost in the dark, and it is the one that decides what your
  reply has to be.

Three hooks. If you come out of their turn holding those three, you can always say something, even
if you missed half the words in the middle. If you come out holding a beautifully built sentence
and none of the three, you have nothing.

**Say the gist back, out loud.** This is the move that makes the whole thing safe, and it is
today's real equipment. When they stop, you do not answer yet. You hand the gist back in one line
and let them confirm it.

> So what you're saying is, the whole thing's been moved to next week.
> Right, so it's basically down to the two of us now. Is that about right?

Look at what that line does. It proves you were listening. It gets you corrected *before* you have
committed to a wrong reply, which is much cheaper than being corrected after. And while you are
saying it, your reply builds itself in the background, because by now you know all three hooks.
It costs you one sentence and buys you the whole turn.

There is one habit to kill on the way. **Stop nodding while the beam is inward.** You already do
it — the nodding runs on autopilot, it looks like listening, and it makes the other person speed
up and add more, which buries you further. If you have lost the thread, the nod is a lie that
costs you the next thirty words.

**Today's phrases** — the gist going back out, so you get corrected before you commit. All of
these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| So what you're saying is … | Handing the whole gist back in one line |
| Let me just check I've got this. | Announcing the check before you make it |
| So the point is … | Naming what the turn was actually about |
| Right, so it's basically … | Compressing a long turn into a single sentence |
| Is that about right? | Handing the check back for them to confirm |
| So it's not so much … as … | You had the shape slightly wrong and you fix it out loud |
| Ah — so that's why … | The gist landed, and you show that it landed |
| And you're asking me whether …? | Naming what they actually want from you |

## Say it now

Each line twice. Once slowly, once at the speed you would really say it — which is fast, because
these are all throwaway lines and none of them is the point of your turn.

1. *So what you're saying is, {{the other person's whole point, in one sentence}}.*
2. *Let me just check I've got this — {{the bit you are least sure about}}.*
3. *So the point is {{what it was actually about}}, not {{what it looked like it was about}}.*
4. *Right, so it's basically {{the short version}}.*
5. *{{Your version of their point}} — is that about right?*
6. *So it's not so much {{what you first thought}} as {{what it turned out to be}}.*
7. *Ah — so that's why {{the thing that now makes sense}}.*
8. *And you're asking me whether {{the actual question}}?*

Then run all eight again with one change: put a real breath at the front of each, and start on the
out-breath. These are lines you say while your reply is still assembling, and the breath is doing
half the work. If you can say them without rushing, your reply gets built inside them for free.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d37-free.m4a`.**

> Find some talk you have not heard before — a radio discussion, a recorded talk, anything where
> somebody speaks for a good stretch. Play a chunk of it, stop it, and **with the beam kept on
> them the whole way through**, say out loud: what it was about, where they stood on it, and what
> they wanted from whoever was listening. Then, and only then, say what you think about it. Do
> that three times with three different chunks.

- **Solo form:** as written. Do not take notes. Notes are the beam swinging inward with a pencil
  in its hand.
- **With a partner:** they talk about something you did not know they cared about, for a good
  stretch, without pausing to help you. You say the gist back before you reply. They tell you
  afterwards which of the three hooks you got wrong — and there is usually one.

## The deliberate failure

Play another chunk of the same talk. This time, **draft your reply out loud, in a whisper, while
they are still speaking.** Do not stop whispering. Record both — them playing, you whispering over
the top. Then, the moment it ends, try to say what the last stretch was about.

**What breaking sounds like:** play it back and listen to where your whisper is. You will hear it
lock on to something they said early and stay there, polishing, while their voice moves on and on
underneath it. Your whisper is a perfectly good sentence about paragraph one, arriving at
paragraph four. Nothing about it sounds panicked or slow. It sounds fluent. That is the
uncomfortable part — **this failure does not sound like a failure**, which is exactly why you have
been doing it for years without noticing.

Then run one more chunk with the whisper banned and the gist said afterwards, and listen to the
difference in what you can report. Same ears, same chunk length. The only variable was where the
beam was pointing.

## When it breaks in the wild

Somebody asks you a two-part question — a bit of context, then the actual question on the end. You
lock on to the context, because it came first and you understood it, and you build a reply to it.
They finish. You answer the context. They wait, then say *no, I meant, are you free on Thursday?*
And now you feel caught out, so you apologise, and the apology takes longer than the answer would
have.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — let me just check I've got this. You're asking me whether I'm free on Thursday?

No apology for your English, no request to repeat the whole thing. You hand back the one hook you
actually need confirmed, and the conversation carries on from there.
