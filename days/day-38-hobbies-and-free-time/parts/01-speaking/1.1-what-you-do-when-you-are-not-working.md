## One-line answer

An answer about your free time needs three pieces — **the thing**, **how often**, and **one small
detail nobody could guess** — because the thing alone is a label, and the detail is what the other
person can actually talk to you about.

## The story

Two people are waiting outside a school gate. It is the end of the day and the children are late
coming out, so there are a few minutes to fill.

> So what do you do when you're not at work?

> Reading.

And that is the end of it. The other person says *oh, nice*, and they both look back at the gate.

Now the same question, and an answer with the other two pieces in it:

> So what do you do when you're not at work?

> I read, mostly at night. About one book a month. Detective stories — the old ones, nothing new.

Now there is something to hold on to. The other person says *my father reads those*, or *which
one are you reading now?*, and suddenly the wait at the gate is a conversation instead of two
people looking at a gate.

Nothing changed in the first word. Everything changed after it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **hobby** — something you do in your free time because you like it, not because you must.
- **free time** — the time when you are not working or studying.
- **how often** — how many times in a week, a month or a year.
- **detail** — one small true thing that makes an answer real.
- **label** — a word that names something without saying anything about it.

Three pieces make an answer, and most beginners give only the first.

| Piece | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The thing | names it | *I cook.* |
| How often | puts it in your life | *Most Sundays.* |
| One detail | gives the other person a way in | *Only rice dishes. I'm bad at bread.* |

The third piece is the one that does the work, and it is the easiest, because it is the only piece
you cannot get wrong. It is your own life.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the conversation you will have most often with a stranger who is being friendly. At a
wedding, in a waiting room, next to somebody on a long journey — after the weather and the work
question, this is the third thing people ask.

And the day 40 role-play is twelve turns in a shop or a café. Twelve turns is longer than buying a
coffee. Turns get filled with exactly this kind of small, true, personal detail, and a speaker who
answers everything in one word runs out of conversation at turn four.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Talking about personal interests](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/talking-about-personal-interests).
It carries a video and these phrases, which are the ones this part builds on:

> What do you do when you're not working?
> How often do you practise?
> Most mornings, usually.
> I normally meditate once or twice a week.

**Piece one — the thing, in a full sentence.** Not the bare noun.

> I cook.
> I play cricket.
> I look after plants.

The bare noun — *cooking*, *cricket* — is an answer to a form, not to a person.

**Piece two — how often.** These are the ordinary words, and they sit in two places:

| Word | Where it goes | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| always, usually, often, sometimes, never | before the verb | *I usually cook on Sundays.* |
| every day, twice a week, most evenings, once a month | at the end, or at the front | *I cook every Sunday.* |

> I usually play on Saturday mornings.
> I go for a walk most evenings.
> I paint once or twice a month, when there's time.

**Piece three — one detail nobody could guess.** This is the piece to prepare, because under
pressure nobody invents detail. Good detail is small and true:

> I cook, most Sundays. Only rice dishes — I'm bad at bread.
> I play cricket on Saturdays. I'm the one nobody wants to field near the boundary.
> I look after plants. Twelve pots, and four of them are the same plant, because I kept splitting it.

**The shape, in one picture:**

```text
   I read,          ← the thing        (a full sentence)
   mostly at night, ← how often        (puts it in your week)
   detective stories — the old ones.   ← the detail (the way in)
```

**And the fourth piece, when the answer is honest:** the reason. It starts with *because*, and it
is the one that makes an answer sound like a person rather than a list:

> I walk in the evening because the house is loud until nine.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the one-word answer**:

> Reading.
> Music.
> Nothing much.

Each one is true and each one ends the conversation. The other person now has to invent the next
question by themselves, and most people only do that twice before giving up.

The second is **the list with no life in it**:

> I like reading, music, travelling, cooking, movies and sports.

Six labels, no detail, and the listener cannot pick one up. A long list says less than one
sentence about one thing.

The third is **the answer that is really an apology**:

> I don't have any hobbies. I just work.

Almost nobody has no free time at all. What people mean is *I have nothing impressive*. A walk to
the market, a programme you follow, the plants on the balcony — those are answers, and they are
better answers than an impressive thing you do not actually do.

The fourth is **how often, invented to sound better**:

> I read every day.

If it is not true, the next question — *what are you reading now?* — arrives immediately and you
are standing in a hole you dug thirty seconds ago.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Between fluent speakers this is a two-way game, not an interview. The answer is short, has one
detail in it, and comes back with a question. Nobody talks about their hobby for a minute
unprompted; they give a handhold and wait to see whether the other person takes it.

Under pressure — a job interview, meeting somebody important, a first conversation with
in-laws — the detail is what people remember afterwards. *He cooks* is forgotten by the car park.
*He cooks, but he can't make bread* is repeated at dinner.

What a native listener notices: whether the frequency word and the detail match. *I play every
day* followed by *I haven't played since last year* is heard clearly, and it makes everything else
in the conversation lighter. What they forgive completely: a small hobby, said honestly.
