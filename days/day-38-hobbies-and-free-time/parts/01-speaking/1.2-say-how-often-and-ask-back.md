## One-line answer

Answer, then hand the turn back — with a question, or with a short sign that you are interested —
because a conversation about free time dies the moment one person is doing all the asking.

## The story

Back at the school gate. This time you have prepared, and your answer has all three pieces in it.

> What do you do when you're not at work?

> I cook, most Sundays. Only rice dishes — I'm bad at bread.

> Oh, nice.

Pause. They look at the gate. You look at the gate. You gave a good answer and the conversation
still ended, because a good answer is only half a turn.

The half that was missing is four words long:

> … I'm bad at bread. What about you — do you cook?

Now they are talking, and you are listening, which is the easiest part of any conversation and the
part beginners forget they are allowed to have.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **hand back** — to give the other person the next turn.
- **follow-up question** — a question about what somebody just said.
- **show interest** — to say a short thing that means *go on, I'm listening*.
- **balance** — both people speaking about the same amount.

A turn about free time has two halves.

1. **Your answer** — the three pieces from
   [part 1.1](1.1-what-you-do-when-you-are-not-working.md).
2. **The hand-back** — a question to them, or a sign that you want more from them.

The hand-back comes in three sizes, and you need all three because they do different jobs.

| Size | What you say | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small | *Really?* · *Oh, nice.* · *I didn't know that.* | to keep them going without taking the turn |
| Medium | *Do you? · Me too. · Do you play too?* | when you share the thing |
| Large | *How often do you go?* · *When did you start?* | when you want a real answer |

## Why Vaani needs it

Twelve turns in the day 40 role-play means six from each side. A speaker who only answers cannot
produce six turns; they produce six half-turns and a silence. Handing back is what turns an
interview into a conversation, and the gate counts conversation.

And this is the piece that decides whether people talk to you twice. Nobody minds a small
vocabulary. Everybody minds doing all the work.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Talking about personal interests](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/talking-about-personal-interests).
The exchange it models is exactly this shape — a thing, then a sign of interest, then a question
back:

> Well, I've recently started doing meditation.
> I do too!
> So, um, how often do you practise?

**The four questions that always work**, whatever the hobby is. Prepare these once and you never
need another:

> How often do you go?
> When did you start?
> How did you get into it?
> Is it hard?

**The frequency words, said with the right weight.** *How often* answers carry the stress on the
number word, and the small words around them go weak and fast:

```text
   I go  EVery  week.
   About  TWICE  a month.
   Most  EVenings,  when it's not too hot.
```

If every word comes out the same size, a listener hears a form being filled in rather than a
person speaking.

**The *me too* family**, which is the cheapest hand-back and the warmest:

> Me too.
> I do too.
> Same here.
> Oh, my brother does that.

None of these is a question, and all of them get you another turn from the other person.

**Softening, so *how often* does not sound like an interview.** Put a small word in front:

> So how often do you go?
> And how often is that — every week?

**What to do when you share nothing at all.** You still have a turn. Ask the beginner's question,
which fluent speakers use constantly and nobody finds strange:

> I've never tried that. Where do you even start?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the answer with no hand-back**, which the story above shows. Your
English was fine; you simply put the ball down.

The second is **the interview**:

> What do you do? … How often? … When did you start? … Do you like it?

Four questions and nothing about yourself. The other person starts answering shorter and shorter,
because they can feel that nothing is coming back.

The third is **the hand-back that takes the turn away**:

> I play cricket on Saturdays.
> Oh, cricket! My cousin plays, and last year he went to a tournament, and …

You asked a question and answered it yourself. This is the commonest way a friendly person
accidentally ends up doing all the talking.

The fourth is **the frequency word that does not fit the tense**:

> I am going to the gym every day.

For a habit — something you do again and again — English wants *I go to the gym every day*. The
*am going* version is about right now or about a plan, and the *every day* on the end makes a
listener pause.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Listen to two friends talking about their weekends and count the turns. They are short — often
under ten words — and they alternate fast, because each one hands back. Long turns happen when
somebody tells a story, and a story is invited, not taken.

Under pressure, people stop handing back. At an interview or in front of somebody senior, nerves
make answers longer and questions rarer, so the conversation becomes a performance. The repair is
small and works everywhere: end one answer in three with *what about you?*

What a native listener notices: the gap where a question should have been. They will not name it —
they will simply feel that the conversation is hard work. And what they respond to immediately is
*me too*, which is two words and buys you a whole turn.
