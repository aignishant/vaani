## One-line answer

Record the same hobby answer twice on purpose — once in a single word and once as a speech that
never hands back — then play both to a real person and ask what they would say next, because both
failures produce the same silence and only one of them feels like a mistake while you are making
it.

## The story

A neighbour asks what you do at the weekend. You have been practising, so you answer properly. And
then you keep answering.

You explain how you started. You explain the equipment. You explain what happened last year, and
the year before, and why the place you used to go closed down, and what you do instead now.

The neighbour nods in all the right places. Nobody interrupts you, because interrupting is rude.
When you finish, they say:

> That's great.

And the conversation is over — the same ending as the one-word answer got you, from the opposite
direction. The one-word answer gave them nothing to hold. The speech gave them no place to stand.

Today you make both on purpose and find out which one a real listener finds harder.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **monologue** — one person speaking for a long time with nobody else's turn in it.
- **handhold** — something in your answer the other person can take and use.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will record three versions of the same true answer about one of your own hobbies.

- **Take A — the one-word answer.** The label, and nothing else.
- **Take B — the speech.** Keep going. Do not stop. No question at the end.
- **Take C — the repaired answer.** The three pieces from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-you-do-when-you-are-not-working.md), then a hand-back from
  [part 1.2](1.2-say-how-often-and-ask-back.md).

Then a real person listens to all three and answers one question about each: **what would you say
next?**

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 role-play is judged on turns, and both of today's failures destroy turns — one by
giving the other speaker nothing, one by leaving them no room. A learner who has only ever been
told *say more* fixes the first failure by building the second.

And you cannot hear a monologue from the inside. While you are speaking it feels like fluency. It
is the one failure in this course that feels like success at the time, which is exactly why it
needs a real listener and a recording.

## The rule

**Record all three takes about the same hobby, one after another**, so nothing changes except the
shape.

**Then count two things in take B**, playing it back with a pen in your hand:

- how many sentences it has;
- how many questions it has. (This number is usually zero, and that is the finding.)

**Then run the listener test.** Play one take at a time to a real person and ask exactly this:

> What would you say next?

Write their answer down word for word before you say anything about it.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the listener cannot produce a real
next turn for take B — when what they offer is *that's nice*, *great*, or a long pause. It is
**GREEN** when take C gets a genuine question or a story back from them, and you can name the
handhold in it that they took.

To make it go red on purpose, let take B run past ten sentences without a single question in it.

**Then do the trimming.** Take your take B and write down, in one line each, the three sentences
in it that were worth keeping. Everything else was the failure. Record those three sentences as
one answer and hear how much stronger it is than the long version.

**And the repair for real life**, because you will be halfway through a monologue one day and
notice:

> — anyway, that's more than you asked. Do you do anything like that?

Cut, hand back, move on. Nobody minds. The only person who remembers the long answer is you.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version in the exercise is **a take B that is not long enough**. You feel the
silence coming and you stop at four sentences, so the listener answers easily and nothing is
learned. If your take B is comfortable, it is not take B.

The second is **a take A said warmly**, with a smile in the voice, which a kind listener rescues
by asking a question. Then you record *reading* and the test says the one-word answer works fine.
Ask your listener to answer honestly, not helpfully.

The third, in real conversations, is **the monologue that starts as a good answer**. The first
three sentences are take C. Then the fourth sentence begins with *and actually*, and you are in
take B without having decided to be.

The fourth is **the answer that ends with a question nobody can answer**:

> … so that's what I do at weekends. What about you? Do you also fix motorbike engines?

A hand-back has to be answerable by the person in front of you. *Do you do anything like that?* is
answerable by everybody.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers give long turns too — but they are invited ones. Somebody asks a second question,
or says *go on*, and only then does the story run. The invitation is the whole difference, and it
takes one sentence to check for: *do you want the long version?*

Under pressure, both failures get worse in the same person. Nervous speakers either shut down to
one word or run on without stopping, and which one happens depends on the day, not on the
language. That is worth knowing, because the fix for both is the same: three pieces, then hand
back.

What a native listener notices in take B is not the length. It is that there was no gap for them.
They will describe it later as *a bit intense*, never as *too long*. What they notice in take A is
nothing at all, which is its own kind of cost: you were there and nothing about you was learned.
