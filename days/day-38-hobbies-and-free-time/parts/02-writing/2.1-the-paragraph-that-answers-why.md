## One-line answer

A paragraph about something you enjoy needs one thing, not six, and it needs the answer to *why* —
and the honest *why* is almost never *because it is good*; it is what the thing gives you that the
rest of the day does not.

## The story

A notice goes up in a building: a small group is starting, and anybody who wants to join should
put a few lines about themselves on the sheet downstairs.

Somebody writes:

> I like reading, music, cooking, walking and gardening. All are very good hobbies. They are very
> useful for the mind and body.

Nobody who reads that sheet learns one thing about the person who wrote it. Every sentence would
be equally true if somebody else had written it.

Below it, somebody else writes:

> I garden, in pots, on a balcony that gets sun for about three hours. I started because the flat
> was on the main road and I wanted one thing in it that was not grey. There are twelve pots now
> and four of them are the same plant, because I keep splitting it and I can't throw any of it
> away.

Three sentences. One thing. A reason that is actually a reason. Anybody reading the sheet now
knows who they would be sitting next to.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **enjoy** — to like doing something while you are doing it.
- **reason** — the answer to *why*.
- **general** — true about many things, and so about nothing in particular.
- **specific** — about one thing, with the details that belong only to it.
- **evidence** — a small true fact that shows the reader you are not making it up.

A paragraph that answers *why* has four sentences' worth of work in it, and they go in this order.

| Sentence | Its job | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The spine | names the one thing, with a detail attached | *I garden, in pots, on a balcony.* |
| The reason | says what it gives you | *I started because I wanted one thing that wasn't grey.* |
| The evidence | one fact only you could write | *Twelve pots, four of them the same plant.* |
| The close | where it is now, or where it is going | *I'm trying tomatoes next, badly.* |

The reason sentence is the one this part is about. Every other sentence can be copied from a
hundred other people's paragraphs. That one cannot.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 note has to do three jobs in about a hundred words, and a writer who cannot hold one
idea for four sentences will not hold three jobs for a hundred words. This is the practice for
that.

And this shape is what a profile is — for a course, a group, a form, a job application later in
this course. Phase 5 asks for a cover letter, and a cover letter is this paragraph wearing a
collar: one thing, why, evidence, what next.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[A personal profile](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/personal-profile).
Its tips include:

> Don't include every single thing. Choose a few points and focus on them.
> Check your spelling and punctuation before you post.

and its model shows exactly the move this part teaches — a preference with a reason attached
rather than a list of labels, as in *I get bored lying on the beach*.

**How to find the honest reason.** Write *because* and then finish the sentence four times, fast,
without judging any of them:

> I walk in the evening because …
> … it's the only quiet hour.
> … the house is loud until nine.
> … I think better when I'm moving.
> … it's free.

Then keep the one that would surprise somebody who knows you a little. That is the reason with
evidence behind it.

**The three reasons that are not reasons**, because they are true of everything:

> because it is good for health
> because it is very interesting
> because I like it

*Because I like it* is a circle: you enjoy it because you enjoy it. A reader learns nothing and
stops reading.

**Where the reason sentence goes.** Second, almost always — after the reader knows what the thing
is and before they have decided whether to care.

**Joining with because and so.** *Because* puts the reason after, *so* puts the result after:

> I cook on Sundays **because** the week has no time in it.
> The week has no time in it, **so** I cook on Sundays.

Both are correct. Choose by what you want the sentence to end on, because the last thing in a
sentence is the thing a reader keeps.

**The shape on the page:**

```text
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │ SPINE    one thing + one detail             │
   │ REASON   because … (what it gives you)      │
   │ EVIDENCE one fact only you could write      │
   │ CLOSE    where it is now / what's next      │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Punctuation, which is part of the job.** A capital letter to open every sentence and a full stop
to close it. No comma where a full stop belongs:

> I garden in pots, I started because the flat was grey, there are twelve pots now.

That is three sentences joined by commas, and a reader hears one long breath with no landings. Use
full stops.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the list**:

> I like reading, music, cooking, walking and gardening.

Five labels in one line. There is no spine, so nothing can support anything, and the *why* has
nowhere to attach.

The second is **the empty reason**:

> I like gardening because gardening is a very good hobby. It is very useful for health.

Both sentences could be about anything. A reader's eye slides off writing like this without
stopping, and they could not tell you a minute later what they read.

The third is **evidence with no numbers or nouns in it**:

> I do it quite often and I enjoy it a lot.

*Quite often* and *a lot* are the two phrases that make writing vanish. *Twelve pots*, *most
Sundays*, *one book a month* — those stay.

The fourth is **the paragraph that changes subject halfway**:

> I garden in pots on the balcony. My brother has a real garden at home and he grows mangoes, and
> last year he sent us a box.

Two sentences in, the paragraph is about your brother. If the second half needs *also* or *and my
brother*, it is a different paragraph.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Anybody who reads a pile of profiles — a group organiser, an office, a landlord choosing a
tenant — reads the first line and then skims. What survives skimming is a concrete noun and a
number. *Twelve pots* survives. *Very useful for health* does not.

Under pressure, writers go general. When you are unsure whether somebody will approve of your
hobby, you reach for the safe, impressive words and end up writing the paragraph anybody could
have written. The honest small reason is always the stronger one, and it is also the easier one,
because you do not have to invent anything.

A formal reader wants the same shape with plainer words. The reason still has to be there — a
cover letter with no *why* reads as a form filled in — but *the flat was grey* becomes *I wanted
something to look after*. Same sentence, different coat.
