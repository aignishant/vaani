## One-line answer

Write the real paragraph — one thing, about eighty words, four to six sentences — and then put it
somewhere a real person will read it, because a paragraph written for nobody is an exercise and a
paragraph written for somebody is writing.

## The story

The sheet downstairs has been up for three days, and the group meets on Sunday.

You have your four sentences. They are honest, they have a reason and a number in them, and they
are sitting in a notebook where nobody will ever see them.

Writing them on the sheet changes two things. First, you check the spelling, because somebody you
will meet in the lift is going to read it. Second, you cut the sentence you were not sure about —
and it turns out that sentence was the weakest one anyway.

A paragraph with a reader gets better before it is even read. That is not a trick; it is the whole
reason this course asks you to send things.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **draft** — the first version of a piece of writing.
- **edit** — to change a draft to make it better.
- **cut** — to remove a word or a sentence.
- **audience** — the person who will read what you wrote.
- **register** — how formal or friendly the writing sounds.

The work has three passes, and they are different jobs. Doing them at the same time is why writing
feels hard.

| Pass | What you do | What you do *not* do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Draft | get all four sentences down, badly | stop to fix a word |
| Edit | cut, join, choose better nouns | add new ideas |
| Check | capitals, full stops, spelling | rewrite |

Eighty words is short. That is the point: a short paragraph has nowhere to hide a sentence that
says nothing.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 gate note is a hundred words and it is **sent**. Not drafted, not prepared — sent to a
real person who will act on it. Today is the smaller version of that, with one job instead of
three.

And a written piece is the half of this course that lasts. The recording is gone once you have
listened to it. The paragraph stays in `lab/writing/`, and in twelve weeks you will read today's
and see exactly how far your English has moved.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[A personal profile](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/personal-profile),
whose tips are the ones this part applies:

> Write about your good points, not your bad points.
> Don't include every single thing. Choose a few points and focus on them.
> Don't add your contact details (e.g. address, phone number). You could get unwanted messages.
> Check your spelling and punctuation before you post.

**Pass one — the draft.** Four sentences in the order from
[part 2.1](2.1-the-paragraph-that-answers-why.md): spine, reason, evidence, close. Write badly and
quickly. Nobody sees this pass.

**Pass two — the edit.** Three specific cuts, in this order:

1. **Cut every *very*, *really* and *a lot*.** Read the sentence again. It is stronger without
   them, every time.
2. **Cut any sentence that does not hold the spine up.** If it needs *also* to get in, it belongs
   to another paragraph.
3. **Replace one weak noun with a real one.** *Things* → *pots*. *Stuff* → *old film magazines*.

**Pass three — the check.** Read it out loud, slowly, with a finger on the page:

- a capital letter at the start of every sentence;
- a full stop, a question mark, or nothing else, at the end of every sentence;
- no comma standing where a full stop belongs;
- every name of a place or a person with a capital letter.

**Then choose a real reader and give it to them.** A message to a friend, a note to a family
member, a post in a group you are already in, or a sheet on a noticeboard. No contact details on
anything public — that is the third tip above, and it matters.

**What the finished shape looks like**, at about eighty words:

```text
   ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  spine      1 sentence   the thing + a detail          │
   │  reason     1 sentence   because …                     │
   │  evidence   1–2 sentences  numbers, nouns, one moment  │
   │  close      1 sentence   now, or next                  │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
            about 80 words · 4–6 sentences · one idea
```

**And the second paragraph, which is the harder rep.** Write the same paragraph again about
something you enjoy that you think is *not* interesting — the programme you watch, the walk to the
shop, the tea you make a particular way. The honest small thing is always writable, and this is
the rep that proves it to you.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the paragraph that grows**:

> I garden in pots. I also like cooking, and at weekends I sometimes go to my sister's house, and
> she has a big terrace, so we sit there in the evening, and …

The spine was fine until *also*. Then the paragraph turned into a week.

The second is **editing while drafting**. You write four words, you delete three, you look up a
spelling, and after twenty minutes you have one sentence you do not like. Draft first, badly, all
the way to the close.

The third is **the check that never happens**:

> i garden in pots on the balcony it gets sun for three hours

No capital, no full stop. The reader understands it and thinks less of it, and a reader who thinks
less of it answers more slowly. In this course capitals and full stops are not manners. They are
the subject.

The fourth is **writing for nobody**:

> This is my paragraph about my hobby for my English practice.

The moment a paragraph announces itself as practice, it stops being writing. Take the frame off
and give a real person the paragraph.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Real profiles are read fast and half-read. The first sentence decides whether the rest is read at
all, which is why the spine carries the detail — *in pots, on a balcony* — instead of waiting.

Under pressure people write longer, not shorter. When a paragraph matters, the temptation is to
add one more thing to be safe, and the added thing is usually the general sentence that flattens
everything around it. The fix is the cut list above, applied after the pressure, before sending.

A formal reader notices the check pass first. Spelling and capitals are the only part of your
writing they can judge in two seconds, and they do judge it — not because the content matters
less, but because it is the part that is visible before reading.
