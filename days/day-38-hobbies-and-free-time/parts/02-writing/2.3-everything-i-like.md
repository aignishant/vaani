## One-line answer

Write the list paragraph on purpose — six things you like, every reason a general one — then give
it and the real paragraph to two readers and ask them, a day later, what they remember, because
the list is not wrong in any sentence and is still the version nobody can repeat back.

## The story

The sheet downstairs comes down on Sunday and somebody reads it out at the meeting.

> This one likes reading, music, cooking, walking and gardening, and says they are all very good
> for the mind and body.

Nobody looks up. Then the next one:

> This one has twelve pots on a balcony and four of them are the same plant.

Two people laugh, and somebody says *that's the one with the tomatoes*. A week later, the second
person has a name in that building and the first does not.

Not one sentence in the first paragraph was incorrect. That is what makes this failure worth
building on purpose: correct English that leaves nothing behind.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **recall** — what somebody can remember later without looking.
- **interchangeable** — could be swapped for anything else without a reader noticing.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will write two paragraphs of about the same length about your own real life.

- **The RED paragraph.** Six things you like in one list. Every reason general: *good for health*,
  *very interesting*, *because I like it*. No numbers, no nouns anybody could picture, no single
  moment.
- **The GREEN paragraph.** The one you built in
  [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-paragraph.md): one thing, an honest reason, evidence with a number or
  a real noun in it, and a close.

Then two real readers read both, once, with no explanation from you — and you ask them a day
later what they remember.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 note is about a hundred words. A hundred words is exactly enough room to write six
general sentences that a reader forgets by the time they put the phone down, and that is the
commonest way a gate note fails: nothing in it is wrong.

And the ledger needs the row. Not *my writing is boring* — that is not a row anybody can act on —
but *six general sentences, two readers, neither could name one thing the next day*.

## The rule

**Write both paragraphs about your own real life**, about eighty words each, so length is not the
thing being tested.

**Give them to two real readers**, in a different order for each reader, and say only this:

> Read these once. I'll ask you something tomorrow.

**Then wait.** The wait is the exercise. A reader asked immediately can repeat almost anything; a
reader asked a day later repeats only what stuck.

**Then ask each of them exactly this, without showing them the paragraphs again:**

> What do you remember about me from what you read?

| Reader | What they remembered from the RED paragraph | What they remembered from the GREEN paragraph |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reader 1 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| Reader 2 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when neither reader can name one specific
thing from the list paragraph — when what comes back is *you like a lot of things* or *reading, I
think*. It is **GREEN** when both readers name something concrete from the other paragraph: a
number, a noun, the reason.

If both paragraphs are remembered equally, your RED paragraph had a real detail smuggled into it.
Take it out and run it again.

**Then do the interchangeable test**, on the page, with a pen. Go through the RED paragraph
sentence by sentence and ask of each one:

> Could my neighbour have written this exact sentence?

Mark every yes. Count them. That number is the finding, and it belongs in the ledger.

**Then write the repair**, which is not a rewrite of the whole thing. Take **one** sentence from
the RED paragraph and give it a number and a noun:

> I like cooking because it is a good hobby.
> → I cook on Sundays because nothing else in the week takes two hours.

Do that for three of the six, and notice that the paragraph is now too long — which is how you
learn that the list was never a length problem. It was six paragraphs pretending to be one.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED paragraph with one good sentence in it**.
You cannot help writing well now, and one real detail rescues the whole thing. Read it back before
you hand it over and remove anything a reader could picture.

The second is **asking the readers straight away**, which turns the test into a memory game about
the last thirty seconds. Wait a day.

The third is **explaining the paragraphs while they read**. Every word you say out loud is a
detail that is not in the writing, and the test is about the writing.

The fourth, in real writing, is **the list that grows out of fear**. A writer who is not sure the
reader will like one thing lists six, so that at least one lands. What actually happens is that
none of them lands, because a list carries no weight anywhere.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Anybody who reads many short profiles — for a group, a class, a job — says the same thing: they
remember one detail per person, if that. The writer's job is to decide which detail it will be,
and a list refuses to decide.

Under pressure, in a form with a box that says *interests*, the list is what almost everybody
writes, because a list feels safer than a choice. The people who get remembered write one thing
and a reason, in the same box, in fewer words.

A formal reader — a course, an employer — is the strictest audience for this. They read a hundred
of these, and their eye is trained to skip every general sentence. In that setting the list is not
neutral: it is invisible, which is worse than a small hobby honestly written.
