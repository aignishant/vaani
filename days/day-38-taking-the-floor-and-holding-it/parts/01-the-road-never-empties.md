# The road never empties, so you match the speed

`IN-05` · Day 38

## The one-line answer

You have been sitting at the junction waiting for a gap that is never coming, and once you are out
there, the thing that loses you the lane is your own brake lights.

## The model

Think of a busy road with no lights on it. If you sit at the junction waiting for it to be empty,
you sit there all evening. Everybody else out there got on by doing the other thing — they picked
a small gap, matched the speed of the traffic, and pulled out. Nobody let them in. They joined.

Conversation in English works the same way, and this is genuinely a difference and not a feeling.
The gap between one person finishing and the next starting is very small — often no gap at all,
with a word or two of overlap. If you are waiting for a clean silence before you speak, you are
waiting for the road to empty. It does not empty. You go home having said three things, and
afterwards you decide your English was the problem, when what actually happened is that you never
pulled out.

### Getting on

**Where the gaps actually are.** They exist, they are just short, and all three are things you can
hear rather than guess at.

- **The in-breath.** Everybody takes one, and it comes at a clause end. It is audible. That is
  your gap, and it is the commonest way in.
- **The fall.** Their pitch drops at the end of a clause. Day 26 taught you that fall — this is the
  same one, and here it means the driver in front has slowed.
- **The echo.** They say a word you can pick up. Repeat that word back with a rise, and they will
  hand you the floor without either of you noticing it happened.

**Do not lead with your point.** This is the mistake that undoes everybody. You wait, you find a
gap, and you launch straight into the substance of what you want to say — a long, careful first
sentence. But the first two words of your turn are doing a completely different job from the rest.
They are the indicator. They say *I am coming out*, and they buy the room a beat to make space for
you. If your first two words are already carrying content, that content gets driven over.

So the front of your turn is a throwaway. Something that costs nothing and means nothing:

> Can I just say — I think we're solving the wrong problem here.
> Just quickly — has anybody actually asked them?

Notice that the useful sentence starts *after* the floor is already yours. That is the whole
trick, and it is a lot less impressive than people expect, which is why so few learners try it.

### Staying on

Getting out into the traffic is the easy half. Holding the lane is where most turns are lost, and
you lose them in three ways, all of which you can hear on your own tape.

**Your pitch falls at the end of a clause.** A fall is a full stop, and a full stop is an
invitation. You did not mean to finish — you were only pausing for breath — but your voice said
*done* and somebody took it. This is the single biggest one. If you are still going, your voice
must not land.

**You pause at the end of the clause instead of inside it.** This is the fix that changes
everything and almost nobody knows it. A silence after a finished clause is an open door. A
silence in the *middle* of one is not, because an unfinished clause cannot be taken — the room
hears something dangling and waits for you. So do not pause after *and the other thing is
important.* Pause after *and the other thing is —* and then take as long as you like.

**Your first sentence lands too neatly.** You make one clean point and stop, expecting somebody to
invite the second one. Nobody does. The floor was yours for as long as you kept using it, and you
handed it back.

**Announce a shape.** A number spoken early is a contract, and English speakers honour it. *There's
two things, really* buys you both of them. The room will wait through the join to hear the second,
because you told them one was coming and they can count.

**Today's phrases** — the indicator on the front, and the moves that keep the lane. All of these
go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Can I just say — | Pulling out into a gap nobody offered you |
| Just quickly — | Promising it will be short, so they make room |
| Before I forget — | Claiming a gap for something you will lose if you wait |
| There's two things, really. | Announcing a shape, so the floor survives the join |
| … and the other thing is … | Crossing from one point to the next without releasing it |
| That's the first bit, anyway. | Closing part one out loud, so they know part two is coming |
| I'll come back to that in a sec. | Parking your own second point where nobody can take it |
| Let me finish that thought. | Somebody has started moving and you are not done |

## Say it now

Each line twice: once on its own, once with the throwaway front said fast and flat, the way you
would really say it. The front is not meant to sound considered. It is meant to sound like
somebody indicating.

1. *Can I just say — {{your actual point}}.*
2. *Just quickly — {{the short version of it}}.*
3. *Before I forget — {{the thing you would lose}}.*
4. *There's two things, really. {{One}}, and {{two}}.*
5. *{{The first point}} … and the other thing is {{the second point}}.*
6. *That's the first bit, anyway. {{The second bit}}.*
7. *I'll come back to that in a sec — {{what you want to finish first}}.*
8. *Let me finish that thought — {{the rest of it}}.*

Now the version that matters. Run lines 4, 5 and 6 again with one rule: **every pause goes inside
a clause, never at the end of one.** Break after *the other thing is —*, after *there's two
things* —, after *and* if you have to. Feel how wrong it feels, and record a couple anyway,
because the wrongness is only unfamiliarity and the tape will tell you it sounds fine.

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d38-free.m4a`.**

> Talk about something you have an actual position on, for a good stretch, with this rule: **you
> may not let your pitch land until the very end.** Every clause finishes level or slightly up.
> Every pause you need goes in the middle of a clause. Announce a shape at the start and keep it —
> if you said two things, deliver two things.

- **Solo form:** as written, into the wall. Then run it again with a second rule stacked on: three
  times during the stretch, pause in the middle of a clause for longer than is comfortable, and
  then carry on without restarting the clause.
- **With a partner:** they start talking about anything at all and do not stop or leave you a gap.
  You get in — on an in-breath, a fall, or an echo — and then hold the floor for your whole point.
  They are allowed to try to take it back, and their instruction is simple: **take it the moment
  your ear says he's finished.** Whenever they take it, that is your voice landing.

## The deliberate failure

Ask a partner to talk continuously, or play a recording of somebody speaking without a break. Your
rule: **you may only start after a genuine silence.** Not an in-breath. Not a fall. An actual gap
with nothing in it. Wait for it. Keep the recorder running the whole time.

**What breaking sounds like:** nothing. That is the finding. Play the tape back and listen to how
much of it is you saying nothing while a gap you would accept never arrives. If you are working
from a recording of real speech, you can count the silences long enough to start in on one hand,
and there will be fewer than you expect.

Then run the same stretch again and get in on the first in-breath you hear. You will feel like you
interrupted. Play the two back to back and listen to which one sounds rude — it is neither of
them. The one that sounds strange is the first, where somebody is present and saying nothing.

## When it breaks in the wild

Four people, a table, and a point you actually wanted to make. You find your gap, you get two
clauses out, and then you pause for breath at the end of a sentence — and somebody else is
talking. Your point is gone and the subject has moved on. Afterwards you tell yourself they talked
over you, and they did not. Your voice fell, the door opened, and somebody walked through it.

**The repair:**

> Let me finish that thought — there's two things and I've only done one.

Both of today's moves at once: you reclaim the lane, and you tell them there is a second part
coming so it survives your next breath.
