# When it doesn't get taken, hand it over differently

`IN-06` · Day 39

## The one-line answer

Repairing your own sentence is finished business from Day 17 — today is the other direction, where
somebody hands you something you did not catch, and you ask for it again in a way that costs you
nothing.

## The model

Two people passing things back and forth across a table. Most of the time it works and nobody
thinks about it. Sometimes you hold something out and it does not get taken. Sometimes they hold
something out and you do not catch it. Those are different problems and they need different hands.

**Your half is already done, and today does not repeat it.** Day 17 gave you the moves that fix a
wrong word without breaking stride — *or rather*, *I mean —*, *well, more like* — all of them
living inside the sentence you are already speaking. Day 1 gave you *let me start again*, Day 2
gave you *that's not quite what I meant*, and Day 6 gave you *let me rephrase that* and *what I
should have said was …*. Between them, everything about your own sentence not getting taken is
covered. Go back to Day 17 if it has gone rusty. It is not taught again here.

Today's weight is entirely on **the other direction**, and it is the one almost nobody trains.

### Why you nod instead

You did not catch it. And instead of saying so, you nod. You know exactly why: asking feels like
an announcement. *My English just failed.* So you nod, you make a small agreeable noise, and you
hope the next sentence rescues you.

It does not, and the nod is far more expensive than the ask. You have now committed to
understanding something you did not understand, and the moment that comes out — three sentences
later, when you answer the wrong thing, or an hour later when you turn up in the wrong place — is
much worse than a five-word question would have been. Fluent speakers in their own first language
ask for things again constantly. It is not a mark of anything. What actually marks somebody out is
nodding at something they did not get.

### The ask that costs face, and the ones that do not

Here is the part that is genuinely craft, rather than courage.

**Asking for the whole thing back is the expensive one.** *Sorry, what?* says *none of that
arrived*. It hands the parcel straight back unopened, and it forces the other person to guess what
went wrong — so they do the only two things anybody can do, which is say it again louder and
slower. Neither of those was the missing part.

**A precise ask costs nothing at all**, because it proves you were with them. It names what you
got, it names where you came off, and it asks for that piece only. Three shapes, and they cover
nearly everything.

**Name the cut point.** You were following, and then at one specific word you were not.

> You've lost me at *provisional*.
> Not the whole thing — from *the second option* onwards.

They restart from that word instead of from the top, which is faster for both of you and tells
them precisely what to change.

**Name the single word.** Everything landed except one thing.

> Sorry, one word — *Thursday*?
> Is that *fifteen*, or *fifty*?

That second one is the cheapest ask in English. You offer two guesses and they answer with one
word. Nothing gets repeated at all.

**Ask for different words, not the same ones.** This is the move people never think of, and it is
the most useful of the three.

> Could you put that another way?

If a sentence did not land the first time, hearing the identical sentence again usually will not
help — the words were the problem, and repeating them does not change the words. Asking for a
rephrase is the only request that actually adds new information to the table.

### Two rules that are audible

**Say sorry once.** *Sorry* on the front of a question is a hinge in English and carries no
meaning at all. A second one in the same breath — *sorry, sorry, could you* — turns a routine
question into a confession, and you can hear the difference immediately on tape.

**Ask early.** The cost of asking goes up with every sentence you let past. Ask on the sentence,
not at the end of the paragraph, because by the end you have to admit to three of them at once.

**Today's phrases** — asking somebody else for it again, precisely. All of these go into
`docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Could you put that another way? | The words themselves were the problem, so a repeat won't help |
| You've lost me at "{{word}}". | Naming the exact word you came off at |
| Not the whole thing — from "{{word}}" onwards. | Asking for a piece, not a full repeat |
| Sorry, one word — {{word}}? | Everything landed except one thing |
| Is that "{{a}}", or "{{b}}"? | You have two guesses and want them to pick |
| Can you spell that for me? | A name or a word you will need again later |
| Was that a question? | You cannot tell whether the turn is yours |
| I'm one step behind you. | Buying a repeat without apologising for yourself |

## Say it now

Each line twice. The first time as written. The second time flat and quick, with no lift on the
front — these are ordinary questions, not requests for a favour, and the moment they sound like a
favour they start costing you something.

1. *Could you put that another way? {{the bit you did get}} I've got.*
2. *You've lost me at {{word}}.*
3. *Not the whole thing — from {{word}} onwards.*
4. *Sorry, one word — {{word}}?*
5. *Is that {{a}}, or {{b}}?*
6. *Can you spell that for me? I'll need it {{later thing}}.*
7. *Was that a question, or {{the other thing it might have been}}?*
8. *I'm one step behind you — {{where you actually are}}.*

Now run all eight again with one rule: **the word *sorry* may appear once across the whole set.**
Pick which line gets it. Everywhere else, ask the question with nothing on the front. Say them one
after another with no gaps. Stripping the padding is uncomfortable and it is the entire point —
padding is what makes an ordinary question sound like an admission.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d39-free.m4a`.**

> Play some talk that is genuinely too fast or too dense for you — not comfortable listening,
> something you have to work at. Every single time you lose the thread, **stop it and say the
> precise ask out loud into the recorder, naming the exact word you came off at.** Then rewind to
> that word only, and carry on. Do that at least four times.

- **Solo form:** as written. The recording cannot answer you, and that does not matter — the ask
  is the rep, and rewinding to the word you named is how you find out whether you named the right
  one. If you rewind and the word is not there, your cut point was a guess.
- **With a partner:** they read something aloud at their normal speed, with one instruction — **if
  you ask, they may not repeat the same words.** They have to say it another way or answer the
  specific question. You will run out of blunt asks within a few lines, which is the lesson.

## The deliberate failure

Have a partner say one dense sentence, or use one from your recording. Ask for the whole thing
back three times in a row — *sorry, what?*, then *say that again?*, then *sorry?* — and record all
three passes.

**What breaking sounds like:** listen to what happens to the sentence across the three attempts.
It does not get clearer. It gets **louder and slower and simpler**, and by the third pass the
other person is talking to you in a way you would not want anybody to hear. That is what the blunt
ask actually buys — not information, just volume and a change in how you are being spoken to.

Working alone, do it to yourself: say one hard sentence into the recorder three times, each one
louder and slower than the last, then play all three back. The finding is the uncomfortable one.
**The second and third passes contain no new information whatsoever.** There was nothing else for
a repeat to give you. Which means every time you have asked for the whole thing again and it did
not help, the problem was never that you failed to listen properly — you asked for the one thing
that could not work.

Then ask for the same sentence a fourth time using *could you put that another way?* Different
words arrive, and one of them is the one you needed.

## When it breaks in the wild

Somebody gives you three pieces of information in one breath — where, when, and who to ask for.
You catch two. Saying *sorry, what?* would mean losing all three and starting again, so you say
thanks instead and work it out later. You do not work it out later. You turn up in the right place
on the wrong day and it costs you the whole afternoon.

**The repair:**

> Sorry, one word — was that Thursday? I've got the place and the name.

Nobody repeats anything. You have shown them exactly what landed, they supply the one missing
piece, and the whole exchange is shorter than the nod would have been.

with denser material. Re-recording is normal. It is not a failed day.

Asking well is not a smaller version of speaking well. It is its own thing, and it is the reason
some people can hold a conversation in a language they only half have — they never spend a whole
turn pretending. Every ask above is four or five words long. That is the entire price.
