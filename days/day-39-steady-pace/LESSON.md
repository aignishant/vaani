## §1 Where we are

You are on the phone giving your own address to somebody who has never heard it, and it comes out
as one long word. The speaking folder is about chunks and the tiny gaps between them, about saying
one real sentence three ways to find the pace you actually want, and about what a listener loses
when a sentence outruns your mouth.

A note is left on the kitchen table for whoever gets home first. The writing folder is about the
four words that join two facts — *and*, *but*, *because*, *so* — about joining half your sentences
and leaving the other half short, and about the one-sentence note that is understood and not acted
on.

Choose either folder first. Each stands on its own. Follow the parts in that folder's order.

## §5 The check that must be able to fail

**Speaking:** In [part 1.3](parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-sentence-that-outran-your-mouth.md), play the
fast take and the steady take once each to a real person and have them write down what they heard.
GREEN is when the steady take delivers all five items — the number, the day, the name, the
negative and the last three words. RED is when the fast take loses at least two of them. To make
it go red on purpose, build a sentence of about twenty-five words with a negative in the middle and
say it in one breath.

**Writing:** In [part 2.3](parts/02-writing/2.3-the-sentence-that-never-stopped.md), give the
run-on note and the repaired note to two different readers and ask each what they have to do.
GREEN is when the repaired note's reader names both instructions unprompted. RED is when the
run-on note's reader misses one — usually the one in the middle of the chain.

Only record what you actually heard or saw. A deliberate exercise is labelled deliberate.

## §7 Traps

- The whole sentence said as one lump, with no gap anywhere in it.
- A gap in the middle of a chunk, which is harder to follow than no gap at all.
- One word at a time, which is what "slow down" produces and nobody can follow.
- The last two words swallowed, where English keeps the meaning.
- A number said at the same speed as the words around it.
- Getting louder instead of longer when somebody misses a word.
- Rehearsing a sentence eleven times and recording take eleven.
- *Because … so …* in one sentence — one join, not two.
- A missing comma before *but* and *so* joining two full sentences.
- *But* where nothing actually turns.
- A comma standing where a full stop belongs.
- Six facts joined by *and*, with the instruction in the middle.
