## One-line answer

Clear speech is not slow speech — it is speech with the pauses in the right places, because
English is heard in small groups of words, and a listener needs the tiny gap between the groups
more than they need you to go slowly.

## The story

You are on the phone to a helpline, giving your address so something can be delivered.

You know the address. It is your own address. So you say it the way you say it to yourself:

> flatfourteenbwestblocknearthewatertankgroundfloor

The person on the other end says *sorry?* Now you are annoyed, so you say it again — the same
words, in the same single lump, only louder.

Then you try it in pieces, with a breath between them:

> Flat fourteen B … West block … near the water tank … ground floor.

They get it the first time, and they read it back to you correctly.

You did not slow down very much. You broke a lump into four pieces, and the listener needed the
gaps to put them anywhere.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **pace** — how fast or slow somebody speaks.
- **chunk** — a small group of words that belong together and are said as one piece.
- **pause** — a short silence between chunks.
- **syllable** — one beat of a word: *wa-ter* has two.
- **swallow a word** — to say it so short and quiet that the listener never hears it.

Two things make speech easy to follow, and only one of them is speed.

1. **The chunks.** English comes in groups of two to six words. *In the morning* is one piece.
   *I'll call you back* is one piece. A listener hears a chunk, understands it, and is ready for
   the next one.
2. **The gaps.** A tiny silence between chunks — far shorter than a full stop — is what tells the
   listener that one piece has ended.

The problem beginners have is almost never speed. It is that the whole sentence arrives as one
piece with no gaps in it, so the listener has nowhere to put anything down.

**Where the gaps go.** Put a gap where the meaning turns a corner:

```text
   In the morning │ I take the bus │ because the train is full.
   Flat fourteen B │ West block │ near the water tank.
   I called him yesterday │ but he didn't answer.
```

The vertical line is not a full stop. It is about the length of a blink.

**The four places a gap almost always belongs:**

| Place | Example |
| --- | --- |
| before *and*, *but*, *because*, *so* | *I waited │ but nobody came.* |
| after a time or place at the front | *On Sunday │ we go to my mother's.* |
| between items in a list | *rice │ dal │ two onions │ and oil* |
| before the important word | *It costs │ four hundred.* |

**The one piece that never gets a gap inside it**: a number, a name or an address unit. *Fourteen
B* is one chunk. *Four hundred and twenty* is one chunk. Break those and the listener has to
rebuild them.

**Length, not loudness.** When a listener misses something, the repair is to make the important
word **longer**, not louder:

> It costs four HUN-dred.

Loud makes a listener defensive. Long makes them accurate.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the lump**:

> ImcallingaboutthedeliveryfromyesterdaywhichdidnotarriveandIwanttoknowwhen

Every word is correct English. There is not one gap in it, so a listener has to hold the whole
thing and take it apart afterwards, and by the time they have, you have started the next sentence.

The second is **the pause in the wrong place**, which is worse than no pause at all:

> I called │ him yesterday because the │ shop was closed.

The gaps have cut two chunks in half. A listener works harder here than with the lump, because
the pieces they are handed do not mean anything on their own.

The third is **one word at a time**, which is what happens when somebody is told to slow down:

> I … called … him … yesterday.

Now every word is a chunk, which is the same as no chunks. This sounds like a machine and is
strangely hard to follow, because the listener cannot tell which words belong together.

The fourth is **the swallowed ending**. The sentence starts strong and the last two words vanish:

> I'll bring it on Tuesday morn—

English puts a lot of meaning at the end of a sentence. If your voice runs out of air before the
end, the listener loses the part that mattered.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers are often fast, and they are still easy to follow, because the gaps survive even
at speed. Listen to a recorded conversation and you can hear the chunk edges clearly even when the
words themselves are half-swallowed — *probably* said as *probly*, *comfortable* as *comfterble*.
Speed eats syllables. It does not eat the gaps.

Under pressure the gaps are the first thing to go. On the phone, in front of an official, or when
you are worried about being a nuisance, everybody speeds up and joins everything together. The
repair is not *calm down*; it is one practised habit: **breathe at the comma**.

What a native listener notices: a speaker who runs out of air. They will not hear it as a language
problem, they will hear it as nervousness, and they will start finishing your sentences — which is
the most uncomfortable thing that can happen in a conversation you are trying to practise.
