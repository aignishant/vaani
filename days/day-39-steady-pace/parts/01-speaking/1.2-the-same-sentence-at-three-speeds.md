## One-line answer

Say one real sentence three times — too slow, too fast, and steady — and keep the version where
the gaps are still there, because you cannot choose a pace you have never heard yourself use.

## The story

A recorded message on a helpline says the office hours twice: once at the start of the call and
once while you are waiting.

Both times it is the same voice and the same words, and the second time is faster, because it is
the version they play to people who have already waited. You understand both.

Then a person comes on the line and says the same thing, quickly, in one breath, and you miss it.

The recording was faster than the person. It was still easier, because the recording had the gaps
in it and the person did not.

That is worth hearing once in your own voice, which is what this part is for.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **take** — one recording of one attempt.
- **steady** — even, not rushing and not dragging.
- **rate** — how many words go past in a stretch of speech.
- **drag** — to speak so slowly that the sentence falls apart.

You will say the same sentence three ways and keep all three, because the middle one only makes
sense next to the other two.

| Take | What you do | What it teaches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow | one word at a time, dragging | that slow is not clear |
| Fast | everything in one breath, no gaps | that fast is not the problem — missing gaps are |
| Steady | ordinary speed, gaps kept | the pace you actually want |

Most learners have only ever tried the first two. The third is not between them; it is a different
thing, and it is closer to the fast take than to the slow one.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 role-play has twelve turns and two voices, both yours. Two voices means you have to
make them different, and the easiest honest difference between two speakers is pace and chunking —
not an accent you do not have.

And a recording is the only way to check this at all. You cannot hear your own gaps from the
inside, because your head knows where the chunks are before your mouth gets there.

## The rule

**The method comes from a page opened and checked on 2026-09-23:**
[How can I improve my English pronunciation?](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/level/improve-your-english-level/how-can-i-improve-my-english-pronunciation),
which says:

> Prepare a few sentences and record yourself saying them, using your phone. Then listen to the
> recording.

and, about listening back:

> Many people dislike listening to their own voice! But if you can get past this reaction, it's a
> good way to identify things to improve.

**Choose real sentences, not practice sentences.** Things you actually have to say: an address,
what you want at a counter, what is wrong with something, when you can come.

**Mark the chunks before you speak.** On paper, with a pencil line, using the four places from
[part 1.1](1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md):

```text
   I'd like to collect a parcel │ it came on Tuesday │ but nobody was at home.
```

**Take one — slow.** Drag it. Say every syllable of every word, with a gap after each one. Listen
back and notice that you cannot tell which words belong together.

**Take two — fast.** One breath, no gaps, as fast as you can. Listen back and find the exact word
where it stopped being followable.

**Take three — steady.** Ordinary speed, gaps at your pencil lines, and the last word of the
sentence as clear as the first.

**Then the test that matters**: play take three to somebody who has not seen the sentence and ask
them to write it down. What they write is the truth about your pace. What you hear is not.

**A rule for the end of the sentence**, because this is where pace goes wrong even in a good take:
take a breath *before* you start, not in the middle. A sentence starts to rush when a speaker can
feel they are running out of air, and the rush always lands on the last three words, which are
usually the important ones.

**And one for conversation, not reading:** the gaps are also where the other person shows you they
have understood. A speaker who never pauses gets no nods, no *mm*, no *right* — and those small
signs are how you know you are being followed.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a steady take that is really a slow take**. Told to speak
clearly, learners drop the rate and keep the gaps out, and produce a sentence that is slow *and*
lumpy — the worst of both. If your take three is much longer than your take two, check that what
you added was silence between chunks, not length inside words.

The second is **rehearsing until it is smooth**. You say the sentence eleven times, and take
eleven is beautiful and useless, because in real life you get one attempt at a sentence you have
never said before. Record early takes, not polished ones.

The third is **the reading voice**. A sentence read off a page comes out flat and evenly spaced,
with the gaps in the wrong places, because your eyes are moving in a different rhythm from your
mouth. Look at the marked sentence, then look up and say it.

The fourth is **choosing a sentence you will never need**:

> The weather is very beautiful in my country in the month of December.

Nothing is learned, because you will never say it under pressure. Use the sentence you will
actually have to say at a counter this week.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers change pace constantly inside one conversation. They slow down for a number, an
unfamiliar name, or a price, and speed up for the parts the listener can predict. That is the
skill this part is pointing at: not one pace, but a fast one with the important pieces given room.

Under pressure people speed up and lose the gaps first, then lose the ends of sentences, then
start again from the beginning when the listener asks. The repair is the breath before the first
word, and it is the only thing that reliably works when you are nervous.

What a native listener notices: whether the number came out slower than the rest. A speaker who
says a phone number at the same pace as the sentence around it will be asked for it again, every
time, in every country.
