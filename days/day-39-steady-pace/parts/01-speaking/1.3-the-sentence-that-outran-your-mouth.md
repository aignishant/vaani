## One-line answer

Record a long true sentence as fast as you can, on purpose, then ask a real person to write down
what they heard — because the words you lose at speed are not random, and until you see the list
you will keep trying to fix this by slowing everything down.

## The story

Somebody calls to ask what happened with an order, and you have the whole answer ready in your
head.

It comes out as one long run: what you ordered, when it was supposed to arrive, who you spoke to,
what they said, and what you want now — no breath, no gaps, because stopping feels like giving
them a chance to interrupt.

They say:

> Sorry, from the beginning?

And you say all of it again, at the same speed, because the only version you have is the fast one.

The interesting part is what they got the first time. Not nothing — they got the beginning and the
end. What vanished was the middle, and the small words, and the day. Those are the same things
that vanish for everybody, which means they can be practised.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **dictation** — writing down exactly what somebody says.
- **drop a word** — to lose it completely, so the listener never hears it.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will make two takes of the same long, true sentence — about twenty-five words, with a number,
a day and a name in it.

- **The RED take.** As fast as you can, one breath, no gaps. Do not help the listener.
- **The GREEN take.** The same sentence, ordinary speed, with the chunks from
  [part 1.1](1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md) and the breath before the first word from
  [part 1.2](1.2-the-same-sentence-at-three-speeds.md).

Then a real person writes down what they heard from each take, without seeing the sentence, and
you compare their two pages with yours word by word.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 gate is a recording judged by whether a stranger can follow it. A stranger is exactly
the person in this exercise: somebody with no idea what you are about to say, who cannot repair
your sentence from context because they do not have the context.

And the ledger row is only worth writing if it is specific. *I speak too fast* helps nobody.
*Under speed I lose the day of the week, the word "not", and the last two words of the sentence*
is a row you can practise against for weeks.

## The rule

**Build the sentence so it can fail.** About twenty-five words, one clause joined to another, and
these four things inside it: a **number**, a **day**, a **name**, and the word **not** or a
negative contraction. Something true, from this week.

**Record the RED take first.** If you record the good one first you will not be able to make
yourself rush properly.

**Then the dictation.** Play each take once — once only — to a real person with a pen, and say:

> Write down exactly what you heard. Leave a gap where you missed something.

**Then fill this in from their two pages:**

| What was in my sentence | Did they get it from the RED take? | From the GREEN take? |

| The number | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| The day | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| The name | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| The negative (*not*, *didn't*) | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| The last three words | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the fast take loses at least two of
those five — and the negative and the last three words are the two that usually go. It is **GREEN**
when the steady take delivers all five to a listener who hears it once.

If the RED take loses nothing, it was not fast enough or the sentence was too short. Add a clause
and run it again.

**Then the repair drill.** Take the five items above. For each one your listener missed, record
three short sentences of your own that contain that kind of item, said steadily, with the item
given its own chunk:

> I'll come on Thursday │ not Tuesday.
> It's four hundred │ and twenty.

**And the real-life repair, for when you hear yourself running.** Do not start again from the
beginning. Stop, breathe, and give the missing piece alone:

> Sorry — Thursday. Not Tuesday.

Starting again from the beginning is what makes a fast speaker exhausting. Handing over the one
lost piece takes four words.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take with gaps in it anyway**. Your mouth
has been practising chunks all day and it will not stop. Read the sentence off the page with your
eyes running ahead — that produces a real rush.

The second is **letting the listener hear it twice**. Once. The whole finding lives in the first
hearing.

The third is **arguing with the dictation**:

> No, I definitely said Thursday.

You probably did say it. They did not hear it. Those are two different facts and only one of them
matters for this check.

The fourth, in real conversation, is **the speed that comes back as soon as the topic matters**. A
learner who speaks steadily about the weather will run every word together when describing
something that went wrong, because that is when they most want to get it all out. That is the
moment this drill exists for.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fast fluent speech drops sounds everywhere and is still understood, because the listener has the
chunks and the stresses to rebuild it with. A learner rushing has usually flattened the stress as
well, so there is nothing left to rebuild from. That is why the same speed is fine from one
speaker and impossible from another.

Under pressure — a complaint, a bad line, somebody who is already impatient — the pieces most
likely to be dropped are the ones that carry the whole meaning: the negative and the date. A
listener who misses *not* does not hear a gap. They hear the opposite of what you said, and they
act on it.

What a native listener notices: they will repeat your sentence back with the date raised as a
question. *So, Tuesday?* That question is not rudeness. It is somebody protecting themselves from
your pace, and it is a gift, because it tells you exactly which piece went missing.

## Check yourself

  person, once each, and fill the table from their two pages. Mark this part RED or GREEN by the
