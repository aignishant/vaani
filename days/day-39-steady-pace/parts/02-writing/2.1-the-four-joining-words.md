## One-line answer

*And*, *but*, *because* and *so* each join two sentences in a different direction — adding,
turning, explaining, resulting — and choosing the wrong one does not break the grammar, it tells
the reader the wrong thing about how your two facts are related.

## The story

A note is left on the kitchen table for whoever comes home first.

> I went to the market. There was no milk. I took the small bag. The gate was open.

Four true sentences, and the reader stands there holding them like four loose keys. Which one
explains which? Did the gate being open matter?

Now the same facts, joined:

> I went to the market **because** there was no milk. I took the small bag, **so** don't look for
> the big one. The gate was open **but** I locked it.

The same four facts, and now the note tells you what to do and what not to worry about. Nothing
was added except four small words.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **join** — to put two sentences together into one.
- **conjunction** — a word whose job is joining: *and*, *but*, *because*, *so*.
- **clause** — a piece of a sentence with its own subject and verb: *I went to the market*.
- **result** — what happened because of something else.
- **contrast** — a difference between two things.

Four words, four directions.

| Word | What it says | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| and | there is more, in the same direction | *I washed the plates and I put the rice on.* |
| but | the second part turns against the first | *I called him but he didn't answer.* |
| because | the second part is the reason for the first | *I'm late because the bus didn't come.* |
| so | the second part is the result of the first | *The bus didn't come, so I'm late.* |

Look at the last two rows. They carry the same two facts in opposite orders. That is the whole
difference between *because* and *so*, and it is the one most worth learning, because it lets you
choose what your sentence ends on.

## Why Vaani needs it

The day 40 note has to do three jobs in about a hundred words — ask, thank, and give a direction.
Three jobs in a hundred words means joined sentences. A note of nine short unjoined sentences
reads as a list of demands, and the same note joined reads as a person writing to another person.

And these four words are what turn a paragraph from a pile into a piece of writing. Every longer
form in this course — the story, the argument, the cover letter — is built out of the joins you
learn here.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Conjunctions: and, or, but, so, because and although](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/conjunctions-or-so-because-although).
It gives each word a job and one example:

> **and** — for adding information
> **but** — for unexpected or different information: "She'd like to go but she can't."
> **because** — for giving reasons: "I'd like to see that Irish band, because Celtic music is
> pretty cool."
> **so** — for talking about results or purposes: "It's one of those cars for seven people, so
> there's plenty of room."

**The comma rule, which is part of the subject.**

Before *but* and *so*, joining two full sentences, English puts a comma:

> I waited an hour, but nobody came.
> The shop was closed, so I went home.

Before *because*, no comma, when the reason simply follows:

> I went home because the shop was closed.

Before *and*, joining two short full sentences, the comma is optional and usually left out when
they are short:

> I locked the gate and I took the key.

**Where *because* can also start the sentence.** It can, and then the comma comes in the middle:

> Because the shop was closed, I went home.

This puts the reason first and the fact second. Use it when the reason is the news.

**What each join does to the end of the sentence**, which is where a reader's attention lands:

```text
   I'm late  because the bus didn't come.     ← ends on the reason
   The bus didn't come,  so I'm late.         ← ends on the result

   I wanted to come  but I couldn't.          ← ends on the refusal
   I couldn't come,  but I wanted to.         ← ends on the wish
```

Same facts. Different last word. Readers keep the last thing they read.

**One join per sentence, to begin with.** Two joins in one sentence is allowed and it is where
run-on sentences come from, so it belongs in
[part 2.3](2.3-the-sentence-that-never-stopped.md), not here.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***because* and *so* in the same sentence**:

> Because the bus didn't come, so I was late.

One join, not two. English takes either *because* at the front or *so* in the middle, and using
both is the single most common joining error made by beginners whose first language allows the
pair.

The second is **the missing comma before *but* and *so***:

> I waited an hour but nobody came.

Understood, and it reads as a hurried note. In anything formal, the comma is what tells the reader
a second full sentence has started.

The third is **and doing every job**:

> I went to the market and there was no milk and I took the small bag and the gate was open.

*And* is direction-free, so a reader gets no help at all. Four facts joined by *and* are four
facts in a row wearing a string.

The fourth is ***but* where nothing turns**:

> I went to the market but I bought rice.

Buying rice at a market is not a turn against going there, so the reader waits for a contrast that
never arrives. *But* promises a surprise; if you do not deliver one, the sentence feels broken.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers pick the join by what the sentence should end on, not by grammar. That is why the
same two facts appear as *because* in a complaint — *the parcel is late because nobody was at the
desk* — and as *so* in a request — *nobody was at the desk, so could you send it again?* The
second one ends on what you want.

Under pressure, everybody's writing collapses towards *and*. A message typed in a hurry joins
everything with *and* and leaves the reader to work out the relationships, which is the real cost:
they get the facts and have to guess the meaning.

A formal reader, and any reader of a complaint or a request, reads the last clause hardest. In an
email that asks for something, ending on the reason instead of the request is the commonest way a
polite note gets no action.
