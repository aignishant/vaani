## One-line answer

Take a real note you would have written as five short sentences, join about half of them, and
leave the other half short — because a page where everything is joined is as hard to read as a
page where nothing is.

## The story

The note on the kitchen table gets written a second time, properly, because it is going to be read
by somebody in a hurry.

The first attempt joins everything:

> I went to the market because there was no milk and I took the small bag so don't look for the
> big one but the gate was open and I locked it because the latch is loose.

One sentence. Thirty-six words. Every join correct, and the reader has to hold all of it at once.

The second attempt joins half:

> I went to the market because there was no milk. I took the small bag, so don't look for the big
> one. The gate was open. I locked it — the latch is loose.

Four sentences. Two joins. The short sentences now do work: *The gate was open* lands, because it
is standing on its own.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **short sentence** — one clause, one full stop.
- **joined sentence** — two clauses held together by *and*, *but*, *because* or *so*.
- **rhythm** — the pattern a reader feels from long and short sentences together.
- **emphasis** — the weight a sentence carries. A short sentence after two long ones is heavy.

Joining is not the goal. Choosing is the goal.

| Keep it short when | Join when |
| --- | --- |
| the fact is the most important one | two facts belong to each other |
| it is an instruction | one fact explains the other |
| you want the reader to stop on it | the two are so small that two full stops feel fussy |
| it comes after two long sentences | a list of facts is leaving the reader to guess the link |

A useful shape for a short note: **join, join, short.** The short one at the end is the one the
reader remembers.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate note tomorrow is about a hundred words doing three jobs. A hundred words is four to seven
sentences. If they are all joined it reads as one breath; if none are, it reads as a form. The
gate is passed by a note somebody can act on, and that is decided here.

And this is the first day of the course where you choose sentence length deliberately. Every
longer piece of writing later — a story, an argument, a letter — is that same choice repeated.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Conjunctions: and, or, but, so, because and although](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/conjunctions-or-so-because-although),
which gives *and* for adding, *but* for unexpected or different information, *because* for giving
reasons and *so* for results, with examples including:

> She'd like to go but she can't.
> It's one of those cars for seven people, so there's plenty of room.

**Step one — write it all short.** Every fact its own sentence, capital letter, full stop. It will
look childish. That is a draft, not a note.

> There was no milk. I went to the market. I took the small bag. The gate was open. I locked it.
> The latch is loose.

**Step two — mark the pairs that belong to each other.** Two facts belong together when one of them
answers *why?*, *and what happened?* or *so what?* about the other. Draw a line between those.

**Step three — join the marked pairs only**, choosing the word by direction:

```text
   There was no milk ──┐
                        because     →  I went to the market because there was no milk.
   I went to the market ┘

   I took the small bag ──┐
                           so       →  I took the small bag, so don't look for the big one.
   don't look for the big ┘
```

**Step four — read it for rhythm.** Out loud. If two joined sentences sit next to each other and
both are long, break one back into two. If four short sentences sit in a row, join one pair.

**Step five — check the commas.** A comma before *but* and *so*. No comma before *because* in the
middle. A comma after a *because* clause that starts the sentence.

**The dash and the full stop as alternatives to joining**, because you now have a choice:

> I locked it — the latch is loose.
> I locked it. The latch is loose.
> I locked it because the latch is loose.

All three are correct. The first is friendly, the second is firm, the third is explanatory. Choose
by what the note is for.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **everything joined**, the first attempt in the story above. It is
not ungrammatical; it is unreadable in a hurry, which for a note is the same thing.

The second is **joining facts that have no relationship**:

> There was no milk, so the gate was open.

*So* promises a result. A reader tries to work out how the missing milk opened the gate, and stops
trusting the rest of the note.

The third is **the comma splice**, which is two full sentences joined with only a comma:

> I went to the market, there was no milk.

This needs a full stop, or a joining word. It is the most common punctuation error in short notes,
and it is invisible to the writer because the two halves are so obviously related in their head.

The fourth is **the long sentence that loses its subject**:

> I went to the market because there was no milk and took the small bag and was open so I locked
> it.

Somewhere in there, *the gate* fell out. Long joined sentences swallow subjects, which is why
half-joined writing is safer than fully joined writing.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Read any short note written by somebody fluent and count the sentence lengths. They vary — twelve
words, then four, then nine. That variety is not decoration. The short ones are where the
instructions are.

Under pressure people join more, because joining feels faster than stopping. A message written
while walking is one long chain, and it is usually answered with a question, which costs more time
than the full stops would have.

A formal reader gets the opposite treatment from most learners: they write shorter and stiffer for
an office and longer and looser for a friend. It is the wrong way round. An office wants one
instruction per sentence, and a friend does not mind a chain.
