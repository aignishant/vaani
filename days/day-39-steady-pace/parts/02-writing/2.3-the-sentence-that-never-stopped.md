## One-line answer

Write the note as one sentence with six *and*s in it, on purpose, and ask a real reader to do what
it says — because a run-on note is understood and not acted on, and the gap between those two
things is invisible until somebody has to act.

## The story

Two notes go to the same person about the same evening.

The first:

> I'm going to be late and the keys are with the neighbour and if you get there before me you can
> wait upstairs and there's rice in the fridge and don't let the cat out and the gate latch is
> loose so push it twice.

The second:

> I'll be late. The keys are with the neighbour. There's rice in the fridge. Two things: don't let
> the cat out, and push the gate latch twice — it's loose.

The reader of the first note understood every word, and forgot the cat.

That is the whole cost of a run-on. Not confusion — understanding without action. It reads
smoothly, nothing sticks, and the one instruction that mattered goes past in the middle of a
chain with five other things.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **run-on** — a sentence that keeps going where it should have stopped.
- **comma splice** — two full sentences joined by only a comma.
- **act on** — to do what a note says.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will write two notes about the same real evening, each with the **same six facts**, two of
which are instructions.

- **The RED note.** One sentence. Joined with *and* and *so* throughout, no full stops until the
  end. Every word correct.
- **The GREEN note.** The same six facts, with the joins from
  [part 2.2](2.2-join-your-own-sentences.md): about half joined, the instructions standing on
  their own, and the most important one last.

Then two real readers each get one note and are asked to do one thing: **tell you what they have
to do.**

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate note tomorrow asks for something and gives a direction. Both of those are actions, and
both live or die on whether the reader can find them. A note that is enjoyable to read and
produces no action fails a gate that is judged by what the reader does with it.

And the ledger row here is unusually useful, because it is not about grammar: *six facts, one
sentence, reader missed instruction number four*.

## The rule

**Write both notes about a real evening**, with six real facts and two real instructions in them.
Same facts, same order, so only the joining changes.

**Make the RED note properly run-on.** At least five joining words in one sentence, no full stop
in the middle. Put one of the two instructions in the middle of the chain, where it is most
likely to be lost.

**Then run the reader test.** Give each note to a different real person, let them read it once,
take it back, and ask:

> What do you have to do?

Write down exactly what they say, and what they leave out.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader of the run-on note misses
at least one of the two instructions, or hands it back saying *that's fine* without naming both.
It is **GREEN** when the reader of the other note names both instructions with nothing prompting
them.

If the run-on reader gets both, your chain was too short or the instructions were at the ends.
Rewrite it with an instruction in the middle and run it again.

**Then do the splice hunt.** Go through the last five real messages you have sent anybody and find
every place where a comma is standing where a full stop belongs:

> I went to the market, there was no milk.

Count them. Write the number down. That is the habit this part is actually about, and it lives in
your phone, not in this exercise.

**Then write the repair rule you will use from now on**, in your own words, and keep it short
enough to remember at a bus stop. Something like: *one instruction, one sentence.*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED note that is only long, not run-on**. Long
sentences with full stops between them do not produce the failure. The failure needs the chain.

The second is **explaining the note while they read it**. Anything you say out loud is not in the
writing, and the test is about the writing.

The third is **the same reader for both notes**. The second note will always win, because they
already know the evening. Two different readers, one note each.

The fourth, in real writing, is **the run-on that arrives when something is urgent**. Everybody
writes one sentence when they are in a hurry, and the hurry is exactly when the instructions
matter most. The habit that protects you is mechanical and small: instructions get their own
sentence, always, even when you are running.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Run-on sentences are normal in speech and cost nothing there, because the voice adds the stops.
On the page there is no voice, so the full stop is the only thing doing that job. That is why a
message that would have been perfectly clear spoken aloud gets misread when typed.

Under pressure, readers skim. They take the first clause and the last clause and trust that the
middle is more of the same. A run-on puts your most important instruction exactly where a skimming
reader does not look.

A formal reader — an office, a landlord, a supplier — does something worse than misreading a
run-on: they answer only the last part of it. If you have ever sent a message with three questions
and had one answered, it was probably one sentence long.
