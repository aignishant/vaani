## One-line answer

The phase 2 speaking gate is a recorded shop or café role-play, both voices yours, of at least
twelve turns — and this part teaches nothing new, because a gate that teaches is not a gate.

## The story

A driving test does not begin with a lesson. Somebody sits beside you, you drive the car you have
been practising in, and at the end they tell you what happened.

That is today. Nineteen days of this folder have taught the pieces: ordering, asking the price,
asking directions, the phone, apologising, the repair when you miss something, the pace that keeps
a listener with you. None of it is repeated here, and none of it is made easier here.

What this part does is set the car down at the start of the road: what the gate says, what counts
as a turn, and how to set the thing up so that when you press record there is nothing left to
decide.

**This is a rehearsal, and it says so.** This day was written before your error ledger had rows in
it. Until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked, the checks below are the
general ones rather than your own recurring mistakes, and the gate is not yet a gate.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate** — the check at the end of a phase, which the whole phase was for.
- **role-play** — acting a real situation, with somebody playing each side.
- **turn** — one person's go at speaking, before the other person speaks.
- **rehearsal** — a practice of the real thing, done as if it were the real thing.

The gate wording is in the plan's §7 and it is copied here without a word changed:

> Speaking: a recorded shop or café role-play, both voices yours, at least twelve turns.

Three conditions, and all three are countable.

| Condition | What it means | How it is checked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a shop or café | a real everyday transaction, not a chat | the situation is recognisable from the first turn |
| both voices yours | you play the customer and the person behind the counter | one recording, one speaker, two voices |
| at least twelve turns | twelve goes at speaking, counted across both sides | counted from the recording, not estimated |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the point of the phase. Twenty days of shops, phones, plans and notes exist so that one
ordinary transaction can be carried from start to finish without stopping — and until it is
recorded, nobody knows whether it can be.

And the gate feeds what comes after it. Phase 3 is about telling what happened: the past, stories,
things that went wrong. A story is longer than a transaction and assumes you can already hold a
conversation that does not collapse.

## The rule

There is no new rule today. What follows is the setup, so nothing has to be decided while the
recorder is running.

**Choose the situation before anything else**, and choose one you have actually stood in:

- buying something that needs a size, a quantity or a price agreed;
- ordering in a café where something is not available;
- collecting something that is not ready;
- returning something that is wrong.

The last three are better than the first, because they contain a problem, and a problem produces
turns.

**A page was opened and checked on 2026-09-23** for what ordinary café turns sound like:
[Ordering in a café](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/ordering-cafe).
It carries audio of people ordering, with turns as short as these:

> Regular or large?
> I'll have a large, then, please. Without ice.
> Can I help anyone?
> What teas do you have?

Notice how short a real turn is. Twelve turns is not a speech. It is about two minutes of ordinary
back-and-forth, and most of the lines are under ten words.

**How to count a turn.** One turn is one person speaking once, however short:

```text
   1  Customer   Hi — I'm collecting a shirt, name of Rao.
   2  Shopkeeper Rao … let me check. Was it Tuesday?
   3  Customer   Tuesday, yes. They said after five.
   4  Shopkeeper It's not back yet.
   5  Customer   Sorry — not back?
   6  Shopkeeper Not back from the tailor. Tomorrow morning.
```

Six turns, and nothing has happened yet except a problem appearing. Twelve is reachable.

**Two voices, one speaker.** You do not need an accent. Make the two sides different in the ways
you already control: pace, loudness, length of turn, and where each one pauses. The person behind
the counter is usually faster and shorter. The customer is usually slower and asks more.

**What must be in the recording**, because these are what the phase taught:

- a greeting and an opening line;
- a question with a real answer — a price, a time, a size;
- at least one **repair** — a moment where one voice misses something and asks;
- a problem, and what happens about it;
- a close that ends the matter: *thanks, I'll come tomorrow then.*

**Set the phone down and do not stop it.** One take, recorded through. Mistakes stay in. The gate
is about carrying a conversation, not about producing a clean one.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the shopping list with two voices**:

> Can I have two kilos of rice? — Here. — Thank you. — Welcome.

Four turns and no problem, so there is nothing to carry. The recording ends at turn five because
the transaction genuinely finished.

The second is **one voice doing all the work**. The customer asks eleven questions and the
shopkeeper says *yes*, *no*, *forty rupees*. That is twelve turns by counting and one voice by
listening.

The third is **the stop-and-restart**. A word goes wrong at turn three, you stop the recording,
and you begin again. Twenty minutes later there are nine beginnings and no gate. Record through.

The fourth is **a situation with nothing at stake**, chosen because it feels safe. A chat about
the weather with a shopkeeper is not a transaction, and it will run out.

These are the errors beginners commonly make in a first gate recording. This day was written
before your own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

A real transaction is shorter than people expect and full of small repairs. Two strangers agreeing
on a size, a price and a day will typically manage it in a dozen turns, with at least one *sorry?*
inside them. That is exactly what the gate asks you to produce, which is not an accident.

Under pressure the customer's turns get shorter and the shopkeeper's get faster. That is worth
knowing when you play both sides, because the difference between your two voices is mostly
pressure, not personality.

What a native listener notices in a role-play: whether anything went wrong in it. A transaction
where nothing goes wrong sounds rehearsed even when it is not, because in real shops something
always does.
