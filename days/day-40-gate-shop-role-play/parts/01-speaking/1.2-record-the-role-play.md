## One-line answer

Press record once, play both sides through to the end of the transaction, and then count the turns
from the recording — because a gate is passed by a recording that exists, not by a plan for one.

## The story

The shirt is not back from the tailor. That is the whole situation, and it is enough.

You sit down with the phone on the table. You are the customer, who came at five because somebody
said five, and the shopkeeper, who is not sorry and is not rude and has four other people waiting.

You start. Somewhere in the middle you forget the word for the thing that fastens the collar, and
you go round it — *the small thing, the button one* — and the shopkeeper voice understands you,
because you are the shopkeeper.

At the end you count: fourteen turns. Two of them are bad. The fourteen exist.

That is what this part is for. Not a good recording. A recording.

**This is a rehearsal of the gate until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked.**

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **take** — one recording of one attempt.
- **carry** — to keep a conversation going to its end without stopping.
- **transaction** — an exchange in a shop: something asked for, something agreed, something ended.
- **count** — to go through the recording and number the turns.

The work of this part is three things, in this order.

1. **Record one take, through to the end.** No stopping, no restarting.
2. **Count the turns from the recording**, with a pen, one line per turn.
3. **Listen once for the five things the phase taught**, and mark which ones are actually in it.

Nothing in that list is *make it better*. That comes after the count, if at all.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate condition is a number — at least twelve turns — and a number cannot be judged by
feeling. A take that felt long is often nine turns, and a take that felt thin is often sixteen.
Counting is the whole check.

And this is the recording that goes in `docs/RECORDINGS.md` and into the Speaking Portfolio. It is
one of the small number of recordings from this course that are kept and listened to again at the
end.

## The rule

There is no new rule today. This is how to run the take.

**Before you press record**, have three things decided from
[part 1.1](1.1-what-the-gate-asks-for.md): the situation, the problem inside it, and how your two
voices differ. Nothing else needs deciding.

**The turn sheet**, drawn before you start, so counting afterwards is mechanical:

```text
    1 ____________________   7 ____________________
    2 ____________________   8 ____________________
    3 ____________________   9 ____________________
    4 ____________________  10 ____________________
    5 ____________________  11 ____________________
    6 ____________________  12 ____________________
```

**Press record and go.** When you lose a word, go round it — *the small thing you put the tea in*
— exactly as you would with a stranger. Going round a missing word is a skill; stopping the
recording is not.

**When the transaction ends, let it end.** A gate recording does not need a farewell scene. When
the matter is closed — *I'll come tomorrow morning, then. Thanks.* — stop.

**Then count.** Play it back with the sheet, one line per turn, writing three or four words of
each. If the count is under twelve, do **not** edit the take: run a second take with more problem
in it. A transaction that ends in nine turns needed a bigger problem, not a longer goodbye.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the take that was never recorded**. The situation gets chosen,
the problem gets planned, the voices get decided, and the evening ends with no file. A gate day
with no recording is not a gate day at all.

The second is **counting from memory**. *It was about fifteen.* It was eleven. The sheet exists
because a speaker inside a conversation cannot count it.

The third is **padding the end**:

> Thank you. — Thank you. — Have a good day. — You too. — Bye. — Bye.

Six turns of nothing, to reach twelve. The count passes and the take does not, because there is no
transaction inside those turns.

The fourth is **restarting after a bad word**. The bad word is not the failure; stopping is. In a
real shop nobody restarts a conversation because you used the wrong word for a button.

These are the errors beginners commonly make in a first gate recording. This day was written
before your own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Nobody ever gets a second take in a shop. That is the thing this exercise is imitating, and it is
why the one-take rule matters more than the quality of any single turn.

Under pressure, real speakers do exactly what you will do here: go round the missing word, accept
a smaller version of the sentence they wanted, and keep the transaction moving. A shopkeeper does
not notice which word you avoided. They notice whether the matter got settled.

What a listener hears in a gate recording that was stopped and restarted nine times is nothing
useful — every take is the first two minutes of a conversation. What they hear in one honest
fourteen-turn take is whether you can finish something.
