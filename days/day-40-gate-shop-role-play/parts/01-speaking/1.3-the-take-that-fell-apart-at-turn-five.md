## One-line answer

Record a second role-play where the person behind the counter says no — no stock, no refund, wrong
day — because a transaction that meets resistance is the one that shows whether the phase actually
holds, and a gate take that never meets any is a rehearsal of the easy half.

## The story

The first take went to fourteen turns and ended politely. Good.

Now the same shop, and one word changes:

> Can I exchange it? I have the bill.
> No.

And the conversation stands still. The customer voice has nowhere to go, because nineteen days of
practice have been about asking for things and being given them.

What comes next — *is there anything you can do?*, *who should I speak to?*, *when does the
manager come?*, *all right, can I leave my number?* — is the part that decides whether you can
hold a real conversation or only a cooperative one.

You build that on purpose today, and you find out at which turn it fell apart.

**This is a rehearsal of the gate until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked.**
The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce, not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **resistance** — the other person not agreeing, not helping, or saying no.
- **collapse** — the point where a conversation stops going anywhere.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will record a second take of the same situation with one change: **the counter voice refuses
twice.**

- **Refusal one**, early: what the customer asked for is not possible.
- **Refusal two**, in the middle: the first alternative is not possible either.

Then you find the exact turn where the customer voice ran out of anything to say, and you play the
take to a real person and ask them what they would have said at that turn.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate says twelve turns of a shop or a café. Nothing in it says the shopkeeper has to be
helpful, and no real phase 2 situation — a parcel, a bill, a repair, a booking — is reliably
helpful.

And phase 3 opens on stories about things that went wrong. Every one of those stories begins in a
conversation like this one. If your English stops at the first *no*, your stories will be about
things that went well, which is a much smaller life to talk about.

## The rule

There is no new rule today. This is how to make the take fail honestly and read the failure.

**Set up the two refusals in advance**, and write them down so the counter voice cannot soften
them while you are speaking:

> Refusal 1: `TODO(me)`
> Refusal 2: `TODO(me)`

**Record straight through.** When the customer voice has nothing, let the silence sit in the
recording. Do not rescue it. The silence is the finding.

**Then find the collapse.** Play it back with the turn sheet and mark the first turn where one of
these happened:

- a silence longer than a breath;
- a repeat of something already said;
- a switch out of English;
- the customer giving up when the matter was not settled.

> The take fell apart at turn: `TODO(me)`
> What happened there: `TODO(me)`

**Then ask a real person.** Play them the take up to that turn — and no further — and ask:

> What would you say here?

Write their answer word for word. This is the single most useful sentence you will collect today,
because it is the line your English did not reach for.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the take collapses at or before turn
eight, or when the matter ends unsettled because the customer voice had nothing left. It is
**GREEN** when both refusals are met with a next move, the take passes twelve turns, and the
matter is closed one way or the other — including *I'll come back with the bill on Monday*, which
is a close.

To make it go red on purpose, let the counter voice refuse flatly, with no reason attached, twice.

**Then the recovery drill.** Record eight next-move lines of your own, two for each of these four
walls, about real situations from your own life:

| The wall | What a next move does |
| --- | --- |
| *We don't have it.* | asks when, or asks what else |
| *That's the price.* | asks what is included, or asks about a smaller one |
| *You're too late.* | asks what is possible now |
| *That's not my department.* | asks who, and when they are here |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a counter voice that cannot be unkind**. You
refuse, and then you immediately offer a solution in the same turn, because you are also the
customer and you want the conversation to work. Write the refusals down first, exactly as they
will be said.

The second is **stopping the recording at the collapse**. That is the one moment worth keeping.
Let it run.

The third is **filling the silence with an apology**:

> Sorry. Sorry, sorry. OK. Thank you.

This is the commonest real collapse, and it ends the transaction with nothing settled. It belongs
in the ledger exactly as it happened.

The fourth, in real life, is **leaving with the matter unclosed**. Walking out with no next step
is what turns one wasted afternoon into three. A close is not agreement; it is knowing what
happens next.

These are the errors beginners commonly make in a first gate recording. This day was written
before your own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Real counters refuse constantly and without any unkindness — it is simply the answer. People who
get things done are not more forceful; they have three or four next moves ready, and they use them
in a level voice.

Under pressure, a learner's English shrinks fastest at exactly this point. The words are there on
a calm evening and gone in front of a queue. That is why the recovery drill is recorded rather
than read: what you have said out loud ten times comes back under pressure, and what you have only
understood does not.

What a native listener notices after a refusal is whether your next line is a question. A question
keeps the counter in the conversation. A statement — *but I need it today* — usually ends it.
