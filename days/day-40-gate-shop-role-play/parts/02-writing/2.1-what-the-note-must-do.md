## One-line answer

The phase 2 writing gate is a note of about a hundred words that does three separate jobs — asks
for something, thanks somebody, and gives a direction — and this part teaches nothing new, because
a gate that teaches is not a gate.

## The story

Somebody is going to look after your flat for four days while you are away. They have agreed, they
are doing you a favour, and they have never been inside it.

One note has to do three things. It has to ask them for something — to water the plants, to take
in a parcel. It has to thank them, properly, for a real thing. And it has to tell them something
they must get right: which key, which switch, which door, which day.

That note is the gate. It is not a test of new English. It is a test of whether a hundred words
you already have can carry three jobs without dropping one of them.

**This is a rehearsal, and it says so.** This day was written before your error ledger had rows in
it. Until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked, the checks below are the
general ones rather than your own recurring mistakes, and the gate is not yet a gate.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate** — the check at the end of a phase, which the whole phase was for.
- **job** — one thing a piece of writing has to achieve.
- **direction** — an instruction telling somebody how to do or find something.
- **rehearsal** — a practice of the real thing, done as if it were the real thing.

The gate wording is in the plan's §7 and it is copied here without a word changed:

> Writing: a hundred-word note that asks for something, thanks someone and gives a direction.

Three jobs, and each one is judged separately.

| Job | What it needs to survive | What kills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asks for something | one clear request the reader can say yes to | a request buried in the middle of a paragraph |
| Thanks someone | one specific thing, not a general thank-you | *thanks for everything* |
| Gives a direction | something a stranger could follow without you | *you know where it is* |

A hundred words is about six to eight sentences. Three jobs in eight sentences means every
sentence is carrying something.

## The rule

There is no new rule today. What follows is how the three jobs sit on a page.

**A page was opened and checked on 2026-09-23** for the shape of a written request:
[An email to ask a colleague to do something](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/email-ask-colleague-do-something).
Its tips are these:

> Explain the reason for the request.
> Give a deadline when possible.
> Give dates clearly.
> Make your request polite by starting it with Do you think you could…? or I would be grateful if
> you/we/I could…

**The order that works**, and the reason for it:

```text
   1  greeting                       one line
   2  why you are writing            one sentence — the reader knows what this is
   3  THE REQUEST                    its own sentence, early
   4  THE DIRECTION                  its own sentence or two, concrete
   5  THE THANK-YOU                  specific, near the end
   6  sign-off                       one line
```

The request goes **early**, because a reader decides whether to act in the first three lines. The
thank-you goes **late**, because a thank-you at the top reads as softening-up before a demand.

**Each job, alone, in a sentence:**

> Could you water the two pots on the balcony while I'm away?      ← the request
> The key that opens the balcony door is the small brass one.      ← the direction
> Thank you for taking the parcel in last month — it saved me a day off work.   ← the thank-you

**Counting a hundred words.** Count them. Not an estimate — an actual count on the page. Ninety to
a hundred and ten is the band. Under eighty, a job is missing. Over a hundred and thirty, two jobs
have merged into a story.

**What "a direction a stranger could follow" means.** Test it with the one question: *could
somebody who has never been here do this?*

> It's in the usual place.        ← not a direction
> It's on the hook behind the door, on the left as you come in.   ← a direction

**Punctuation, which is part of the deliverable.** Capital letters, full stops, a blank line
between blocks, and the reader's name spelled correctly. On a gate, these are visible before the
content is.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **three jobs melted into one paragraph**:

> Thanks so much for everything and if you could please water the plants and the key is there,
> thanks again.

All three jobs are technically present. None of them is findable, and a reader acts on none of
them.

The second is **the thank-you that is not about anything**:

> Thank you for all your help.

Any note to anybody could carry that sentence. The gate asks for a thank-you; a general one does
not do the job.

The third is **the direction that assumes**:

> The switch is the usual one, you know it.

If the reader knew it, the direction would not need to be in the note.

The fourth is **the request with no reason and no date**:

> Please water the plants.

The page checked above says a request wants a reason and a date. Without them, a reader puts it
off, and putting it off is the same as not doing it.

These are the errors beginners commonly make in a first gate note. This day was written before
your own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Notes that do three jobs are the commonest real writing there is — to a neighbour, a landlord, a
colleague, a teacher. What separates the ones that get acted on is that each job stands on its own
line and can be found by a reader who is skimming with one hand on a door handle.

Under pressure people write the thank-you first and the request last, because the request is the
uncomfortable part. A reader who is busy reads the first two lines and puts the note down, so the
uncomfortable part never gets read.

A formal reader adds one requirement: a date. *This week* is not a date. *Before Friday* is. A
request without a date is a request that arrives at the bottom of somebody's list and stays there.
