## One-line answer

Write the hundred words, count them, check the three jobs are each findable in one pass, and
**send it to the real person** — because the gate is a note that was received, not a note that was
drafted.

## The story

The note for the person looking after your flat gets written on the second attempt.

The first attempt is a hundred and sixty words, warm, and the request is in sentence five. Reading
it back, you can see the problem: you were being polite for four sentences before you said what
you wanted.

The second attempt is ninety-six words. The request is in the third line, the direction has the
colour of the key in it, and the thank-you at the end names the parcel from last month.

Then you send it. That is the part that makes it a note. Until then it is a page about a note.

**This is a rehearsal of the gate until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked.**

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **draft** — the first version of a piece of writing.
- **word count** — the actual number of words, counted.
- **findable** — able to be seen by a reader who reads once, quickly.
- **send** — to give it to the person it is for, by any real means.

The work is four passes, and the last one is the one that makes it a gate.

| Pass | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| Draft | the six-block order from [part 2.1](2.1-what-the-note-must-do.md), written badly and fast |
| Count | the real number of words, written at the bottom of the page |
| Check | the three jobs, each findable in one pass; capitals, full stops, spelling, the name |
| Send | to the actual person, by message, on paper, or by email |

evening.

## The rule

There is no new rule today. This is how to run the four passes.

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[An email to ask a colleague to do something](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/email-ask-colleague-do-something),
whose tips this note follows:

> Explain the reason for the request.
> Give a deadline when possible.
> Give dates clearly.
> Make your request polite by starting it with Do you think you could…? or I would be grateful if
> you/we/I could…

**Pass one — draft.** Write the six blocks in order without stopping to fix anything. Long is
fine here.

**Pass two — count.** Count the words on the page and write the number at the bottom. Then cut to
between ninety and a hundred and ten. Cut in this order:

1. anything that says how sorry you are to ask;
2. *very*, *really*, *just*, *actually*;
3. any sentence that is background rather than one of the three jobs.

**Pass three — the findability check.** This one is mechanical. Mark your own note:

| Job | Which sentence carries it | Could a reader find it in one pass? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The request | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| The direction | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| The thank-you | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

If two jobs are in the same sentence, split them. Then read it once for the things a reader sees
before they read: capital letters, full stops, blank lines between blocks, and the reader's name
spelled right.

**Pass four — send it.** A message, a printed page, a note on a table, an email. Real person, real
delivery, today.

**Then keep the evidence.** Put the sent version, the word count, and what the person actually did
into your lab page. The Writing Portfolio row is about a note that was received, and what happened
to it is part of the record.

**The shape on the page**, with a blank line between blocks so the reader's eye can find the
edges:

```text
   Hi <name>,

   <why you're writing — one sentence>

   <THE REQUEST — with a reason and a date>

   <THE DIRECTION — concrete enough for a stranger>

   <THE THANK-YOU — one specific thing>

   <sign-off>
   <your name>
```

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the note that is never sent**. It is written, checked, counted,
and left in the notebook. Everything about it is finished except the only part that makes it real.

The second is **sending a note with the name spelled wrong**. It is the one error a reader always
notices, and it colours everything under it.

The third is **cutting the direction instead of the padding**. Under pressure to reach a hundred
words, writers cut the concrete sentence, because it feels like detail. It is the job.

The fourth is **three jobs in three paragraphs of forty words each**. Now the note is a hundred
and twenty words and none of it is skimmable. The blocks are short on purpose.

These are the errors beginners commonly make in a first gate note. This day was written before
your own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Real notes are read on a phone, standing up, with something else happening. That is the reader
this shape is built for: blank lines, one job per block, the request near the top.

Under pressure people write longer and later. A request sent the night before is a request with no
room in it, and no amount of polite language repairs a missing day of notice. The date in the note
is worth more than the politeness around it.

A formal reader will answer the part of the note that is easiest to answer. If your request is
buried and your thank-you is at the top, you will get a warm reply that agrees to nothing.
