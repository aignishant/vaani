## One-line answer

Write the same note again with one job quietly removed, give both versions to real readers, and
ask each of them what they have to do — because a note that does two jobs out of three looks
complete, reads warmly, and fails silently.

## The story

Two notes about the same four days away.

The first has the request, the direction and the thank-you. The second is warmer, shorter and
easier to read, because the direction has been left out — the key is *in the usual place*, which
felt fine while writing it.

Both readers say yes. Both are happy to help.

One of them stands outside a door on Tuesday evening with a bag in each hand, looking at a hook
that has three keys on it, and calls you while you are on a train with no signal.

Nothing in the second note was wrong. It was missing, and missing is invisible to the person who
wrote it, because they know where the key is.

**This is a rehearsal of the gate until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked.**
The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce, not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **silent failure** — something that goes wrong without anybody noticing at the time.
- **complete** — having all its parts.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will write three versions of your gate note, all about the same real situation.

- **Note A — the two-job note.** The direction removed, and replaced with something that assumes:
  *the usual place*, *you know the one*, *where it always is*.
- **Note B — the melted note.** All three jobs present, but joined into one warm paragraph with no
  blank lines, the request in the middle.
- **Note C — the gate note.** The one you sent in
  [part 2.2](2.2-write-and-send-the-note.md).

Then two real readers, one note each, and one question: **what do you have to do, and how?**

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate is judged on three jobs, and the way a gate note actually fails is not bad grammar. It is
a job missing, or a job present and unfindable. Both are invisible from the writer's chair.

And the phase 3 writing that comes next is longer — paragraphs, stories, pages. Everything learned
here about a job being findable gets harder at three times the length.

## The rule

There is no new rule today. This is how to make the failure visible.

**Write note A by deletion, not by rewriting.** Take your sent note and remove only the direction,
putting an assuming phrase in its place. Everything else stays. This keeps the test honest: only
one thing changed.

**Write note B by joining.** Same three jobs, no blank lines, the request in the middle of the
paragraph, warm sentences at both ends.

**Then the reader test.** One note per reader, read once, then taken away. Ask exactly this:

> What do you have to do, and how?

Write down what they say, and — this is the column that matters — **what they did not mention.**

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader of note A says yes,
sounds confident, and cannot say *how* — and when the reader of note B misses the request or names
it only after being prompted. It is **GREEN** when note C's reader names all three jobs, including
the direction, in their own words, with nothing prompting them.

To make it go red on purpose, make the assuming phrase in note A a friendly one. *The key's in the
usual place* is far more dangerous than leaving the key unmentioned, because it stops the reader
asking.

**Then the three-job audit of your own real messages.** Take the last five messages you sent that
asked somebody to do something. For each one, mark whether a reader could have found the request
and the how in one pass. Count the ones that could not.

**Then repair note A**, not by pasting the direction back, but by writing the direction for
somebody who has never been to the place — a colour, a side, a landmark, a number of steps.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an assuming phrase that is obviously vague**:

> The key is somewhere.

No reader accepts that, so nobody fails and nothing is learned. The dangerous phrase is the
confident one.

The second is **using the same reader for two notes**. Once they know the situation, every later
note is clear. One note per reader.

The third is **helping them while they read**. Every word you add out loud is a job the note did
not have to do.

The fourth, in real writing, is **the note that assumes because the writer is tired**. At the end
of a long day, *you know where it is* costs one second to write and somebody else an evening. That
is the whole failure, and it never announces itself.

These are the errors beginners commonly make in a first gate note. This day was written before
your own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Silent failures are the expensive ones everywhere in writing. A confused reader asks. A confident
reader with a missing instruction acts, and acts wrongly, and neither of you finds out until it
matters.

Under pressure, the direction is the first job to go, because it is the least polite-sounding part
of a note and the one the writer knows best. The instinct is exactly backwards: the part you know
best is the part your reader knows least.

A formal reader does not ask either. An office, a landlord or a supplier answers the part of the
note that was clear and ignores the rest, and the record then shows that you were answered.
