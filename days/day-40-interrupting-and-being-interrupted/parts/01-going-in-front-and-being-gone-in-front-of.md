# Going in front, and being gone in front of

`IN-07` · Day 40

## The one-line answer

Cutting in is allowed when you say out loud that you are doing it, and being cut in on is only a
loss if you stop — because stopping is how you agree.

## The model

Picture a queue that is not moving and is not going to. Sometimes you genuinely have to go in
front of somebody. Everybody knows there is a way to do that which people accept and a way that
starts an argument, and the difference is not how big you are. It is whether you named it while
you were doing it.

Yesterday was joining a gap that existed. Today there is no gap. They are going to keep talking,
and the thing you need to say has to go in anyway.

### Going in front

**Naming it is the permission.** *Sorry to jump in —* is not an apology. It is you announcing the
cut while you make it, and that announcement is the entire reason it is allowed. Said before the
cut it is a request, and requests get refused. Said during, it is a fact, and people move over.

> Sorry to jump in — only I think we're answering two different questions here.
> Can I stop you there? Because that's the bit I don't follow.

Note the second half of each. **Carry a reason in the same breath.** A cut-in with a reason
attached reads as useful. A bare one reads as impatience, and the reason costs you six words.

**Start. Do not get louder.** This is the one everybody gets wrong. People yield to a *start*, not
to a volume. If you cut in at the volume you were already at, the room hears somebody taking a
turn. If you cut in by going up — and it is almost always the pitch that goes up, not just the
loudness — the room hears a disagreement, and now the conversation is about the interruption
rather than about your point.

**Overlap, then commit.** Real cut-ins begin two or three words before the other person finishes.
That overlap is not rudeness, it is the mechanism — it is how they know a turn is being taken
rather than a noise being made. And once you have started, you finish. Which brings us to the
thing that actually goes wrong.

**Half-starting is worse than not starting.** You say *but* — and stop. You say *yeah, but* — and
stop. Each of those was audible. Everybody heard it. What the room learns is that noises from you
do not turn into sentences, and after two or three of those it stops turning towards you at all.
You did not fail to interrupt. You interrupted three times and abandoned it three times.

**And you are almost certainly doing this too little rather than too much.** In an English-speaking
group, somebody who never cuts in is not read as polite. They are read as not having anything to
say, or as not being interested, and both of those cost more than a cut-in ever does.

### Being gone in front of

Somebody rolls over you. You have three options and all three are fine. The failure is not
choosing.

**Hold.** Keep going. Same volume, same speed, do not speed up and do not stop. Most interruptions
are tentative — the other person is half-committed and will fold within a few words if there is
nothing for them to land on. But this only works if you carry on, because **if you stop, you have
agreed.** Stopping is consent, and it is instant, and neither of you decided it.

**Yield on purpose, out loud.** *Finish what you were saying.* Said deliberately, this is not
losing. You have handed them the turn, which puts you next, and everybody in the room heard you
do it.

**Reclaim afterwards.** *I wasn't quite done.* And then the move that makes reclaiming possible:
name where you had got to.

> I'd got as far as the second option, and I want to finish that bit.

Without the marker you have to start your whole point again from the top, which is enough work
that you usually decide not to bother — and that is how the thing you actually wanted to say ends
up never being said.

**What you must not do is trail off.** It looks like politeness. It is not a choice at all: you
lose the turn *and* you get no credit for yielding, because nobody heard you decide anything.
Pick one of the three, and pick it on purpose.

**Today's phrases** — going in front, and coming back after somebody went in front of you. All of
these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Sorry to jump in — | Naming the cut while you are already making it |
| Sorry, can I come in here? | Cutting in when no gap is coming and you want it softened |
| Can I stop you there? | You have to stop them before they go further |
| I wasn't quite done. | Somebody rolled over you and you are taking it back |
| I'd got as far as {{…}}. | Reclaiming by naming where you stopped |
| Finish what you were saying. | Yielding on purpose, and saying so out loud |
| You go first. | You both started on the same beat |
| No, after you. | Ending the polite standoff in one move |

## Say it now

Each line twice. The first four are said **over the top of something** — put a recording on, or
just talk to yourself in your other voice, and cut across it. These lines have never once been
said into silence and they should not be practised that way.

1. *Sorry to jump in — only {{your reason}}.*
2. *Sorry, can I come in here? {{your point}}.*
3. *Can I stop you there? Because {{the bit you don't follow}}.*
4. *I wasn't quite done — I'd got as far as {{where you stopped}}.*
5. *Finish what you were saying about {{their point}} — then I'll come back.*
6. *You go first.*
7. *No, after you — {{yours}} can wait.*
8. *I'd got as far as {{…}}, and then {{what the rest of it was}}.*

Now run lines 1 to 4 again with one rule: **your volume must not change when you cut in.** Record
a couple. Cut in at exactly the level you were speaking at before. It will feel like it cannot
possibly work, and on the tape it works — the start is doing the job, and the volume was never
doing anything except making you sound cross.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d40-free.m4a`.**

> Put on some continuous talk — a discussion, a lecture, anything where somebody speaks and does
> not stop. Two halves, both on the same file.
>
> **First:** cut in **six times**. Three by naming the cut, three by attaching to something they
> just said. Every single one has to turn into a full sentence of your own. No half-starts.
>
> **Second:** start talking over the recording and keep going while it carries on underneath you.
> Do not raise your volume, do not speed up, and do not stop until your point is finished. Then
> reclaim out loud, naming where you had got to.

- **Solo form:** as written. The recording is a good sparring partner precisely because it will
  never stop for you, which is the condition you are training for.
- **With a partner:** they talk, and their instruction is to interrupt you at every clause end
  they can find. You must **hold** at least twice, **yield deliberately** at least once, and
  **reclaim** once. They are not allowed to be kind about it.

## The deliberate failure

Put the recording on again. Half-start three times: say *but —* and stop, *yeah, but —* and stop,
*I —* and stop. Do not turn any of them into a sentence. Keep recording through all three.

**What breaking sounds like:** play it back and count what is actually on the tape. Three
interruptions. Not none — three, all of them audible, all of them abandoned. And listen to what
your voice does across them: it gets **quieter** each time, not louder, because each abandoned
start makes the next one less convincing to you.

That is the uncomfortable finding, and it reframes something you have probably been telling
yourself for years. On the days you came out of a meeting having said nothing, you were not
silent. You tried three times, out loud, where everybody could hear, and pulled out of all three —
and to the room that reads as somebody who starts and does not mean it, which is a worse thing to
be than somebody who cuts in.

Then do it once more and finish the sentence, however badly. Same start, same volume, same
awkwardness. The only variable is that this one arrived.

## When it breaks in the wild

A meeting, and you have the answer. You start — *but* — and somebody keeps going. You start
again — *yeah, but* — and pull back because it felt rude. Then a colleague says almost exactly the
thing you were going to say, everybody agrees with them, and the meeting moves on. Afterwards you
put it down to your English. It was not your English. The sentence was ready both times.

**The repair:**

> Sorry to jump in — I'd got as far as the second option, and I want to finish that.

Both halves in one breath: the cut is named as you make it, and the marker tells everybody there
is something specific coming, so the room waits for it.

None of this is about becoming a person who talks over other people. It is about the gap between
*I did not get to say it* and *I decided not to say it*, which is a large gap, and only one side of
it is a choice.
