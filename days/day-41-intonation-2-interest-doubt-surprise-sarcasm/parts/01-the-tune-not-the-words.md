# The tune that says what the words don't

`PR-13` · Day 41

## The one-line answer

The same three words can arrive as interested, doubtful, surprised or dry with nothing changed
but the tune — and one of those four, misfired, lands as rude.

## The model

Think of the words as the dish and the tune as the seasoning. The rice is the rice. What you put
on it decides whether the person eating it feels looked after, suspicious, or quietly insulted.
Nobody at the table is tasting the rice on its own.

Day 26 gave you the two directions — the last word steps down onto the floor and lands, or stops
on the step above and waits. That is salt or no salt. It tells the listener whether you are
finished. It does not tell them how you feel about what you just heard.

Today is the other half. Not *which direction* but **how far the voice travels, how fast it gets
there, and where the move starts.** That is the whole of today, and it is carried almost entirely
by three or four words at a time.

Take these three words and keep them exactly as they are:

> Oh, that's interesting.

**Interested.** Start *Oh* high, above your ordinary speaking level. Then let *interesting* climb
and drop inside itself — up on *in*, down through the rest. Wide and quick. Say it and notice how
much room your voice used. Interest in English is expensive: it costs pitch. A narrow voice cannot
sound interested no matter how sincere you are.

**Doubtful.** Now the opposite recipe. Start low. Keep *Oh* level. Stretch *interesting* out and
let the last part sit in between — it does not land on the floor and it does not lift. Then add
the ingredient most people miss: **a small gap before you speak at all.** The gap is half the
doubt. Say the line with no gap, then again with one, and hear which of the two makes the listener
want to explain themselves.

**Surprised.** This one is a jump. Start low, then step up fast on the stressed syllable, and keep
it short. Surprise is sudden, not loud. Say it once with more volume and no jump — it sounds like
you are cross. Say it once with the jump and your ordinary volume — it sounds surprised. The
ingredient is the jump. Volume is not in this recipe at all.

**Dry.** Nothing. No move. Flat, low, even, with the words a little longer than usual. Flat is not
the absence of seasoning here. Flat *is* the seasoning, and it is a strong one.

**And here is the part nobody tells you.** Flat is not neutral in English. A positive word on a
flat, low, slow tune does not read as *calm* or *polite* — it reads as unimpressed, or as
sarcastic. This matters because a careful voice is a narrow voice. When you are concentrating on
getting the sentence right, your pitch flattens out without your permission. So the sentence comes
out correct and the tune comes out cold, and what the other person receives is a person who is not
impressed by them. They will not tell you. They will just be slightly less warm next time.

**Sarcasm — taste it before you cook with it.** The rule is simple enough to hear: sarcasm is a
mismatch. A warm word on a cold tune. If the words are positive and the tune is flat, slow and
low, the speaker means roughly the opposite, and the flatness is doing the whole job.

> Well, that's one way of doing it.
> You'd think so, wouldn't you.

Say both flat and low. Neither sentence contains a single negative word, and neither one is a
compliment.

**Recognise it first.** The real risk is not that you fail to produce sarcasm — it is that you are
the only person in the room taking a sarcastic line at face value, and you answer it seriously
while everybody else moves on. So today's first job is hearing it: warm words, cold tune, means
the opposite.

**Produce it second, and carefully.** The same mismatch pointed at the wrong person, or used in a
room where you do not yet know how people talk to each other, is not funny — it is rude, and you
do not get to explain the tune afterwards. Two safeguards, and they are both audible. Aim it at a
thing rather than a person: the weather, the queue, your own morning, never their work. And use it
only after somebody in that room has used it in front of you first. Dryness is the one seasoning
you copy from the kitchen you are standing in.

**Today's phrases** — short reactions whose whole meaning lives in the tune, which is why they are
worth owning. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Oh, really. | Interested with a wide climb and drop — doubtful flat and stretched |
| Oh, that's interesting. | The workhorse; the tune decides between fascinated and bored |
| Wait, what? | Genuine surprise, carried by the jump rather than the volume |
| You're joking. | Dropped, you believe them and you're delighted; lifted, you don't believe them |
| Since when? | Doubt aimed at a claim rather than at the person making it |
| Mm, I don't know about that. | Doubt you're willing to own, with the gap in front of it |
| Well, that's one way of doing it. | The dry one — learn to hear it before you ever say it |
| You'd think so, wouldn't you. | Dry agreement, on a dropped tag from Day 26 |

## Say it now

Each line twice, with the two tunes named after it. The words never change between the two — only
the seasoning does. If the pair sounds like the same sentence said twice, your voice is not
travelling far enough on either one.

1. *Oh, really. {{something somebody told you today}}.* → interested, then doubtful.
2. *Oh, that's interesting — {{a detail from it}}.* → interested, then dry.
3. *Wait, what? {{the bit you didn't expect}}?* → surprised, then doubtful.
4. *You're joking. {{the good news}}.* → dropped and delighted, then lifted and disbelieving.
5. *Since when? {{the claim}}.* → doubtful, then genuinely surprised.
6. *Mm, I don't know about that. {{the thing you doubt}}.* → doubtful with a gap in front, then
   doubtful with no gap.

Then run all six again with the tunes shuffled — decide the seasoning silently before each line
and do not say which one you picked. Recording that pass is useful, because you will find out
whether the tune you chose is the tune that came out. It very often is not.

  from their side, and you tell them which ones were invented. Both lists are useful.

## The deliberate failure

Take three sentences you actually mean — a real compliment, a piece of good news, something you
genuinely want to hear more about — and say all three flat. Low, level, unhurried, no movement
anywhere. Mean every word while you say them.

**What breaking sounds like:** sarcasm, or boredom. You will not be able to hear your own sincerity
anywhere on the tape, because none of it made it into the tune. That is the uncomfortable finding,
and it is the reason this day exists: the flat version is not a smaller version of the warm one, it
is a different message. Nobody would ever ask you whether you meant it — they would simply take
the tune at its word and remember you as unimpressed.

Now say the same three with the voice moving as far as it will go. From the inside this feels
theatrical, close to embarrassing. On playback it sounds ordinary. That mismatch — feels like far
too much, sounds like just enough — is the single most useful thing on today's recording, and you
can only find it by hearing both versions back to back.

## When it breaks in the wild

Somebody shows you something they have been working on for weeks. You are concentrating on your
English, so your voice goes narrow and careful, and what comes out is *oh, that's good* — flat,
level, on its way to somewhere else. Their face changes. They say *you don't like it, do you*, and
now you are explaining your own enthusiasm, which never sounds like enthusiasm.

**The repair:**

> No — sorry, I do. I mean it, that's really good.

Say *really* with the voice climbing and dropping inside the word. The repair is not the apology.
The repair is the movement you finally put into the sentence, and it has to be wider than feels
natural, because the first version already set the level.

same six words with four seasonings until the seasoning stops feeling like a decision.
