## One-line answer

To tell somebody what you did yesterday, you need three things: the past form of the verb, a time
word to anchor each step, and the decision to leave most of the day out.

## The story

A friend was ill yesterday and missed everything. Today he wants to know what happened.

You start:

> I go to the office. There is a meeting. My manager say the thing about the new floor …

He follows you, because he knows you and he knows the office. But something is wrong with the
story and he cannot put it down: it is happening now, in the room, while he listens. He keeps
waiting for it to become yesterday.

You start again:

> I went in early. There was a meeting at eleven. The manager told us about the new floor.

Now it has landed. The events are behind you both, and he can listen to a finished thing instead
of watching an unfinished one.

The vocabulary did not change. Three verbs changed shape.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **past** — time that has already happened.
- **verb** — the doing word: *go*, *eat*, *call*, *finish*.
- **past form** — the shape a verb takes for things that are finished: *go* → *went*.
- **time word** — a word or phrase saying when: *yesterday*, *at eleven*, *after lunch*.
- **anchor** — to fix something in place, so a listener knows where they are.

Three pieces make a spoken account of yesterday.

| Piece | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The past form | tells the listener it is finished | *I went*, *she called*, *we waited* |
| The time word | anchors each step | *yesterday*, *in the morning*, *after that* |
| The cut | leaves out everything that does not matter | six sentences, not sixty |

The third piece is the one nobody teaches and everybody needs. A day has a hundred events in it. A
spoken account has about six, and choosing them is most of the skill.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3 is about telling what happened, and it ends in a recorded story from your life of at least
two hundred spoken words. That is a much longer version of this, and it is built from the same
three pieces.

And this is the commonest thing anybody is asked. *How was your day?* is a question you will
answer several thousand times in your life, and the difference between an answer that goes
somewhere and an answer that dies is decided in the first two sentences.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple).
It gives the regular pattern as adding *–ed*, with examples including *called*, *liked*, *wanted*
and *worked*, and these model sentences:

> I **met** my wife in 1983.
> We **went** to Spain for our holidays.
> They **got** home very late last night.
> When I was a boy, I **walked** a mile to school every day.

Notice that three of those four verbs do not end in *–ed* at all. Both kinds live side by side in
any real account of a day, so both are here from the start.

**The ten verbs you will use to describe almost any day:**

| Now | Yesterday |
| --- | --- |
| go | went |
| get up | got up |
| have | had |
| eat | ate |
| take | took |
| see | saw |
| say | said |
| come | came |
| work | worked |
| finish | finished |

Eight of those change shape completely; two add *–ed*. That is what real English is like at this
level, and it is why the account matters more than the pattern today.

**Where the time words go.** At the front, to anchor a step; at the end, to add a detail:

> **Yesterday morning** I went in early.
> I got home **at nine**.

Do not put one in every sentence. Two or three in a six-sentence account is right; six sounds like
a timetable.

**The shape of a six-sentence account:**

```text
   1  where it started       Yesterday I went in early.
   2  the main thing         There was a meeting about the new floor.
   3  what happened in it    The manager asked me to take the Tuesday shift.
   4  the turn               I said yes, and then I remembered the class.
   5  what you did about it  So I called the office in the evening.
   6  where it ended         In the end they moved it to Thursday.
```

That is a whole day in six sentences, and only one of them is about the meeting.

**The one word that keeps an account moving**: *then*. It is the cheapest anchor in English and
carries no weight of its own, which is why it is useful in the middle and tiring at the start of
every sentence.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the day told in the present**:

> I go to the office. My manager say about the floor. I am waiting outside.

A listener understands it and has to do the work of moving everything backwards. Over a long
account they stop doing that work, and the story stops being followable.

The second is **the complete day**, from waking to sleeping:

> I got up at six. I brushed my teeth. I had tea. I took the bus. The bus was late. I reached at
> nine. I sat down. I opened my email …

Nothing is wrong except that it has no shape. A listener cannot tell which of the twenty sentences
is the one they are supposed to react to.

The third is **the past form used with *did***, which is the error that survives longest:

> I didn't went.

*Did* already carries the past, so the verb goes back to its plain form: *I didn't go*. That rule
has a whole day of its own later in the phase, and it is named here so that you recognise it when
you hear yourself do it.

The fourth is **mixing the two halves of a sentence**:

> Yesterday I go to the shop and bought rice.

The second verb moved; the first did not. A listener hears the sentence change time in the middle.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Real answers to *what did you do yesterday?* are much shorter than learners expect — two or three
sentences, with one interesting thing in them. The six-sentence account is what you give somebody
who actually wants to know, not what you give everybody.

Under pressure the tense slips backwards into the present, because the present forms are the ones
you learned first and they come out under load. It happens to everybody, and it is not worth
stopping for: a listener forgives a slipped tense inside a moving story and cannot forgive a story
that never moves.

What a native listener notices is not the verb shapes. It is whether the account has a point — the
moment where something changed. An account with no turn in it gets a polite *oh, right* and a
change of subject, whatever the grammar is doing.
