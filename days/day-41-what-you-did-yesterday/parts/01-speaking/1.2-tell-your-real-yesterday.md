## One-line answer

Tell three real days to three different listeners — the short version, the six-sentence version,
and the version with the one moment stretched out — because the length of an account is chosen by
who is asking, not by how much happened.

## The story

Three people ask about the same yesterday.

The neighbour on the stairs, who has one hand on the railing, gets:

> Long day. The meeting went on and on, and then I had to change my class.

Your friend who was ill gets the six sentences, in order, with the part about the Tuesday shift in
the middle, because he needs to know what he missed.

Your brother on the phone in the evening gets one moment only, stretched:

> So the manager asks me — in front of everybody — can you do Tuesdays. And I've got the class on
> Tuesdays. And everyone's looking at me.

Same day. Three lengths, three shapes, and all three are correct. The mistake is not choosing —
giving the six-sentence version to the neighbour on the stairs, or two sentences to the friend who
missed everything.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **account** — what you say about something that happened.
- **stretch** — to tell one small part slowly, with detail.
- **compress** — to say a lot in very few words.
- **audience** — the person listening.

Three lengths, and you need all three, because they are used for different people.

| Length | About | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two sentences | the whole day, compressed to its feeling and one fact | somebody passing, somebody busy |
| Six sentences | the day with its turn in the middle | somebody who actually wants to know |
| One moment, stretched | ninety seconds of the day, with detail | somebody close, in the evening |

The third is the hardest and the most useful, because it is a story. Phase 3 ends in one.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story from your life of at least two hundred spoken words with a
beginning, a middle and an end. Two hundred words is the stretched version — one moment, told
properly — not a longer list of everything that happened.

And this is the rep that makes the tense automatic. You can learn *went*, *said*, *took* from a
table and still reach for *go*, *say*, *take* the moment you are actually telling something. Only
telling real days fixes that.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple),
whose model sentences show both kinds of verb side by side:

> I **met** my wife in 1983.
> When I was a boy, I **walked** a mile to school every day.

**Three real days, not one.** Yesterday, the day before, and one day last week that you remember
for a reason. Different days keep you from polishing one account into a performance.

**For each day, find the turn first.** The turn is the moment something changed: a plan broke,
somebody said something unexpected, a thing went wrong or went right. Say it out loud before you
record:

> The turn was when `TODO(me)`.

An account with no turn is a list. If a day genuinely has no turn, that is a finding — say so, and
pick a different day for the stretched version.

**The two-sentence version.** One sentence for the shape of the day, one for the fact worth
carrying:

> It was a long one. The meeting ran over and I had to move my class.

**The six-sentence version.** The shape from
[part 1.1](1.1-yesterday-in-six-sentences.md), with two or three time words and not six.

**The stretched version.** Take the turn and slow down around it. Three things make a moment
stretch:

```text
   where you were      I was standing by the door with my bag already on my shoulder.
   what was said       And he says, can you take Tuesdays from next week.
   what you thought    And I'm thinking about the class, and everybody's waiting.
```

**A real thing fluent speakers do, which is worth knowing now**: inside a stretched story they
often switch into the present — *and he says*, *and I'm thinking* — to make it feel close. That is
not an error; it is a storytelling move, and it works only if the story was clearly set in the
past first. Keep the frame in the past and let the hot middle sit wherever it wants.

**Record each version for a different real person**, and tell them which version they are getting.
A listener who knows they are getting the short one does not wait for more.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **one length for everybody** — usually the long one. The neighbour
on the stairs gets six sentences and starts moving towards the door at sentence three.

The second is **the account with no turn**:

> I went to work. It was normal. Then I came home. I watched something. I slept.

There is nothing for the listener to react to, so the polite thing they say is *oh, OK*, and the
conversation ends there.

The third is **starting the stretched version at the beginning of the day**. A stretched story
starts as close to the turn as possible. If your ninety seconds begin with getting up, the turn
arrives after the listener has stopped listening.

The fourth is **the past that slides into the present and never comes back**:

> Yesterday I went in early. There is a meeting. He says Tuesday. I am not knowing what to say.

The story started in the past and then left it for good. The present-tense storytelling move only
works when the listener has been told where they are standing.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

People answer *how was your day?* with about eleven words, almost always. The long answer is
invited by a second question — *what happened?* — and giving it before the invitation is the
commonest way a good story lands badly.

Under pressure, at a desk or a counter, only the short version exists, and it has to carry the
fact that matters: *I came on Tuesday and it wasn't ready.* A person who can only tell long
versions cannot make a complaint.

What a native listener notices in the stretched version is the detail that has no job — the bag on
your shoulder, the door. Those are the parts people repeat afterwards. Facts get summarised;
details get quoted.
