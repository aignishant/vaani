## One-line answer

Tell yesterday entirely in the present on purpose, play it to a real person, and ask them one
question — *when did this happen?* — because a listener who has to keep moving your story
backwards stops being able to say what it was about.

## The story

Somebody tells you about a problem with a delivery.

> So I am waiting at home. The man is calling me. He is saying the address is wrong. I am telling
> him the address. He is not finding it.

You understand every sentence. What you cannot answer is the only question that matters: **is this
happening now?** Is the man outside at this moment? Was this last week? Should you be doing
something?

That question sits under the whole story and never gets answered, and by the end you are not
listening to the story any more — you are trying to place it in time.

Today you make that on purpose, with your own real day, and find out what a listener does with it.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **time frame** — where in time a story is standing: now, or finished.
- **place in time** — to work out when something happened.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will record the same real day twice, with one difference.

- **The RED take.** Every verb in the present. *I go, he says, I am waiting, she is telling me.*
  Same facts, same order, same length.
- **The GREEN take.** The same day in the past, with the shape from
  [part 1.1](1.1-yesterday-in-six-sentences.md).

Then a real person hears each take once and answers two questions:

> When did this happen?
> What was the main thing?

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story of at least two hundred spoken words. Two hundred words is
long enough that a listener who cannot place the story in time gives up somewhere in the middle,
and the gate is judged on whether a listener can follow it.

And this is the error that survives longest in a learner's speech, because it is invisible from the
inside. You know when it happened. You have never had to find out.

## The rule

**Use a real day with a real turn in it**, not an easy one. Six sentences, both takes.

**Record the RED take first**, or your mouth will refuse to do it afterwards.

**Then run the listener test.** Play one take, once. Ask the two questions and write the answers
word for word. Then play the other take — to a **different** person, because the first listener
now knows the story.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take that keeps its anchors**. One
*yesterday* at the front rescues it, and the listener places it perfectly. Remove every time word
and let the verbs do the work alone.

The second is **a listener who is being kind**:

> Well, I suppose it was yesterday?

Ask them to say what they actually thought while listening, not what they worked out afterwards.

The third, in real speech, is **the slip that arrives with emotion**. A learner who tells a calm
day in perfect past tense will slide into the present the moment the story is about something that
made them angry. That is worth knowing, because complaints are exactly where being placed in time
matters most.

The fourth is **fixing it by slowing down**. The slip is not caused by speed; it is caused by the
present forms being the ones you learned first. Slowing down produces careful sentences in the
wrong tense.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers do move a story into the present on purpose — *so he says to me* — and it is a
strong move. The difference is the frame: they place the story in the past in the first sentence,
and the present-tense part sits inside that frame, where the listener already knows when they are.

Under pressure, tense is the first thing to go and the last thing anybody complains about. A
listener will ask you what happened, when, and what you want done — and they will never mention
the verb. That is why this check needs a question, not an opinion.

What a native listener actually notices is confusion about *now*. If your story could be happening
at this moment, some part of them stays alert, waiting to be asked to do something. It is a small
discomfort, it is constant, and it is entirely fixed by six verbs changing shape.
