## One-line answer

Most English verbs make their past by adding *-ed*, and the four spelling changes that go with it
— drop nothing, add *-d*, double the consonant, or turn *y* into *ied* — are decided by the last
two letters of the verb, not by the meaning.

## The story

A register sits by the door of a small workshop. Each day, whoever closes writes one line about
what happened.

Monday's line says:

> Ravi stoped the machine at four. I tryed the new belt. It worked.

Nobody sends it back. Everybody understands it. But it goes into a book that stays, and anybody
who opens that book in a year will read those two words before they read the sentence.

The next line, written after somebody looked the rule up:

> Ravi stopped the machine at four. I tried the new belt. It worked.

Three verbs, three different spellings, and only one of them is simply *verb + ed*.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **regular verb** — a verb whose past is made by adding *-ed*: *work* → *worked*.
- **irregular verb** — a verb whose past is a different word: *go* → *went*. They have their own
  day.
- **vowel** — the letters *a*, *e*, *i*, *o*, *u*.
- **consonant** — every other letter.
- **infinitive** — the plain form of the verb, with nothing added: *work*, *stop*, *try*.

Four spelling rules, decided by how the verb ends.

| The verb ends in | What you do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| most things | add *-ed* | work → worked |
| *-e* | add *-d* only | like → liked |
| a vowel then a consonant | double the consonant, then *-ed* | stop → stopped |
| a consonant then *-y* | *y* becomes *ied* | try → tried |
| a vowel then *-y* | just add *-ed* | play → played |

The last two rows are one rule with two halves, and the half people forget is the second: *play*
keeps its *y* because the letter before it is a vowel.

**And one thing that does not change at all:** the past form is the same for everybody. *I worked,
you worked, he worked, we worked, they worked.* No extra *s* anywhere, ever.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple – regular verbs](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/past-simple-regular-verbs).
It states the rule and its exceptions in these words:

> Regular past simple forms are formed by adding -ed to the infinitive of the verb.
> If a verb ends in -e, you add -d. (agree → agreed, like → liked, escape → escaped)
> If a verb ends in a vowel and a consonant, the consonant is usually doubled before -ed.
> (stop → stopped, plan → planned)
> If a verb ends in consonant and -y, you take off the y and add -ied. (try → tried, carry →
> carried)
> But if the word ends in a vowel and -y, you add -ed. (play → played, enjoy → enjoyed)
> The past simple form doesn't change at all for I, you, he, she, we and they.

**A second page, opened the same day**, for the sentences this is used in:
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple),
which gives *called*, *liked*, *wanted* and *worked* as regular examples, and:

> When I was a boy, I **walked** a mile to school every day.

**How to decide, in two steps**, looking only at the end of the verb:

```text
   Look at the LAST letter.
     ends in e         →  + d            like → liked
     ends in y         →  look at the letter BEFORE the y
                            consonant + y   →  y becomes ied     carry → carried
                            vowel + y       →  + ed              enjoy → enjoyed
     anything else     →  look at the last TWO letters
                            vowel + consonant  →  double it, + ed   plan → planned
                            otherwise          →  + ed              work → worked
```

**The doubling rule needs care with longer verbs.** *Stop* and *plan* are one-syllable verbs and
they double. For verbs of two syllables the answer depends on which syllable is stressed, and the
page checked above does not state that case. `TODO`: look up the two-syllable doubling rule —

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the missing double letter**:

> I stoped at the shop. He planed it last week.

Both are understood, and both change the look of a page. *Planed* is also a real English word,
which is worse: a reader meets a word that exists and means something else before they work out
what you meant.

The second is ***tryed* and *studyed***:

> I tryed to call you. I studyed in the evening.

The *y* rule is the one that goes missing most, because it is the only rule here that changes a
letter rather than adding one.

The third is **an extra *s* on the past**:

> He workeds late. She calleds me.

This comes from the present-tense habit — *he works*, *she calls* — travelling into the past,
where nothing takes an *s*.

The fourth is ***-ed* added to a verb that has its own past**:

> I goed home. She taked the bus.

These are irregular verbs and they have their own day in this folder. What is worth knowing now is
that adding *-ed* to them is not a small error: it produces a word that does not exist, and a
reader stops on it.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Readers do not think about spelling rules; they recognise word shapes. *Stoped* is the wrong shape
and the eye catches it in the same way it catches a missing letter in your own name. That is why
this matters more on a page that stays — a form, a register, an email — than in a message that is
read once and swiped away.

Under pressure, writers double nothing. Typing fast, everybody writes *stoped* and *planed* and
*tryed*, and the fix is not to type more carefully; it is to know the four rules well enough that
the correct shape comes out first.

A formal reader is unforgiving here in a particular way: they will not mention it, and they will
read the rest of your writing more slowly. Spelling is the part of a page that is judged before
the content, which is unfair and completely reliable.
