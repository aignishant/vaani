## One-line answer

Write yesterday as eight true sentences in the past, then check them in two separate passes — one
for the verbs, one for everything else — because checking both at once is how wrong spellings
survive a re-read.

## The story

The register by the workshop door has a rule nobody wrote down: one line a day, and the line has
to be true.

Somebody who is new writes their first entry and it comes out like this:

> Today is very busy. We are finishing the order and the belt is breaking again.

It is Tuesday's line, written on Tuesday evening, about Tuesday — and it is written as though the
belt is breaking while you read it. Next March, when somebody goes back through the book to find
out when the belt last went, that line will be no use at all.

The register wants the past, because a register is read later. That is the whole reason this
folder is about writing yesterday rather than saying it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **entry** — one dated piece of writing in a book or a diary.
- **pass** — one read-through of your own writing, looking for one kind of thing.
- **verb pass** — a read-through that looks only at verbs.
- **true** — actually what happened, not what would look better.

The work is three steps, and the third is two separate passes.

1. **List the events.** Eight things that actually happened yesterday, in order, as short notes.
2. **Write them as sentences**, in the past, with a capital letter and a full stop on each.
3. **Check twice:**
   - **the verb pass** — every verb, one at a time, by the four rules from
     [part 2.1](2.1-add-ed-and-what-happens-to-the-spelling.md);
   - **the everything-else pass** — capitals, full stops, names, numbers.

Two passes, because a reader checking spelling and punctuation together does neither.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3's writing is all in the past: a story in six sentences, a memory, a trip. This is the
smallest version of it, and it is where the spelling either becomes automatic or does not.

And there is a practical reason too. A dated line about what happened is the most useful thing
anybody writes: a repair, a complaint, a form, an insurance claim, a conversation with a landlord
about what was said and when. A page with dates and past-tense verbs settles arguments.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple – regular verbs](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/past-simple-regular-verbs),
which gives the spelling rules used in the verb pass, and:

> The past simple form doesn't change at all for I, you, he, she, we and they.

**Step one — the list.** Eight events, as notes, not sentences:

```text
   opened at 8
   belt slipped again
   called the supplier - no answer
   waited till 11
   changed the belt myself
   tested it twice
   finished the small order
   locked up at 6
```

Notes are allowed to be ugly. They are not the deliverable.

**Step two — the sentences.** Turn each note into a full sentence with a subject:

> I opened the workshop at eight.
> The belt slipped again before ten.
> I called the supplier, but nobody answered.

Notice the second one: a note says *belt slipped*, and a sentence needs *the belt*. Notes drop
words that sentences need, which is why this step exists.

**Step three, pass one — the verb pass.** Put a mark over every verb and check each one alone:

Do not read the sentences while you do this. Read only the verbs. It feels strange and it is the
point: a verb inside a sentence you wrote is almost invisible to you.

**Step three, pass two — everything else.** Capital letters at the start, full stops at the end,
names and places with capitals, numbers written the way you would say them.

**Then the one extra sentence that makes it writing rather than an exercise:** end the entry with
a line about what it means for today.

> The belt needs replacing properly before Friday.

**And the second entry.** Do this again for a different day this week, so the eight sentences are
not the only eight you have ever written in the past.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the entry that slides into the present halfway**:

> I opened at eight. The belt slipped. I call the supplier and he is not answering.

The first two verbs moved and the rest did not. On a page this is more visible than in speech,
because a reader sees both tenses at once.

The second is **notes handed in as sentences**:

> Opened at 8. Belt slipped. Called supplier no answer.

Useful to you, useless to anybody else, and not writing. Every sentence needs a subject and a full
stop.

The third is **the verb pass that never happened**. The page is read once, from the top, for
meaning, and every *stoped* in it survives, because you are reading the story you already know.

The fourth is **eight sentences that are all the same shape**:

> I opened the shop. I called the supplier. I waited. I changed the belt. I tested it. I finished
> the order. I locked up.

All correct, all starting with *I*. Vary the opening: a time word, a thing as the subject, a
joined sentence from the four joining words you already use.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Anybody who keeps a record — a shop, a clinic, a site office — writes exactly this: dated,
past-tense, one line per event. The reason is never grammar. It is that somebody will read it in
six months to find out what happened, and a line in the present tense cannot be placed.

Under pressure people write notes rather than sentences, and notes are fine in your own book. The
moment the page is going to somebody else — a landlord, an office, an insurer — the notes have to
become sentences, and that conversion is the skill practised here.

A formal reader reads a dated past-tense account as evidence. The same facts written in the
present read as an opinion about now. That difference has nothing to do with English teaching and
everything to do with what the page can be used for.

## Check yourself

- **Write:** `TODO(me)` Write eight true sentences about yesterday, in order, from a list of notes
  you make first. Run the verb pass on its own and fill in the verb table, checking every spelling
  by the four rules and in a dictionary where you are unsure. Then run the everything-else pass.
  Add the closing line about what it means for today. Then write a second entry, for a different
  day this week, and vary the sentence openings so that not all of them start with *I*.
- **Say out loud:** Read your eight sentences aloud. Say which one would be useful to somebody
