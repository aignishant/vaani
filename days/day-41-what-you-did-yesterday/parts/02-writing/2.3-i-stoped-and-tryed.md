## One-line answer

Write the entry with every *-ed* spelling broken on purpose, then hand both versions to a real
reader and ask them to mark anything that looks wrong — because you cannot proofread your own
spelling by reading, and a real reader's pen is the only instrument you have.

## The story

Two pages from the same register, copied out for somebody who has to sign them.

Page one:

> I stoped the machine at four. I tryed the new belt and it worked. We planed to finish the order
> on Friday.

Page two:

> I stopped the machine at four. I tried the new belt and it worked. We planned to finish the
> order on Friday.

The person signing reads both and signs both, because both say the same thing. Then, while
signing, they say:

> Your keyboard eating letters?

That sentence is the finding. Not *this is wrong* — nobody says that — but the small note that
somebody noticed, which is what wrong spelling actually costs on a page that stays.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **proofread** — to read your own writing looking only for mistakes.
- **blind spot** — something you cannot see because you wrote it.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will produce three pages about the same real day.

- **Page A — the broken page.** Every *-ed* spelling rule broken: *stoped*, *tryed*, *planed*,
  *studyed*, *carryed*. Everything else correct.
- **Page B — your own unchecked page.** The eight sentences from
  [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-yesterday.md) **before** you ran the verb pass. Keep that version; do
  not clean it up.
- **Page C — the checked page.** After both passes.

Then a real reader marks anything that looks wrong on each page, and you count what they caught.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3's pages are longer than this — a story, a memory, a trip — and long pages hide spelling
better. The habit that catches it is built here, on eight sentences, or it is not built at all.

And the ledger row from this part is one of the most useful in the whole course, because it is
about your own hand: *the rules I actually break are the y rule and the doubling rule, four times
in eight sentences.*

## The rule

**Write page A by breaking rules, not by inventing words.** Take real verbs and apply the wrong
rule to each one, so the page is a test of the four rules rather than a mess:

| Rule broken | Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| doubling | stoped, planed | stopped, planned |
| consonant + *y* | tryed, studyed | tried, studied |
| verb ending in *-e* | likeed, moveed | liked, moved |
| vowel + *y* | plaied, enjoied | played, enjoyed |

**Then the reader test.** Give each page to a real person — one page each, three people if you
can, or the same person with a day between — and say exactly this:

> Mark anything that looks wrong. You don't have to explain it.

Take the pages back and count.

| B — my unchecked page | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| C — after both passes | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when your own unchecked page B contains
at least one wrong *-ed* spelling that you did not see and a reader did. It is **GREEN** when page
C comes back with nothing marked on it.

Page B is the real exercise. Page A only proves that readers notice; page B tells you whether you
do.

**Then the pattern count.** Go back through page B and every piece of writing in your lab folder
from the last week, and count your wrong *-ed* spellings by rule:

> Doubling: `TODO(me)` · consonant + y: `TODO(me)` · ending in e: `TODO(me)` · vowel + y:
> `TODO(me)`

Most people have one rule that accounts for nearly all of it. That rule is yours to drill.

**Then the drill.** For the rule you break most, write twenty past forms of real verbs you use,
checking each one in a dictionary, and then five true sentences using five of them.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **cleaning up page B before showing it**. The
unchecked page is the one with the information in it. Keep it exactly as it was.

The second is **a reader who marks everything**, including things that are correct, because they
have been told to look for mistakes. Ask them to mark only what looks wrong to them without
thinking about it, and to leave anything they are unsure of.

The third, in real writing, is **proofreading by reading**. Your eye repairs your own text — it
knows what the word should be and shows you that instead. The verb pass exists because it stops
you reading: verbs only, one at a time, out of order if necessary.

The fourth is **relying on a red underline**. Spell-checking catches *tryed*. It does not catch
*planed*, because *planed* is a real word, and *planed* is the one that makes a reader stop.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Readers notice spelling in the first sentence and then stop noticing, and what they are left with
is an impression rather than a list. Nobody will tell you that your *-ed* forms are wrong. They
will read the rest of the page more slowly and reply more carefully.

Under pressure, nobody proofreads at all. The habit that survives pressure is not a careful
re-read; it is a single mechanical pass on one thing — in this case, the verbs. One pass, one kind
of error, twenty seconds.

A formal reader — a form, an office, an application — is the one place where this costs something
real. A page that stays is read by people you will never meet, at a time you cannot predict, and
the register in the story will still be on that shelf in March.
