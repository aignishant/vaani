## One-line answer

The *-ed* ending is spelled one way and said three ways — *t* as in *walked*, *d* as in *played*,
and a whole extra syllable *id* as in *wanted* — and which one comes out is decided by the sound
just before it, not by the spelling.

## The story

Two people are walking back from a cricket ground, talking about the match.

One of them says:

> They play-ed well. We watch-ed the whole thing. I want-ed to stay.

Three past verbs, all given the same extra beat on the end. Nothing is misunderstood — but every
sentence has a small bump in it, like a step in a floor where the tiles change.

The other says the same three sentences:

> They playd well. We watcht the whole thing. I wanted to stay.

Only the third one has an extra beat, and that one has to.

Same spelling on all three. Three different endings coming out of the mouth, and the mouth chose
them without being asked.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **syllable** — one beat of a word: *want* is one, *wan-ted* is two.
- **voiced sound** — a sound made with the voice box buzzing: *b*, *g*, *v*, *z*, *m*, *n*, and
  every vowel. Put a finger on your throat and you can feel it.
- **unvoiced sound** — a sound made with air only, no buzz: *p*, *k*, *f*, *s*, *sh*, *ch*.
- **ending** — the last sound of the base verb, before *-ed* is added.

Three endings, and the rule is about the sound immediately before the *-ed*.

| If the verb ends in this sound | *-ed* is said as | Example | Beats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *t* or *d* | **id** — a new syllable | wanted, needed, started | 2 |
| any other unvoiced sound: *p, k, f, s, sh, ch* | **t** | walked, watched, stopped, asked | 1 |
| any other voiced sound, including all vowels | **d** | played, called, opened, lived | 1 |

The first row is the only one that adds a beat. That is the whole thing most learners get wrong,
in both directions: adding the beat everywhere, or never adding it at all.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3 is spoken in the past. The gate is a recorded story of at least two hundred spoken words,
and in two hundred words of story there are perhaps twenty *-ed* verbs. Twenty small bumps is not
a small thing — it is the difference between a story and a recitation.

And the extra syllable matters for meaning in one place. *Wanted* has two beats; *want* has one. A
listener counts beats faster than they count letters, so the beat is often how they know the verb
was past at all.

Look at what the transcriptions show. *Walked* ends in **t** and is one syllable. *Played* ends in
**d** and is one syllable. *Wanted* has a dot in the middle — *ˈwɒn.tɪd* — because it is two.

**The finger test, which decides every verb you will ever meet.** Say the base verb without the
*-ed*. Hold the last sound. Put two fingers on your throat:

```text
   walk ──  k  ──  no buzz  ──  unvoiced  ──→   t     walkt
   play ──  eɪ ──  buzz     ──  voiced    ──→   d     playd
   want ──  t  ──  it IS a t          ──────→   id    wan-ted
   need ──  d  ──  it IS a d          ──────→   id    need-ed
```

**Why the third row exists at all.** Try saying *wantd*. Your mouth has to make a *t* and then a
*d* in the same place, with nothing between them, and it cannot — so English puts a small vowel in
between and the word grows a syllable. The rule is not arbitrary; it is what the mouth does.

**The ones that catch people out**, because the spelling lies about the sound:

| Verb | Ends in the sound | Said as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| watched | *ch* (unvoiced) | watcht |
| finished | *sh* (unvoiced) | finisht |
| asked | *k* (unvoiced) | askt |
| opened | *n* (voiced) | opend |
| called | *l* (voiced) | calld |
| lived | *v* (voiced) | livd |

**And a detail from the page above, worth hearing:** the entry for *wanted* also lists a fast
General American form /ˈwɑ.nɪd/, where the *t* itself softens. Fast speech shortens things
further, and none of that changes the beat count. Two beats stay two beats.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the extra beat everywhere**:

> I walk-ed to the ground and watch-ed the match and call-ed my brother.

Every verb given two beats. Nothing is misunderstood, and the whole sentence sounds like a reading
exercise. This is the single most recognisable feature of a learner reading past-tense English off
a page.

The second is **no beat anywhere**, which is rarer and costs more:

> I want to stay.

If *wanted* loses its extra syllable, it becomes *want*, and the sentence moves into the present.
The listener now thinks you still want to stay.

The third is **the wrong one of the pair**, saying *d* where *t* belongs:

> I askd. I finishd.

This is a small thing and it is audible. The mouth has to change from an unvoiced sound to a
voiced one in the same breath, and it comes out heavy.

The fourth is **adding the beat only to long words**, which is a habit rather than a rule:
*managed* gets two beats, *walked* gets one. The length of the word has nothing to do with it —
only the last sound does.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

No native speaker has ever thought about this rule, and every native speaker follows it perfectly,
because it comes from what the mouth can do easily. That is why it is worth drilling rather than
memorising: you are training a reflex, not learning a table.

Under pressure and at speed, the *t* and *d* endings get even smaller — sometimes almost nothing
before another consonant, so *walked back* comes out close to *walk back*. The *id* ending never
disappears, because a syllable cannot be swallowed the way a single consonant can. That is another
reason the two-beat verbs carry so much of the past for a listener.

What a native listener notices is not the ending itself. It is the number of beats in the
sentence. A sentence with three extra beats in it sounds carefully assembled, and people
unconsciously slow their own speech down to match — which makes every conversation you have
slightly heavier than it needed to be.
