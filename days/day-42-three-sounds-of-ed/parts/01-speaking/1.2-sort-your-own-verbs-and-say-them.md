## One-line answer

Take the verbs you actually use, sort them with the finger test before you say them, and then say
them inside real sentences — because the ending only becomes automatic in the middle of a
sentence, where you are not thinking about it.

## The story

Back from the ground, the match still being argued about.

> We batted first. They dropped two catches. We finished at about a hundred and forty. Then it
> rained, and everybody waited under the trees, and in the end they called it off.

Six past verbs in three sentences, and the endings come out as *id*, *t*, *t*, *id*, *id*, *d* —
in that order, without anybody choosing.

That is the target. Not a list of verbs said correctly one at a time, but a sentence that arrives
with its endings already in place while your attention is on the match.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **sort** — to put things into groups.
- **drill** — a short repeated practice of one thing.
- **in context** — inside a real sentence, not alone.
- **automatic** — happening without you deciding it.

There are three stages, and skipping the first is why lists do not transfer to speech.

1. **Sort silently.** Decide the group with the finger test, on paper, before your mouth is
   involved.
2. **Say the group as a block.** Four verbs from the same group, one after another, so the ending
   settles.
3. **Say them in sentences about your own life**, mixed, so no two next to each other are from the
   same group.

Stage three is where it gets hard and where the learning happens. Mixed order is how English
actually arrives.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3's gate recording is a story, and a story runs. Nobody in a story has time to work out
which ending a verb takes. Either it is already there or the story slows down every time a past
verb appears.

And this is one of the few sound rules in English with no exceptions worth teaching. Drill it
properly for one day and it holds for the rest of the course.

## The rule

**The three pages checked on 2026-09-23**, one per group, each with a sound file to listen to
before you record:
[walked](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/walked) — /wɔːkt/, one syllable;
[played](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/played) — /pleɪd/, one syllable;
[wanted](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/wanted) — /ˈwɒn.tɪd/, two.

**Stage one — the sorting sheet.** Write your own verbs into three columns before saying a word:

```text
   ── t ──────────┬── d ──────────┬── id ─────────
   walked         │ played        │ wanted
   watched        │ called        │ needed
   asked          │ opened        │ started
   finished       │ lived         │ waited
   stopped        │ cleaned       │ decided
   ───────────────┴───────────────┴───────────────
```

Use **your** verbs. The ones in that sheet are examples; the drill only works on words you will
actually say this week.

**Stage two — block practice.** Say four from one column, twice through, with the recorder on.
Keep the *t* verbs short and clean. Keep the *id* verbs two beats, not three.

**Stage three — mixed sentences.** One verb per sentence, about your own life, with the columns
alternating:

> I finished the work before six.        ← t
> Then I called my sister.               ← d
> She wanted to come on Sunday.          ← id
> So I cleaned the back room.            ← d

**The hard case worth its own drill**: a past verb followed by a word that starts with the same
sound. English does not say the sound twice; it holds it once, slightly longer:

> I walked to the corner.   → *walkt-to* becomes one held *t*
> She called Latha.         → *calld-La* holds the *l*

**And the negative case, which sounds nothing like the rule**: after *didn't*, the verb goes back
to its plain form and the *-ed* disappears completely — *I didn't finish*, not *I didn't
finished*. The grammar of that has its own day later in the phase. What matters today is that your
mouth should not reach for an ending there at all.

**Record a real account, not a list.** Six sentences about something that actually happened this
week, with at least one verb from each column in it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **correct in the list and wrong in the sentence**. You say
*walked, watched, asked* perfectly as a block, and then in a sentence it comes out *walk-ed*,
because the attention went to the meaning.

The second is **a pause before every past verb**:

> I … finished the work and then I … called her.

The mouth is checking the rule before each one. That is stage two happening during stage three,
and the fix is more block practice rather than more care.

The third is **the three-beat *id***:

> want-ted, need-ded

The extra syllable is small — *id*, said fast and light. If it comes out as a full stressed beat,
the verb ends up longer than the words around it.

The fourth is **giving the ending to a verb that does not have one**:

> I goed. I thinked. I buyed.

These are irregular verbs. They do not take *-ed* at all, and the writing folder today is about
exactly that.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Native speech blurs these endings heavily. *Finished the* comes out closer to *finish-the* with a
single soft *sh-t*, and nobody hears a missing sound, because the beat count is right. That is
what you are training: the count, not the crispness.

Under pressure the *id* ending is the one to protect. If a listener needs to know whether
something is finished — *I waited an hour*, *I needed it yesterday* — that second beat is carrying
the whole fact.

What a native listener notices, once you have this, is nothing at all, which is the point. Endings
are invisible when they are right and are the first thing heard when they are not.
