## One-line answer

Say a real account with an extra beat on every past verb, on purpose, then play both versions to a
real person and ask which speaker sounds like they are reading — because this failure never
confuses anybody and changes how people talk back to you.

## The story

Two recordings of the same three sentences about the same match.

> We watch-ed the whole thing. They drop-ed two catches. In the end they call-ed it off.

> We watcht the whole thing. They dropt two catches. In the end they calld it off.

Play both to somebody and they will tell you the meaning is identical, because it is. Then ask
which one sounds like somebody reading aloud and they answer immediately, without being able to
say why.

That gap — no confusion, instant impression — is what this part is for. It is the commonest
learner sound in past-tense English, and it is invisible from the inside, because the extra beat
feels like carefulness.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **impression** — what a listener feels about a speaker, without deciding it.
- **beat count** — how many syllables a sentence has.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will record the same real account twice.

- **The RED take.** Six sentences, at least eight past verbs, every single one given the *id*
  ending: *walk-ed*, *play-ed*, *call-ed*, *finish-ed*.
- **The GREEN take.** The same six sentences with the three endings from
  [part 1.1](1.1-t-d-and-id.md) in their right places.

Then a real person hears both and answers two questions — one about meaning, one about
impression.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story of at least two hundred spoken words. That is long enough for
an impression to form and settle, and the impression decides how the listener speaks back — slowly
and simply, or normally.

And the ledger wants this one written down precisely, because it is a habit and not a mistake.
*Eight verbs, eight extra beats, and a real listener called it reading* is a row you can drill
against.

## The rule

**Build the account so the failure is visible.** Six sentences, about something that really
happened, with at least eight past verbs in them and at least two from each of the three groups.

**Record the RED take first**, with every ending as *id*. Do it deliberately and evenly — this is
not a caricature; it is what a careful reader produces naturally.

**Then the GREEN take**, same words, right endings.

**Then the listener test.** Play both, in a different order for each listener, and ask exactly
these two questions:

> Did you understand both? Is anything different in what they mean?
> Which one sounds like somebody reading aloud?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take that is secretly correct**. Your mouth
has been drilling all day and refuses to add the beats. Read the six sentences off the page slowly
— reading aloud produces the failure by itself, which is the whole point of the failure.

The second is **asking the listener which one is better**. That invites politeness. Ask which one
sounds like reading; it is a question about fact, and people answer it honestly.

The third, in real speech, is **the habit returning whenever the content is difficult**. A speaker
who has this right in easy sentences will add beats again while describing something complicated,
because the attention moves. That is normal, and it is why the self-check pass exists.

The fourth is **over-correcting into no endings at all**:

> I want to stay. I need it yesterday.

Told that they add too many beats, learners start removing them everywhere, including from the
*id* group, where the beat is compulsory and carries the past.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

The extra beat never causes a misunderstanding. Not once, in any conversation you will have. That
makes it easy to dismiss and expensive to keep, because what it costs is not accuracy but the way
people pitch their English at you.

Under pressure — reading something aloud, giving an account to an official, speaking in front of
a group — the beats come back first, because all three of those situations push a speaker towards
carefulness, and carefulness is exactly what produces this.

What a native listener notices is rhythm, always. They will describe a speaker with this habit as
*formal*, *careful*, *very correct* — never as wrong. And they will answer more slowly and choose
smaller words, which is the real price of the habit.
