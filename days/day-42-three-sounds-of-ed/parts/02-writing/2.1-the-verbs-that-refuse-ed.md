## One-line answer

About two hundred English verbs make their past by changing shape instead of adding *-ed* — *go →
went*, *buy → bought*, *think → thought* — and roughly twenty of them are the ones you cannot get
through a week without, so those twenty are learned as words, not as a rule.

## The story

A message has to explain what happened with a booking.

> I buyed the ticket on Monday. Then I goed to the counter and they telled me the train is
> cancelled. I thinked they will give the money back.

Every sentence is understandable, and every verb in it is a word that does not exist. A reader
gets the meaning and gets something else at the same time: the writer has built four words out of
a rule, and English refused all four.

> I bought the ticket on Monday. Then I went to the counter and they told me the train was
> cancelled. I thought they would give the money back.

The facts did not move. Four words changed into four different words, and that is exactly what
irregular verbs are: different words that have to be known.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **irregular verb** — a verb whose past is a different word, not *verb + ed*.
- **base form** — the plain verb, with nothing added: *go*, *buy*, *think*.
- **past form** — the finished-time form: *went*, *bought*, *thought*.
- **frequency** — how often a word is used.

There is no rule to learn here, and pretending otherwise wastes weeks. There is a list, it is
short at the top, and the top of it is where almost all of your sentences are.

Why this is worth the effort: the irregular verbs are the **commonest** verbs in English. *Be*,
*have*, *go*, *say*, *get*, *make*, *take*, *see*, *come*, *know* — those ten alone carry an
enormous share of everything anybody says. The rule-following verbs are mostly the longer, rarer
ones.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3 is written in the past — a story in six sentences, a memory, a trip, a problem. Every one
of those pages will need *went*, *said*, *saw*, *took*, *had*, *made*. A page with *goed* and
*telled* in it does not read as a learner's page; it reads as a page nobody checked.

And this is the day that makes the rest of the phase possible. The writing that follows assumes
these forms, and no amount of good structure survives four invented words in a paragraph.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Irregular verbs](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference-0).
It carries a reference table of the base form, past tense and past participle for around fifty
verbs, and it puts the position plainly:

> most verbs have a past tense and past participle with –ed

and

> many of the most frequent verbs are irregular

with the forms it lists including *go → went*, *have → had*, *see → saw*, *make → made*, *take →
took*, *come → came*, *say → said* and *get → got*.

**The twenty you cannot avoid.** Learn these as twenty separate words, in pairs, out loud:

| Base | Past | | Base | Past |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| be | was / were | | make | made |
| have | had | | take | took |
| go | went | | give | gave |
| say | said | | find | found |
| get | got | | think | thought |
| come | came | | buy | bought |
| see | saw | | bring | brought |
| know | knew | | tell | told |
| do | did | | leave | left |
| eat | ate | | put | put |

**Three things in that table that trip people up**, and each is worth its own look:

1. ***be* has two past forms** — *was* and *were* — which is the only verb in English that does.
   It has its own day later in this phase, and until then: *I was*, *he was*, *we were*, *they
   were*.
2. ***put* does not change at all.** *I put it there yesterday* is past. So are *cut*, *let*,
   *shut*, *cost*. The sentence around them carries the time.
3. ***said*, *told*, *thought*, *bought*, *brought*** are spelled nothing like they sound. The
   writing folder cares about the spelling; the sound is the speaking folder's business.

**Groups that make the list smaller.** Several irregulars move in the same way, and noticing that
is the only shortcut that actually exists:

```text
   -ought / -aught     buy → bought · bring → brought · think → thought · teach → taught
   -old                tell → told  · sell → sold
   i → a               sit → sat    · drink → drank · begin → began
   ee → e              feel → felt  · keep → kept   · sleep → slept · meet → met
   no change           put → put    · cut → cut     · let → let     · cost → cost
```

**And the rule that catches even good learners:** after *did* or *didn't*, **no past form at
all**. The verb goes back to its base:

> Did you go? — not *did you went*
> I didn't see him. — not *I didn't saw*

That has a day of its own in this folder later in the phase. It is named here because half of all
irregular-verb errors in real writing are actually this error.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the invented regular past**:

> I goed. He telled me. They buyed it. She thinked about it.

These are not spelling mistakes. They are words that do not exist, and a reader stops on each one.

The second is **the past form used after *did***:

> Did you went to the office? I didn't saw him.

Understood everywhere, wrong everywhere, and the most persistent error in this whole area.

The third is **the past participle used as the past**:

> I have went. I seen him yesterday. I done it.

The table on the page checked above has three columns for a reason: *go, went, gone* and *see,
saw, seen*. The third column is for later days in this course, and borrowing it into a plain past
sentence is one of the errors that stays longest.

The fourth is **half a sentence changing**:

> Yesterday I go to the market and bought rice.

One verb moved, one did not. On paper both are visible at once, which makes it look careless
rather than foreign.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Nobody learns these from a table alone. They arrive through repetition in real sentences, which is
why the next part makes you write your own week with them rather than copy the list out.

Under pressure, learners fall back on *-ed* because it is a rule and rules are what you reach for
when you are thinking about something else. That is why the twenty are drilled as words: a word
comes back whole, a rule has to be applied.

A formal reader treats *goed* differently from *stoped*. A spelling slip reads as haste; an
invented verb reads as not knowing, and it is the single fastest way for a written page to be
downgraded by somebody skimming it.
