## One-line answer

Write a real account of something that went wrong, using at least ten irregular verbs, and check
each one against the list before you send it — because these forms only stick when they arrive
inside sentences you actually needed to write.

## The story

The booking message gets written properly, and it has a job: somebody has to understand what
happened and do something about it.

> I bought the ticket on Monday morning. On Tuesday I got a message saying the train was
> cancelled. I went to the counter on Wednesday and they told me to write to this address. I left
> a copy of the ticket with them. I brought the receipt home and I have it here.

Five sentences, six irregular verbs, and every one of them was needed. Nobody chose these verbs
to practise them; they are simply the verbs that describe what happened.

That is the difference between a list and writing. On a list, *brought* is a word to remember. In
that message, *brought* is the reason they cannot ask you for the receipt again.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **account** — a written record of what happened.
- **sequence** — the order events happened in.
- **check against** — to compare your writing with a reliable list.
- **useful** — able to be acted on by the person who reads it.

The work is four steps.

1. **Choose something that actually went wrong** — a delivery, a booking, a repair, a bill, an
   appointment. Something with a sequence in it.
2. **Write it in order**, with dates, in the past.
3. **Underline every verb** and mark the irregular ones.
4. **Check each marked verb against the list**, one at a time, before the page is finished.

Step four is where the learning is. Checking twelve verbs one at a time is slow on day one and
takes seconds by day sixty.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past, and phase 4 asks for arguments and explanations
that lean on the same verbs. Everything written from here to day 120 uses these twenty.

And an account of something that went wrong is the most useful page in ordinary life. It is what
you send to a landlord, an office, a company or a school, and it is the form of writing where the
order of events and the dates carry everything.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Irregular verbs](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference-0),
whose table gives the base form, past tense and past participle for around fifty verbs, including
*go → went*, *have → had*, *see → saw*, *make → made*, *take → took*, *come → came*, *say → said*
and *get → got*, and which states that many of the most frequent verbs are irregular.

**Step one — the sequence, as dated notes:**

```text
   Mon 14   bought ticket online
   Tue 15   message - train cancelled
   Wed 16   went to counter - told me to write
   Wed 16   left copy of ticket
   Thu 17   no reply yet
```

Dates first. A note with no date is a memory; a note with a date is a record.

**Step two — the sentences.** One event per sentence, in order, each with a subject and a full
stop. Keep the dates in:

> I bought the ticket on Monday the fourteenth.
> On Tuesday I got a message saying the train was cancelled.

**Step three — underline and mark.** Every verb underlined; irregular ones circled. In a
ten-sentence account you will usually find six to ten irregulars, which is more than most people
expect.

**Step four — check them one at a time**, against the list, not from memory:

**Then the two checks that are not about verbs**, because an account is judged on usefulness:

- **Is every date there?** A sentence that says *later* instead of *on Thursday* cannot be acted
  on.
- **Is the last sentence about what you want?** An account with no request is a story.

> I would be grateful if you could confirm the refund by Friday.

**Then write a second, shorter account** of a different real thing, in five sentences, aiming for
five irregular verbs. Two accounts is what makes it practice rather than a one-off.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **avoiding the verbs you are unsure of**:

> I purchased the ticket. I proceeded to the counter. I submitted the receipt.

Every irregular verb has been swapped for a long, formal, regular one. The page is now correct,
heavy, and nothing was learned. Write *bought*, *went*, *gave*.

The second is **an account with no order**:

> The train was cancelled. I have the receipt. I bought the ticket on Monday. They told me to
> write.

Four true sentences in no sequence, so the reader has to rebuild the week themselves.

The third is **checking the verbs at the end, all at once**, by reading the page. The eye slides
over verbs inside sentences you wrote. Circle them first and check them out of context.

The fourth is **the mixed tense inside one sentence**:

> I went to the counter and they tell me to write.

The second half stayed in the present. On a page this is more visible than in speech, and in a
complaint it weakens the whole account.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Accounts like this get forwarded. Somebody at a counter sends your message to somebody else, and
the second reader has none of the context — only your dates and your sequence. That is why the
plain verbs matter more than the polite ones: *went*, *told*, *left* survive forwarding, and long
formal words just make it slower to read.

Under pressure, in a complaint about something expensive, writers reach for formal vocabulary they
do not control and produce sentences that are harder to act on. The strongest complaint is a
sequence of short past-tense sentences with dates.

A formal reader reads for two things: what happened, and what you want. If the irregular verbs are
wrong, they will still find both — and they will read the page as one written by somebody who can
be put off, which changes what happens next.
