## One-line answer

Write the account with *goed*, *telled*, *buyed* and *thinked* in it on purpose, then write the
version that avoids every irregular verb instead — and give both to a real reader, because the
second failure is the one you will actually commit for the next ten years.

## The story

Three versions of the same message about the cancelled train reach three people.

The first says *I buyed the ticket and goed to the counter and they telled me to write*. The
reader understands it, and answers slowly, in short words, the way people do when they have
decided how much English you have.

The second says *I purchased the ticket and proceeded to the counter, where I was advised to
submit a written communication*. Every word is correct. It sounds like a form, and the reader
cannot find what actually happened.

The third says *I bought the ticket and went to the counter, and they told me to write in*. It
gets answered first.

The interesting failure is the second one, because it is invisible. Nobody ever corrects it. It is
what learners do the moment they are frightened of the irregular verbs, and it makes every page
they write heavier for the rest of their lives.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **avoidance** — going round a word you are not sure of instead of using it.
- **plain** — using ordinary short words.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Three versions of your own account from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-with-the-twenty.md), same facts, same order.

- **Note A — the invented pasts.** *Goed*, *telled*, *buyed*, *thinked*, *bringed*. At least five.
- **Note B — the avoidance version.** Not one irregular verb anywhere. Every one replaced by a
  long regular verb: *purchased*, *proceeded*, *submitted*, *communicated*, *received*.
- **Note C — the real account**, as you wrote and checked it.

Then a real reader reads one version each and answers two questions: **what happened**, and
**what do they want you to do**.

## The rule

and everything else correct, so the test is about the verbs and nothing else.

part of the finding. Keep the facts identical.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **note B written with plain verbs sneaking in**.
*Had* and *got* slip past unnoticed because they are so common. Check note B against the twenty
before you hand it over.

The second is **one reader for all three**. By the third version they know the story and every
version reads perfectly. One reader per note.

The third, in real writing, is **avoidance that looks like progress**. A learner's writing gets
longer, more formal and more correct, and gets less done. Nobody flags it, because there is
nothing to flag.

The fourth is **fixing the invented pasts by writing shorter sentences**:

> Ticket bought Monday. Train cancelled. Went counter.

The verbs are now right and the sentences have stopped being sentences. An account needs subjects
and full stops.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Plain past-tense verbs are what get read. *I went, they told me, I left a copy* — a reader takes
those in without effort, and effort is the whole currency of a page somebody did not choose to
read.

Under pressure, both failures appear in the same paragraph: an invented past in the middle of a
formal sentence — *I proceeded to the counter and they telled me to wait*. That combination is
common, and it tells a reader that the formality was borrowed.

A formal reader is not impressed by long verbs. They are responsible for acting on your page, and
they will act faster on twelve short sentences with dates in them than on six long ones. The
irregular verbs are not a hurdle to get past; they are the words that make writing readable.
