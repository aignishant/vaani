# Digging where the ground is already soft

`IN-08` · Day 43

## The one-line answer

A follow-up is a question built out of a word the other person has just said, aimed at the part
they warmed up on — and it is the difference between a conversation that keeps going and one that
has to be started again every turn.

## The model

Every first answer is topsoil.

Ask somebody about their job and you get the version they have given fifty times: thin, tidy,
polished by repetition, and completely dead. Ask about their weekend and you get *yeah, quiet,
nothing much*. This is not them being closed. It is the top layer, and the top layer is the same
everywhere. Nothing grows in it because nothing was ever planted there.

Everything you actually want is one spade down. And the whole skill today is that **you do not dig
at random.** You dig where the ground is already soft.

**Soft ground sounds like something.** In any answer, one or two words come out differently from
the rest. They get a little more time. The voice moves further on them — that is yesterday's tune,
arriving as information. Sometimes they say the word twice. Sometimes they start to explain and
then stop, because they assumed you were not interested. That is the soft spot, and it is where
your next question goes. Everything else in the answer is packed hard, and a question there gets
you a fact and a full stop.

**The cheapest spade there is: their own word, lifted.** Take the word they warmed up on and say it
back with the rise from Day 26. Nothing else.

> Her brother?
> On your own?

One or two words, and it is a whole question. It costs you no planning, which matters, because
planning is exactly what you cannot do while listening. And it proves you were listening in a way
that no amount of nodding does — their word came back out of your mouth.

**Ask for the story, not the fact.** This is the one that changes conversations. A fact question
has an answer, and when the answer arrives, the ground closes over. *Who else was there?* —
*Just the two of us.* Done. A story question has no natural end.

> What was the worst bit?
> What's the story there?
> So how did that go?

Same subject, opposite result. Notice that none of these is harder to say than the fact version.
You are not learning a difficult sentence today. You are learning to point the easy one somewhere
better.

**Not every follow-up is a question.** A guess works, often better:

> You must have been furious.

People correct a wrong guess far more readily than they answer a right question — a guess gives
them something to push against, and pushing is easy. Say it with the voice landing on the floor, as
a statement. If you lift it, it becomes a question again and you have lost the effect.

**Name the heat when you have heard it twice.** If the same thing comes up in two different
answers, that is not a coincidence and it is not small.

> That's the second time you've mentioned {{…}}.

Say it and then stop. What follows is usually the best thing you will hear all evening, and you got
there by noticing rather than by being clever.

**Today's phrases** — all of them go one layer down, and all of them are built to be said without a
run-up. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| So how did that go? | They named the event but never said how it ended |
| What's the story there? | A detail that plainly has something behind it |
| Back up a bit — {{the thing they skipped}}? | They went straight past the interesting part |
| Was that the first time? | Turning one event into a pattern, which is where the good answers live |
| What was the worst bit? | Asking for the story rather than the summary |
| You must have been {{…}}. | A guess they can correct — not a question at all, and stronger for it |
| And is that still going on? | Bringing something they said in the past tense into now |
| That's the second time you've mentioned {{…}}. | Naming the heat you have already heard twice |

## Say it now

Use one real thing somebody has actually told you. The same subject for all eight lines, so you can
hear the ground opening.

1. *{{their word}}?* — just the word, lifted.
2. *So how did that go?*
3. *What's the story there — {{the detail}}?*
4. *Back up a bit — {{the thing they skipped}}?*
5. *Was that the first time?*
6. *What was the worst bit?*
7. *You must have been {{a guess at how they felt}}.* — landed, not lifted.
8. *That's the second time you've mentioned {{the thing}}.*

Then run the eight again, this time as if the answers were arriving in between — leave a real gap
after each one and only then say the next. A follow-up said into a gap sounds like interest. Eight
follow-ups said in a row without gaps sound like a form being filled in, and you want to have heard
both.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d43-free.m4a`.**

> **Three things, two follow-ups each.** Take three things somebody has actually told you recently.
> Say each one out loud in a sentence or two, the way they told it. Then ask it two follow-ups: the
> first built out of a word you have just said, the second asking for the story rather than the
> fact. Never answer them.

- **Solo form:** as written. If you have a voice message somebody has sent you, use that instead —
  play it, stop it, and ask into the silence. The silence is exactly where a follow-up has to
  arrive in real life, and it is shorter than you think.
- **With a partner:** they talk about anything and you ask only. For the whole rep you are not
  allowed one sentence about yourself. This is harder than it sounds and it is the version that
  finds out whether you are listening or waiting.

## The deliberate failure

Pick one subject and ask five fact questions in a row. When, where, how many, who with, how long.
Nothing else.

**What breaking sounds like:** an interview, and a bad one. With a partner you will hear the answers
shrink — a sentence, then a few words, then one word, each delivered a little more politely than the
last. On your own you will hear something almost as useful: all five of your questions have the same
tune. Flat, quick, downward, the tune of somebody checking a list rather than wanting to know.

The finding is not comfortable. **Asking more questions can kill a conversation faster than asking
none**, because every fact question closes the ground it lands on. You have not been too quiet in
those conversations that went nowhere. You have been digging into hard ground with a very small
spade.

Now go back to the same subject and ask one soft-ground question — the story rather than the
fact — and listen to how much longer the answer is. Same person, same subject, one question different.

## When it breaks in the wild

A work social. You are talking to somebody you have just met, you ask the three standard questions,
they answer all three, and then it stops. You are now hunting for a new subject while they wait, and
the silence is doing that thing where it gets heavier the longer you look for something to fill it
with.

**The repair:**

> Back up a bit — you said {{the thing they said earlier}}?

The next subject was never somewhere else. It was in the third sentence they said, and you walked
past it because you were preparing the next question. Going back is not awkward — from their side it
lands as the opposite, because it means the thing they mentioned once was worth remembering.
