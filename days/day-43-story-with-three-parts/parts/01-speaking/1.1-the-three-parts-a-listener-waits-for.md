## One-line answer

A spoken story has three parts a listener is waiting for — where we are, what went wrong, and how
it ended — and a story that skips any one of them leaves the listener holding a piece of your life
with nothing to do with it.

## The story

A barber's chair, a slow afternoon, and somebody two chairs down is telling about his motorbike.

> So I took it to the fellow near the bridge. He said Thursday. I went Thursday. Not ready. I went
> Saturday. Not ready. Third time I just stood there.

Everybody in the shop is listening, including the people who came in halfway. Nobody has been
told a single thing about the motorbike, the fellow, or why it mattered — and nobody needs it,
because the three parts are all there: where we are (a bike at a repair shop), what went wrong
(not ready, twice), and how it ended (he stood there).

Then somebody else tries:

> My cousin's bike also had problem. Actually he bought it in 2019, second hand, from a person in
> our old area, that time the price was …

Two sentences in, the room has gone back to the mirror. Everything he is saying is true. None of
it is any of the three parts.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **story** — an account of something that happened, told so that somebody wants to hear the end.
- **setting** — where and when it happened, and who was there.
- **problem** — the thing that went wrong, or the thing that changed.
- **ending** — how it finished, and what it left behind.
- **detail** — a small fact that makes a moment real.

Three parts, and a listener is holding a space open for each one.

| Part | What it answers | How long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beginning | where were you, when, who with | one or two sentences |
| Middle | what went wrong, and what you did | most of the story |
| End | how it finished, and how you felt | one or two sentences |

The commonest shape a beginner produces is a long beginning, a short middle and no end. The shape
that works is the opposite: a short beginning, a long middle, and an end that lands.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story from your life with a beginning, a middle and an end, of at
least two hundred spoken words. Those three words in the gate are not decoration; they are the
thing being checked.

And a story is how anybody gets to know you. Facts about a person are forgotten in a week. One
story about them standing outside a repair shop for the third time is remembered for years.

## The rule

**The page checked for the verb forms this needs, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple),
which gives the past of regular verbs as *-ed* — *called*, *liked*, *wanted*, *worked* — and shows
irregular pasts in its model sentences:

> I **met** my wife in 1983.
> We **went** to Spain for our holidays.
> They **got** home very late last night.

**The beginning: three things, fast.** Where, when, who. Nothing else:

> Last month I took my bike to a man near the bridge.

That single sentence carries a place, a time and two people. A beginning that needs four sentences
is usually carrying the middle's job.

**The middle: what went wrong, and what you did about it.** This is where the story lives, and it
needs two things in it:

- **the turn** — the moment something changed or broke;
- **one detail** — small, true, and doing no work except being real.

> He said Thursday. I went on Thursday and it wasn't ready. He didn't look up from the tea.

That last sentence is the detail. Nothing in the story depends on it, and it is the sentence
somebody will repeat later.

**The end: how it finished, plus one line of what it left.**

> In the end I took it back and fixed the cable myself. It still makes the noise.

**The shape, drawn:**

```text
   BEGINNING   ▇▇                 where, when, who
   MIDDLE      ▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇     what went wrong · what I did · the detail
   END         ▇▇▇                how it finished · what it left
```

**Where the listener's attention actually goes.** People decide whether to keep listening in the
first sentence, and they decide whether the story was any good in the last one. The middle is
where they relax. That is why a story with a weak first and last sentence fails even when the
middle is excellent.

**And the one-sentence version you should be able to say first**, because it protects you from
wandering:

> This is about the time I took my bike in three times and gave up.

Say that to yourself before you start. A story you cannot summarise in one sentence is usually two
stories.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the endless beginning**:

> My cousin's bike — actually he bought it second hand in 2019, from a person in our old area,
> and that time the price was …

Four sentences of setting and no problem yet. Listeners leave during long beginnings, not during
long middles.

The second is **the story with no ending**:

> … and then I went back on Saturday and it was still not ready. Anyway.

*Anyway* is what people say instead of an ending. The listener has nowhere to put the story down
and usually asks *so what happened?*, which is the polite version of *you did not finish*.

The third is **the ending given away first**:

> I'll tell you what happened with my bike — in the end I fixed it myself, because the fellow near
> the bridge never did it. So, I took it to him last month …

Now the middle has nothing to pull anybody through it. Everything after the first sentence is
detail attached to a known ending.

The fourth is **a story with no problem in it**:

> Last Sunday I went to my uncle's house. We ate. Then we came back.

That is an account of a day, and it is fine as an answer to *what did you do at the weekend?* It
is not a story, because nothing changed, and a listener who is told a story is waiting for the
thing that went wrong.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Listen to people telling stories in a shop or a waiting room. The beginnings are almost rudely
short — *so I took the bike in* — and the detail sits in the middle, and the ending is often one
short line with a shrug in it. Nobody announces that they are telling a story.

Under pressure, in a second language, the beginning swells. Everything you are sure you can say
correctly is background, so background is where you stay, and the problem — which needs harder
sentences — keeps getting postponed. That is worth knowing in advance, because it feels like
carefulness while it is happening.

What a native listener notices: whether they were given the problem early. If it arrives late,
they will interrupt with *what happened?*, and that question is the clearest possible signal that
the beginning went on too long.
