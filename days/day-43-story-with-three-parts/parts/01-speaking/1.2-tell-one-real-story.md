## One-line answer

Tell one real story to a real person, recorder on, and then tell it again to somebody else — and
keep what changed between the two tellings, because the second telling is always the better one
and nobody can explain why until they hear both.

## The story

The barber's shop again, later, and the man with the motorbike tells the same story to a new
customer.

It is shorter this time. The bit about the tea stays. The bit about the second Thursday is gone.
He has moved one sentence to the end — *it still makes the noise* — and everybody laughs, which
did not happen the first time.

Nobody coached him. He told it twice, and the second telling kept what worked.

This is what practice actually is for a story: not rehearsing one version until it is smooth, but
telling it to two people and letting the differences teach you.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **telling** — one time you tell a story to somebody.
- **cut** — to leave something out.
- **reaction** — what the listener does while you speak: a laugh, a question, a nod, nothing.
- **land** — to have the effect you wanted.

The work is two tellings and one comparison.

1. **Telling one**, to a real person, recorded, with the three parts from
   [part 1.1](1.1-the-three-parts-a-listener-waits-for.md).
2. **Telling two**, to a different person, recorded, with no preparation in between. Do not write
   anything down; just tell it.
3. **The comparison**, with both recordings, listing what you cut, what you moved, and where the
   listener reacted.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate is a recorded story of at least two hundred spoken words. Two hundred words is longer
than any single stretch you have recorded so far in this course, and length is where stories fall
apart — the middle sags, or the end never arrives.

And this is the rep that builds the thing the gate actually checks: whether a stranger can follow
a story from your mouth. Two listeners who were not there is the only way to find out.

by adding what people said, what you thought, and what the place was like at that moment.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **rehearsing between the tellings**. You listen back, write a
better version, and then perform it. What you learn is how to read aloud from memory, which is not
this skill.

The second is **the same story to a listener who was there**. They fill in everything you left
out, they laugh in the right places from memory, and the test tells you nothing.

The third is **stopping to correct a verb**:

> I goed — sorry, I went — I went to the shop and …

One repair is fine and it is what real speakers do. Three repairs in a minute and the story is
about your English rather than the bike.

The fourth is **adding length with background**:

> Before I tell you, you should know that the shop has been there twenty years and the man's
> father also …

Two hundred words of story is not two hundred words of anything. Length comes from the middle: the
moment, the words people said, what you were thinking.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

People tell their good stories dozens of times, and the version you hear from them is the fortieth
telling. That is why fluent storytellers sound effortless: the cutting has already happened. You
are doing in one evening what usually takes a year of accidental repetition.

Under pressure — a story told to somebody senior, or in a group where you are the only one
speaking — the beginning grows again. The protection is the one-sentence version: say it in your
head, and start at the problem.

What a native listener gives you, without being asked, is the truth about your middle. They laugh,
they lean in, or they check their phone, and all three are answers. The only unusable listener is
one who already knows the story.
