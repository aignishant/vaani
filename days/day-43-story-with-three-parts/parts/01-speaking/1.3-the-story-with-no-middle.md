## One-line answer

Tell the same story twice with a part deliberately removed — once with no middle, once with no end
— and ask a real listener the same question after each: *so what happened?* — because the question
they ask tells you exactly which part was missing, every time.

## The story

Three tellings of the bike story in the same afternoon.

No middle:

> I took the bike to the fellow near the bridge. In the end I fixed it myself.

The listener says: *so what happened?*

No end:

> I took it in. He said Thursday. Thursday it wasn't ready. Saturday it wasn't ready. Third time I
> just stood there and he didn't even look up from his tea.

The listener says: *so what happened?*

The same five words, for two completely different failures. That is what makes this pair worth
building on purpose: the listener's question is identical, so you cannot tell from the question
alone which part you dropped. You can only tell from where you stopped.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **gap** — a part of the story that is missing.
- **compressed** — squeezed into too few words.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will record three versions of one real story.

- **Take A — no middle.** Beginning and end only. Two or three sentences. Everything that
  happened is compressed into *in the end*.
- **Take B — no end.** Beginning and middle in full, and then it stops. No line about how it
  finished.
- **Take C — the whole story**, with all three parts, from
  [part 1.2](1.2-tell-one-real-story.md).

Then a real listener hears one take each — three different people if you can — and you write down
the first thing they say, unprompted.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate names all three parts. A gate recording that loses one of them is the commonest
way this gate goes red, and both losses sound fine from inside your own mouth, because you know
the whole story.

And this is the check that produces a ledger row you can use for months: *my stories lose the
end*, or *my stories compress the middle*. Almost everybody has one of those two habits, not both.

## The rule

**Use a story you have already told**, from part 1.2, so only the shape changes.

**Record take A, then take B, then take C**, in that order, so the full version is not sitting in
your mouth while you try to make a broken one.

**Then the listener test.** Play one take to each listener, and say nothing afterwards. Write down
the **first thing they say**, exactly:

| C — the whole story | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when takes A and B both get a question
instead of a reaction — *so what happened?*, *and then?*, *so is it fixed?* It is **GREEN** when
take C gets a reaction rather than a question: a laugh, their own story back, or a comment about
the thing that happened.

That is the whole measure, and it is a good one: **a finished story gets a story back; an
unfinished one gets a question.**

If take C also gets a question, write down which question. It names the part still missing, and
that is the most useful sentence in today's work.

**Then the habit check**, which is what you keep. Play back the three tellings from
[part 1.2](1.2-tell-one-real-story.md) and this part's take C, and for each one mark how many

Most people find a pattern: a beginning that is too long, or an end of zero sentences. That number
is your habit, and it is fixable because it is countable.

**Then the repair drill.** Record five endings — one sentence each — for five different real
stories from your life. Just the endings, nothing else:

> And it still makes the noise.
> We got there ten minutes after it closed.
> He never did call back.

Endings are the part beginners have least practice at, because a story that trails off never needs
one.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take A that still has a middle in it**. You
cannot help yourself; one sentence about the second Thursday gets in. Two or three sentences
total, and nothing about what went wrong.

The second is **explaining after the listener's question**. The moment they ask *so what
happened?*, you tell them. Then the next take is contaminated, because they now know the story.
Write the question down and say *I'll tell you at the end*.

The third, in real conversation, is **the trailed-off ending with a hand gesture**. A shrug is not
an ending in a recording, and it is not an ending on the phone either. If your endings depend on
being seen, they do not exist for half your listeners.

The fourth is **the story that ends with a summary instead of an event**:

> So that was my experience with the bike shop. It was very bad service.

That is a conclusion, not an ending. The end of a story is the last thing that *happened* — plus,
at most, one line of what it left behind.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

A finished story is answered with another story. That is the clearest signal in conversation and
it is almost automatic: somebody tells you about the bike, and you find yourself telling them
about your fridge. If your stories reliably get questions instead, something is being dropped.

Under pressure, people drop the end. Speaking in a second language costs effort, the story is
mostly told, the hard part is over — and the last two sentences get abandoned because the speaker
is already relieved. That is why the ending drill exists.

What a native listener does with a missing middle is different and worth knowing: they do not ask
what happened out of curiosity. They ask because they were given the shape of a story with no
content, which feels like being shown a closed box.
