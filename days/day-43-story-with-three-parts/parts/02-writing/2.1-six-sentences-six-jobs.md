## One-line answer

A story in six sentences works because each sentence has one job — set it up, start it, turn it,
make it worse, resolve it, land it — and six is enough for a whole story only if no sentence is
doing two jobs or none.

## The story

A family group message, the kind that goes to eleven people at once.

> Yesterday something happened with the water tank. It was very bad. Anyway it is fine now. I will
> tell you all when we meet. Everything is okay. Don't worry.

Six sentences, and not one of them says what happened. Everybody now has to ask, and eleven people
asking the same question is the most expensive thing a short message can produce.

Six sentences that do their jobs:

> The tank on the roof started leaking on Tuesday night. I heard water on the stairs at about
> eleven and went up with a torch. The joint near the outlet had cracked, and the tank was half
> empty already. I shut the inlet valve, but the water kept coming down the wall into the landing.
> The plumber came at six in the morning and replaced the joint in an hour. The wall is still wet,
> and he says it will take a week to dry.

Same length. One of them is a story, and the other one is six sentences about not telling it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **job** — the one thing a sentence is there to do.
- **set-up** — the sentence that says where and when.
- **turn** — the sentence where things change or get worse.
- **resolve** — to settle the problem, or to say how it stopped.
- **land** — the last sentence, which gives the reader something to keep.

Six sentences, six jobs. Written out as a shape, so you can hold it:

| Sentence | Its job | From the example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | where, when, what started | *The tank started leaking on Tuesday night.* |
| 2 | how you found out, and what you did first | *I heard water on the stairs and went up.* |
| 3 | what was actually wrong | *The joint had cracked, and the tank was half empty.* |
| 4 | the turn — it gets worse, or your first fix fails | *I shut the valve, but the water kept coming.* |
| 5 | how it was settled | *The plumber replaced the joint in an hour.* |
| 6 | what it left behind | *The wall is still wet.* |

Sentence four is the one beginners leave out, and it is the one that makes six sentences a story
rather than a report.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 writing gate is a one-page story in the past, in paragraphs, with speech marks used
once. A page is three or four of these six-sentence blocks. If a six-sentence story is solid, a
page is a matter of joining; if it is not, a page is six hundred words of the same problem.

And this is the most-used piece of writing in ordinary life: the message that tells people what
happened. It gets written badly by almost everybody, in every language, and the cost is always the
same — a round of questions.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[My last holiday](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/my-last-holiday),
a model of a short written account in the past, whose tips are:

> Try to make your writing interesting for the reader. To do this, you can make it personal with
> your own memories and experiences.
> Use adjectives to add detail to your descriptions.
> Write clear and simple sentences and organise your ideas in short paragraphs. Give each
> paragraph a different topic.
> Use *so, but, and, because* and other linking words.

**Start at the event, not before it.** Sentence one carries the beginning of the *problem*, not the
beginning of the day:

> The tank started leaking on Tuesday night.     ← the event
> On Tuesday I came home from work at seven.     ← the day

**Put a date or a time in sentence one and nowhere else.** Two time words in six sentences is
enough. More, and the story turns into a timetable.

**Sentence four must contain a *but* or a *then*.** This is a test you can apply mechanically: if
no sentence in your six turns against the one before it, there is no story in there yet.

> I shut the inlet valve, **but** the water kept coming down the wall.

**Sentence six is not a summary.** It is one fact left behind:

> The wall is still wet, and he says it will take a week to dry.     ← a fact
> So it was a very bad experience for our family.                    ← a summary

**Detail goes in sentences two and three**, and one concrete noun is worth five adjectives:

> I heard water on the stairs at about eleven and went up with a torch.

*A torch* does nothing for the plot and is the reason the reader can see the scene.

**The shape on the page:**

```text
   1  ▇▇▇▇         set-up, with the only date in it
   2  ▇▇▇▇▇▇       how you found out · what you did first · a detail
   3  ▇▇▇▇▇▇       what was actually wrong
   4  ▇▇▇▇▇▇▇      THE TURN — but / then
   5  ▇▇▇▇▇        how it was settled
   6  ▇▇▇          what it left behind
```

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the message that reports feelings instead of events**:

> Something happened with the tank. It was very bad. Anyway it is fine now.

Every sentence is about how it was rather than what it was, and the reader has to ask.

The second is **five sentences of set-up and one of story**:

> On Tuesday I came home at seven. I had my food. Then I watched something. At about eleven I was
> about to sleep. My wife also was awake. Then the tank leaked.

The story starts at the last sentence and the message is over.

The third is **no turn**:

> The tank leaked. I shut the valve. The plumber came. He fixed it.

Four facts in a straight line. Nothing went wrong twice, so there is nothing for the reader to
care about — and in a real message about damage, the thing that went wrong twice is usually the
part the reader most needs.

The fourth is **two jobs in one sentence and none in the next**:

> The tank leaked on Tuesday night and I heard it and went up and the joint was cracked and the
> tank was half empty. It was bad.

Sentence one is doing three jobs, and sentence two is doing none. The six-job shape exists to stop
exactly this.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Short written accounts get forwarded, and the forwarded version arrives without you. Somebody
sends your six sentences to a landlord, an insurer or a relative who was not in the conversation,
and what they can act on is exactly what those six sentences say.

Under pressure people write the feelings version, because the feelings are what they have. The
repair is mechanical: write the six jobs down the side of the page first, then fill them.

A formal reader — an office, an insurer, a landlord — reads sentences three and six hardest: what
was wrong, and what is still wrong. In a claim or a complaint, sentence six is the one the whole
message exists for.

- **Say out loud:** Read your six sentences aloud to somebody who was not there. Say which job
