## One-line answer

Write the story where all six sentences are set-up, give it to a real reader with the good version,
and ask them both to tell you what happened — because a page full of background feels like careful
writing while you produce it and reads like nothing at all.

## The story

Two messages about the same night.

> The tank on our roof is about six years old. My uncle put it in when he was staying with us. It
> is the black kind, not the white one. The outlet is on the side near the stairs. We have had no
> trouble with it before. Anyway, on Tuesday there was a problem.

And:

> The tank on the roof started leaking on Tuesday night. I heard water on the stairs at about
> eleven and went up with a torch.

The first message is six sentences long and ends where the second one starts. Everything in it is
true, relevant-sounding, and about nothing that happened.

The writer of the first message worked harder. That is the trap: background is the easiest thing
to write in a second language, because it needs no turn, no result and no uncomfortable fact, so a
tired writer produces it and feels productive.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **background** — information about how things usually are, rather than what happened.
- **event** — something that happened at a particular moment.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will write two versions of one real story.

- **The RED version — all setting.** Six sentences, every one background: how things usually are,
  who installed what, what the place is like, what normally happens. The event may be mentioned
  only in the last few words, as *anyway, then there was a problem*.
- **The GREEN version — the six jobs**, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-six-sentences-six-jobs.md).

Then two real readers, one version each, and one question: **what happened?**

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story. A page is four times the length of six sentences, and
background expands to fill whatever space it is given, so a page is where this failure does its
real damage: three paragraphs of setting and one of story.

And the ledger row is about a habit rather than an error: *given six sentences, I spend four on
background*. That is countable, which means it can be trained.

## The rule

**Write the RED version about the same real event as your GREEN one**, so the only difference is
where the words went.

**Make it properly background.** Every sentence should be true and none should be an event. The
test for each sentence:

> Does this sentence say something **happened at a moment**, or something **is generally so**?

If it is generally so, it is background.

**Then the reader test.** One version per reader, read once, taken away, and ask:

> What happened?

Write their exact words, and whether they had to guess.

**Then the conversion drill.** Take five background sentences from your own writing and turn each
one into an event sentence about the same thing:

> The tank is about six years old.            ← background
> The tank cracked at the joint on Tuesday.   ← event

**And the rule to keep afterwards:** background is allowed when it is needed to understand the
event, and only then. *My uncle installed it* matters if the story is about who pays for the
repair, and not otherwise.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED version with the event in the last
sentence**. That is a real message pattern and it is worth writing down separately — but for this
test, the event must not be in it at all, or the reader can answer.

The second is **a reader who works it out from what they know**:

> Oh, the tank leaked, no?

They filled it in from their own knowledge of your house. Use a reader who does not know the place.

The third, in real writing, is **background that grows because the event is unpleasant**. Writing
about something that went wrong, people build a longer and longer approach to the moment. The
approach is where the writing stops being useful.

The fourth is **cutting all background and leaving the story bare**:

> The joint cracked. I shut the valve. The plumber came.

Nobody can see anything. The fix for too much background is not none; it is one detail in the
right sentence — the torch, the eleven o'clock, the wet wall.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Readers of a real account want the event in the first line and will forgive everything else. That
is why news and complaints and messages from hospitals all open the same way: what happened, when.
The background comes afterwards or not at all.

Under pressure, in a second language, background is the safe harbour. Its sentences use verbs you
control, need no sequence and cannot be wrong. That safety is exactly why it has to be counted
rather than trusted.

A formal reader is the one who punishes this hardest. An insurer or an office reading six
sentences of background will either ask for the facts again — which takes days — or treat the
matter as less serious than it is, because nothing in the page said anything happened.
