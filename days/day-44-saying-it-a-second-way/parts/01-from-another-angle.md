# The same thing from another angle

`FL-06` · Day 44

## The one-line answer

When something you said did not land, the fix is a completely different sentence — not the same
sentence louder, and not standing still hunting for the one word you think was missing.

## The model

You have taken a photograph of something and handed it across, and the other person is turning it
around trying to work out what they are looking at. There are two useless things to do next, and
almost everybody does one of them.

**You take the identical photograph again with the flash on.** Same angle, same distance, more
light. That is the repeat: same sentence, more volume, slower. It cannot work, because the problem
was never that they could not hear you. Volume carries no information. A louder copy of a sentence
that did not mean anything to them still does not mean anything to them.

**Or you stand there trying to remember the name of the thing.** The sentence stops, you go
quiet, and everybody waits while you hunt. That hunt has its own day later on. Today is not about a
word you cannot find — today you know exactly what you meant. **They didn't get it.**

The move is to walk round the thing and shoot it from another side. Same subject, different
picture, and you say out loud that it is a different picture.

> Right, different way —
> Put it this way —
> Forget that, here's the simple version.

Say one of those before the second attempt, every time. It costs three words and it does two jobs.
It tells them a new sentence is coming rather than a repeat, so they listen again instead of
nodding through it. And it puts the failure on your side of the table, where it belongs — *my
version wasn't clear*, not *you didn't follow*. That distinction is entirely audible and people
respond to it.

Then pick an angle. Four of them, and they are not interchangeable.

**Split it in two.** Most sentences that fail are not hard ideas. They are two ideas welded into
one piece of grammar, with the joins doing more work than joins can do. Take the weld out.

> Two things, actually — {{one}}. And {{the other}}.

Say it and listen to what happens to your own delivery. Two short sentences give you two landings,
and a landing is where the other person gets to catch up. The long version had nowhere to catch up
in.

**Swap the description for an example.** A general statement is a photograph from a long way off. A
worked case has somebody in it doing something.

> Say you're {{in this situation}} — then {{what happens}}.

This is the strongest of the four and the one people reach for last, because it feels less precise.
It is less precise. It also lands, and the precise version did not.

**Come at it from the other end.** Start with the result and work backwards to the reason.

> I'll come at it from the other end.

Ordinary explanations run cause, then effect, and if the cause is the hard part you lose them
before the effect arrives. Reversed, they know where you are going and can follow the reasoning
towards a place they can already see.

**Find out which half missed.** Before any of the above — one question saves you re-explaining the
half they already had.

> Was it the {{word}} that threw you?

Most failed explanations fail in one specific place. Re-explaining all of it is slow, slightly
insulting, and it hides the actual problem. And when you do rebuild it:

> Same thing, fewer words: {{…}}

The second attempt should be shorter than the first, almost always. The first one was long because
you were being careful. Careful is what made it fail.

**Today's phrases** — the second attempt, and the words that mark it as one. All of these go into
`docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Right, different way — | Launching a genuinely different sentence instead of a repeat |
| Put it this way — | The first version was accurate and useless, and you're reframing it |

## Say it now

Pick one thing you explain often — something from your work, or something you are into that most
people are not. The same subject for all eight lines.

1. *{{your usual one-sentence explanation}}.*
2. *Right, different way — {{a completely different sentence for it}}.*
3. *Put it this way — {{the same idea, no technical words}}.*
4. *Forget that, here's the simple version. {{the simple version}}.*
5. *Two things, actually — {{one}}. And {{the other}}.*
6. *Say you're {{a situation}} — then {{what happens}}.*
7. *I'll come at it from the other end. {{the result}}, and that's because {{the reason}}.*
8. *Was it the {{word}} that threw you? Same thing, fewer words: {{…}}.*

Then run lines 2 to 8 again with one extra rule: **no content word may appear twice across the
whole run.** If you said *system* in line 2, it is gone for the rest. This is uncomfortable and it
is the actual skill — the ability to describe the same thing from a shelf of words you have not
already used.

> **Three things, twice each.** Pick three things you explain often. Say each one your usual way.
> Then announce a second attempt out loud and say it again — with the one word that carries the
> whole thing banned, no sentence from the first version reused, and a different angle each time.

- **Solo form:** as written. Say the banned word out loud before each pair, so it is on the tape and
  you can hear it if it creeps back in. It will creep back in.
- **With a partner:** they say *no* the instant you use the banned word or reuse a sentence, and you
  carry on from where you were without stopping. No restarting, no apologising. Three refusals on
  one item and you move to the next.

## The deliberate failure

Take the hardest of your three. Explain it once. Then, as if it had not landed, say the same
sentence again — louder. Then a third time, louder and slower still, hunting mid-sentence for the
word you think would fix it.

**What breaking sounds like:** the third version is the first version with more volume and a longer
hole in the middle. Word for word. No new information entered the room across three attempts, and
you can hear yourself getting slightly cross with a sentence rather than with the situation.

With a partner there is a worse finding. Somewhere around the second repeat they start agreeing
with you — *yeah, yeah, no, I get it* — and they do not get it. **Repeating louder buys silence,
not understanding**, and on the tape you can hear the exact moment they stopped asking. That moment
is what this whole day exists to prevent, and it is much earlier than you would guess.

Now say it once more from another angle, with an example in it, and listen to what comes back. It
will be a real question rather than an agreement.

## When it breaks in the wild

A call, no faces, and somebody asks what you actually do. You give them your usual sentence, the
one with the word from your work in the middle of it. There is a pause and a *sorry — what?* So you
say your usual sentence again, more clearly. Which is to say: identically.

**The repair:**

> Right, different way — say you're {{an ordinary situation they're in}}, and then {{what your work
> does about it}}.

Nothing about the first attempt is worth rescuing. It is a photograph from an angle that did not
work, and there is no amount of light that fixes an angle.
