## One-line answer

The four time words that hold a spoken account together — *first*, *then*, *after that*, *in the
end* — go at the front of the sentence with a small pause after them, and the pause is what makes
them work.

## The story

Somebody asks how you got to the wedding, because they have to go tomorrow and there are three
buses involved.

You know the route perfectly. It comes out like this:

> You take the bus to the junction and then the other one comes and it takes about twenty minutes
> and then you have to cross and there is an auto usually.

They nod, and they will get it wrong tomorrow, because you gave them a flat stream with one
*then* in it and no edges.

Now with the four words, and a small gap after each:

> First, │ take the bus to the junction.
> Then │ wait on the same side for the number four.
> After that, │ cross at the temple and get an auto.
> In the end │ you'll come out behind the hall, not in front of it.

The route did not change. It now has four doors in it, and a listener can walk through them one at
a time.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **sequence** — a set of things in the order they happen.
- **signpost** — a word that tells a listener where they are in a sequence.
- **stress** — saying a word with more force than the words around it.
- **pause** — a short silence.

Four words, four positions in a sequence.

| Word | Where it belongs | What it tells the listener |
| --- | --- | --- |
| first | the opening step | this is where it starts |
| then | any middle step | one more step, same track |
| after that | a middle step where something changes | a new stage, not just a next step |
| in the end | the closing step | this is the last one, and it is the outcome |

They are not interchangeable. *Then* costs nothing and adds nothing; *after that* marks a change
of stage; *in the end* promises that nothing follows.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story with a beginning, a middle and an end, of at least two
hundred spoken words. Two hundred words is long enough for a listener to lose the order, and these
four words are what stops that happening.

And they carry the whole of practical speech: directions, a recipe, how to fill a form, what
happened at the office. Any time somebody has to do something in order, these four words are how
English gives them the order.

Two sounds inside those words are worth care. *First* starts with *f* and carries the long vowel
that this course has practised before; *then* starts with the soft *th* made with the tongue
between the teeth, not with a *d* — *then*, not *den*.

**Where they go, and what the voice does.** At the front, with the voice up slightly on the time
word and a small gap after it:

```text
   FIRST,  │ take the bus to the junction.
   THEN    │ wait on the same side.
   AFTER THAT, │ cross at the temple.
   IN THE END  │ you'll come out behind the hall.
```

The gap is the whole technique. A time word said inside the flow of the sentence does nothing at
all — the listener hears it as filler, because that is what it sounds like.

**Comma or no comma is a writing question.** In speech there is only the gap, and the gap belongs
after all four.

**What each one costs you.** *Then* is free and you can use it three or four times. *After that*
is heavier and should mark something real: a change of vehicle, a change of place, a change of
person. *In the end* can only be used once, at the end, and using it in the middle confuses a
listener badly, because they stop waiting for more.

**Two more that belong with them**, and which fluent speakers use constantly:

> **So** — result: *so I went back the next day.*
> **Anyway** — back to the main track after a detour: *anyway, the bus came at six.*

**The rhythm of a four-step account**, so the listener can hear the shape:

> First, I called the office. Then I went there. After that, they sent me to the other building.
> In the end nobody was there at all.

Four sentences, four doors, one outcome.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the endless *and then***:

> I went there and then I asked and then they said no and then I waited and then …

*And then* five times is a listener holding five things in a row with no stages. It is the single
most recognisable shape of a beginner's story and it is fixed by replacing two of them with *after
that* and one with *in the end*.

The second is **the time word swallowed inside the flow**:

> firstyoutakethebus

No gap, no stress, so it does not signpost anything. A listener needs the small silence to know a
new step has started.

The third is ***in the end* in the middle**:

> In the end I went to the office, and then I waited, and then they called me.

You promised an outcome and then carried on. Listeners stop tracking once they think the account
is finished.

The fourth is ***at last* used for *in the end***:

> At last I went home.

*At last* carries relief — a long wait finally over — and it is not a neutral closing word. If
there was no waiting, it sounds strange. `TODO`: if you want this distinction stated by a

dictionary and add a dated row to `docs/SOURCES.md`.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use fewer signposts than learners expect — two or three in a whole story — and
place them where the story changes stage rather than at every step. Too many signposts sound like
instructions being read out.

Under pressure, giving directions to somebody who has to act on them, the signposts get stronger
and the gaps get longer. That is the right instinct: the harder the sequence is to follow, the
more the listener needs the doors.

What a native listener notices is whether *in the end* actually ends things. If you use it and
keep going, they will interrupt to ask whether you are still on the same story — not because of
grammar, but because the word made a promise.
