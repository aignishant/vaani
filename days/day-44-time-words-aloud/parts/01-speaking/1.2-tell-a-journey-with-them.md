## One-line answer

Give a real sequence to a real person who has to act on it, and check it the only way that counts
— by asking them to say it back — because a sequence that cannot be repeated has not been given.

## The story

The person going to the wedding tomorrow listens to your four steps and says:

> So, bus to the junction, then the number four from the same side, cross at the temple, auto,
> and I come out at the back.

Everything is there, in order, in their own words. That is the test passed, and it took eleven
seconds.

If instead they had said *so … the junction, and then which bus?*, that is also useful, and it
points exactly at the step where your gap was missing or your signpost was doing no work.

This part is that test, run four times on four real sequences.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **say it back** — to repeat something in your own words to show you have it.
- **step** — one action in a sequence.
- **stage** — a group of steps that belong together.
- **check** — a way of finding out whether something worked.

The work is three things.

1. **Choose sequences somebody actually needs.** Directions to your house, how to do a thing at
   work, how to cook something, how you fixed something.
2. **Give each one in four to six steps**, with the time words from
   [part 1.1](1.1-first-then-after-that-in-the-end.md) and a gap after each.
3. **Ask them to say it back**, and write down where it broke.

The say-back is not politeness. It is the only reliable way to find out whether a spoken sequence
landed, and it is exactly what a good shopkeeper, nurse or official does when the sequence
matters.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a spoken story of at least two hundred spoken words, and the same signposting
carries it: a story is a sequence with feelings attached.

And the say-back is a habit worth having for the rest of your life in English. When you give
instructions in a language you are still building, you cannot hear which part was unclear. The
other person can show you in one sentence.

## The rule

**Three pages checked on 2026-09-23** for the target words, each with audio:
[first](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/first) — /fɜːst/ UK, /fɚst/ US;
[then](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/then) — /ðɛn/;
[after](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/after) — /ˈɑːftə/ UK, /ˈæftəɹ/ US.

**Step one — write the steps as notes before you speak.** Four to six, no sentences:

```text
   bus → junction
   number four, same side
   cross at temple
   auto
   come out at back of hall
```

**Step two — decide where the stages change.** One *after that* per stage change, not per step. In
the notes above there is really one stage change: when you stop using buses. That is where *after
that* goes.

**Step three — give it, with the gaps.** Recorder on if the person agrees, or record yourself
giving it again immediately afterwards.

**Step four — the say-back.** Say exactly this:

> Can you say it back to me, so I know I've said it properly?

That wording matters: it puts the possible failure on you, not on them, so nobody feels tested.

The right-hand column has only a few possible answers, and they are worth knowing in advance: no

given out of order.

**The repair, when a say-back breaks.** Do not repeat the whole thing. Give the broken step alone,
with its signpost:

> Almost — after the second bus, cross at the temple first, then take the auto.

**And the harder version, once four sequences have worked**: give a sequence over the phone, where
the listener cannot see your hands. Hands do an enormous amount of signposting, and losing them
shows you how much of your sequence was in the air rather than in the words.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **giving the sequence and asking *understand?***:

> … and then the auto. Understand?

Everybody says yes. *Yes* is not a say-back and it carries no information at all.

The second is **six steps where four would do**. A sequence with every small action in it —
*then you show the ticket, then you wait, then the door opens* — overloads the listener, and the
say-back comes back with the important step missing because it was one of nine.

The third is **the stage change not marked**:

> Then take the bus, then cross the road, then take an auto, then ask for the hall.

All the same weight. The listener does not know that crossing the road is where everything
changes, so they look for the auto on the wrong side.

The fourth is **correcting a say-back by starting again**. The listener got four steps right; they
do not need those four again. Repeating everything makes them lose the ones they had.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

People who give instructions for a living — nurses, mechanics, officials, teachers — all use the
say-back, in every language, without calling it anything. It is how they avoid doing the work
twice.

Under pressure and on a phone, sequences collapse. The listener has no hands to watch and no face
to read, so the gaps and the signposts are carrying everything. If you have ever given directions
on a call and had somebody arrive at the wrong gate, the words did the work alone and there were
not enough of them.

What a native listener does with a good sequence is say it back without being asked — *so, second
left, past the temple, right?* That unprompted say-back is the strongest signal that your
signposting worked.
