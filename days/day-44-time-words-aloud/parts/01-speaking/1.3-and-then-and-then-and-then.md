## One-line answer

Give a real sequence joined entirely by *and then*, with no gaps anywhere, then ask a real person
to say it back — because the chain version is understood while you say it and cannot be repeated
thirty seconds later, and only the say-back shows you that.

## The story

The same directions to the wedding hall, given twice on the same evening to two different people.

The chain version:

> You take the bus to the junction and then there's another bus from the same side and then you
> cross near the temple and then take an auto and then you come out at the back.

The listener nods the whole way through. They understand every word. Then they are asked to repeat
it, and what comes back is:

> Bus … and then another bus … and then somewhere near a temple?

Three steps became two and a question mark, in under a minute, from somebody paying attention.

Nothing was wrong with the English. There were no doors in it, so nothing could be held.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **chain** — steps joined end to end with no marks between them.
- **recall** — what somebody can repeat back without hearing it again.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will give the same five-step sequence twice, to two different people.

- **The RED version — the chain.** Every step joined with *and then*. One breath. No gaps, no
  *first*, no *after that*, no *in the end*.
- **The GREEN version — signposted.** The four time words from
  [part 1.1](1.1-first-then-after-that-in-the-end.md), with a gap after each and one *after that*
  at the real stage change.

Then each listener says it back, **thirty seconds later**, not immediately. The wait is part of
the test.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate is a recorded story of two hundred spoken words. A chain that long is followable in the
moment and gone by the end, which is why a gate take can feel fine and still fail when somebody
listens who was not in the room.

And this failure is invisible to the speaker in a way most are not: the listener nods, the words
are all correct, and nothing about the conversation tells you it did not work. Only a say-back
does.

## The rule

**Choose one real five-step sequence** that a real person would actually need, and give both
versions of it to two different people.

**Build the RED version properly.** Five steps, *and then* between each one, no pause anywhere,
one breath if you can manage it. Do not slow down to be kind.

**Wait thirty seconds after each version.** Talk about something else. Then ask:

> Can you say that back to me?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the chain version comes back with
fewer than four of the five steps, or with the stage change lost. It is **GREEN** when the
signposted version comes back with all five, in order, in their own words.

If the chain version comes back perfectly, either the sequence was too familiar to the listener —
use a sequence they do not already know — or a gap crept in. Record yourself and listen for it.

**Then the count on your own speech**, which is the part that lasts. Play back your four
recordings from [part 1.2](1.2-tell-a-journey-with-them.md) and today's takes, and count:

> *and then* used: `TODO(me)` times · *first*: `TODO(me)` · *after that*: `TODO(me)` ·
> *in the end*: `TODO(me)`

Most people find *and then* outnumbers everything else together. That ratio is the habit.

**Then the replacement drill.** Take five real sequences and record each one with a hard rule: **no
*and then* at all**. Nothing else is banned. This is uncomfortable for about ninety seconds and
then it stops being uncomfortable, which is the whole point.

**And the repair for real conversation**, when you hear a chain coming out of your own mouth: stop,
and re-open with a signpost.

> — sorry, let me do that again. First, the bus to the junction.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a chain with gaps in it**. Your mouth has been
practising signposts all day and puts silence where the words used to be, which rescues the chain
and hides the finding. Say it in one breath.

The second is **asking for the say-back immediately**. Anything can be repeated after two seconds.
The thirty-second wait is where the difference lives.

The third is **using a sequence the listener already knows**. Your own street, your own office —
they fill in the gaps from memory and both versions come back perfectly.

The fourth, in real speech, is **the chain returning as soon as the story gets exciting**. A
speaker who signposts directions beautifully will chain a story about an argument, because
excitement pushes everything into one breath. That is worth watching for, because the exciting
stories are the ones people most want to follow.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Everybody chains sometimes, including fluent speakers, and among people who share all the context
it costs nothing. It costs when the listener has to act — directions, instructions, what to bring,
who to ask — and that is exactly when learners chain hardest, because those are the moments with
the most steps.

Under pressure the chain gets longer and the breath gets shorter. Listeners respond by nodding
more, which feels like success and is really a way of asking you to stop.

What a native listener notices is that they cannot repeat what you said. They will not tell you
that. They will go away, get one step wrong, and come back and ask, and the conversation you
thought was finished happens twice.
