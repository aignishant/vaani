## One-line answer

To ask or deny something in the past, English puts the past into *did* or *didn't* and takes it
out of the main verb — *Did you go?*, *I didn't go* — so the verb goes back to its plain form and
never carries the past twice.

## The story

A message thread with a friend who went to an event you missed. You want the details.

> Did you went? Did they served food? I didn't saw the photos. Why you didn't called me?

Your friend answers every question, because the meaning is not in doubt. But look at what the four
sentences have in common: each one is carrying the past twice, once in *did* and once in the verb.

> Did you go? Did they serve food? I didn't see the photos. Why didn't you call me?

Four sentences, four verbs handed back their plain form. Nothing else changed — the past is still
there, and it is all sitting in *did* and *didn't*.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **statement** — a sentence that tells: *I went.*
- **question** — a sentence that asks: *Did you go?*
- **negative** — a sentence that denies: *I didn't go.*
- **auxiliary** — a helping verb that carries the grammar for the main verb. *Did* is one.
- **plain form** — the verb with nothing added: *go*, *see*, *call*, *finish*.

One rule, three shapes:

| Shape | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statement | subject + past verb | *I saw the photos.* |
| Question | **Did** + subject + **plain verb** | *Did you see the photos?* |
| Negative | subject + **didn't** + **plain verb** | *I didn't see the photos.* |

The rule in one line: **the past goes into *did*, so the verb gives it up.**

And it is the same *did* for everybody — *did I*, *did he*, *did they*. There is no *dids*.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3 is the past, and half of anything written about the past is questions and denials. A
complaint says what did **not** happen. A message to somebody who was there asks what happened. A
form asks whether you did something.

And this is the most persistent error in English for most learners. It survives long after the
past forms themselves are solid, because *did* and the past verb both feel correct, and saying
both feels safer than choosing.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple).
It states both halves plainly:

> We use **did** to make questions with the past simple.

with the examples:

> **Did** she **play** tennis when she was younger?
> Where **did** you **go** for your holidays?

and:

> We use **didn't** (**did not**) to make negatives with the past simple.

with:

> They **didn't go** to Spain this year.
> I **didn't see** you yesterday.

Look at the verbs in those four examples: *play*, *go*, *go*, *see*. Every one is the plain form.

**Questions, built in order:**

```text
   Did  +  you  +  see   +  the photos?
   Did  +  they +  serve +  food?
   Did  +  it   +  rain?

   with a question word in front:

   Where  did  you  go?
   What   did  he   say?
   Why    didn't you call?
```

**The question word goes first, then *did*.** That is the order beginners most often lose:

> Why you didn't call?          ← the order slips
> Why didn't you call?          ← question word, then didn't, then subject

**Negatives:**

> I didn't finish the work.
> She didn't tell me.
> We didn't have the receipt.

**The full form, *did not*, is for writing that is formal or firm:**

> I did not receive the parcel.

In a complaint this is worth knowing: *didn't* is ordinary, *did not* is heavier, and the heavier
one is what people use when they are stating a fact that somebody has disputed.

**The one place the past verb stays**: an ordinary statement, where there is no *did* at all. That
is the whole of yesterday's work and it does not change:

> I went. I saw. I called.

**And one thing that catches everybody**: with *be*, there is no *did*. The past forms of *be* do
the work themselves:

> Were you there?     — not *did you were*
> It wasn't ready.    — not *it didn't was*

*Be* has its own day later in this phase. The rule today is for every other verb.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the past kept twice**:

> Did you went? I didn't saw him. She didn't came.

Understood everywhere, and it is the error that stays longest in most learners' English. The fix
is one letter-group each time: *go*, *see*, *come*.

The second is **the missing *did* in a question**:

> You went yesterday?

In speech, with the voice going up, this is real English and perfectly normal. On paper, in
anything that is not a casual message, it reads as unfinished. Write *Did you go yesterday?*

The third is **the word order in a question with a question word**:

> Where you went? Why he didn't call?

The pattern is question word, then *did* or *didn't*, then the subject, then the plain verb.

The fourth is ***didn't* with a verb that has no past to give up**, which is really a spelling
slip:

> I didn't went — I didn't gone — I didn't wented.

All three appear in real beginner writing. The plain form is *go*, every time, whatever the
sentence around it is doing.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

In speech, questions without *did* are everywhere — *you went?*, *she told you?* — and nobody
minds. In writing they change the register: a message to a friend can carry them, and a message to
an office cannot.

Under pressure, the double past comes back first. A learner who has this right in careful writing
will produce *did you went* in a hurried message or an argument, because both halves feel like
they are helping.

A formal reader notices the double past more than almost any other error, because it appears in
the first line of complaints and requests — *I didn't received the item* — where their eye is
already sharpest.
