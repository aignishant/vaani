## One-line answer

Write two real pieces — a message that asks somebody what happened, and a message that says
plainly what did not happen — because questions and negatives in the past are not an exercise;
they are how you find things out and how you complain.

## The story

Two messages, written on the same evening, to two different people.

To the friend who went to the event:

> Did they start on time? Who did you sit with? Did anybody ask where I was?

To the shop that was supposed to deliver on Tuesday:

> The parcel did not arrive on Tuesday. Nobody called, and the driver did not leave a card.

Both are made of the same grammar, and they are doing opposite jobs. The first one opens a
conversation. The second one closes an argument before it starts, because it says exactly what did
not happen and leaves no room for *maybe you weren't at home*.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **enquiry** — a message that asks for information.
- **denial** — a sentence saying something did not happen.
- **specific** — naming the day, the time, the person, the thing.
- **register** — how formal or friendly the writing sounds.

You will write two pieces, one of each kind.

| Piece | What it is | What makes it work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The enquiry | six to eight questions to somebody who was there | real questions you actually want answered |
| The record | five to seven sentences about what did **not** happen | every denial with a date or a time on it |

The second one is the harder and the more useful. A negative sentence with no date attached is an
opinion; with a date it becomes a fact somebody has to answer.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3 writes about what happened, and phase 4 argues. The bridge between them is being able to
state what did not happen in a way that cannot be waved away.

And the enquiry is the only way to get a story you were not in. Every account you will ever write
about something you missed is built on questions in the past.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple),
which gives:

> **Did** she **play** tennis when she was younger?
> Where **did** you **go** for your holidays?
> They **didn't go** to Spain this year.
> I **didn't see** you yesterday.

**The enquiry — how to build six good questions.** Mix the two shapes, because they do different
work:

```text
   closed  (did …)            → a yes or a no, fast
      Did they start on time?
      Did anybody ask about me?

   open    (question word + did …)   → a sentence back
      Who did you sit with?
      What did they say about the date?
      Why did it finish so early?
```

Two or three closed questions at the top get you facts. One or two open ones get you the story.
Six is about the limit before a reader answers only the last one.

**One question per line.** A paragraph with three questions inside it gets one answer.

**The record — how to write a denial that holds.** Each sentence needs three things: what did not
happen, when it did not happen, and who was involved:

> The parcel did not arrive on Tuesday the fifteenth.
> Nobody called me that day, and the driver did not leave a card.
> I did not receive any message about a second attempt.

Compare that with the version that says the same thing and cannot be acted on:

> The delivery did not come properly and nobody informed me anything.

**Choose *didn't* or *did not* on purpose:**

| Form | Where it belongs |
| --- | --- |
| didn't | a message to a friend, an ordinary note, anything spoken |
| did not | a complaint, a formal email, a sentence somebody may dispute |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a question that carries the past twice**:

> Did they started on time? Who did you sat with?

The commonest error in the language, in the piece of writing where you most want an answer.

The second is **the paragraph of questions**:

> Did they start on time and who did you sit with and did anybody ask about me because I wanted to
> know?

One answer will come back, and it will be about the last question.

The third is **the vague denial**:

> They did not do the work properly.

Nothing here can be checked, so nothing can be answered. What was not done, and on which day?

The fourth is **mixing the two jobs in one message**:

> Did you get my message? The parcel did not arrive and nobody called me and did the driver come
> at all?

A message that both asks and complains gets answered as whichever one the reader prefers to deal
with — usually the question, never the complaint.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Written questions get answered in order, and only the ones on their own lines get answered at all.
Anybody who deals with messages all day answers what is easy to see and skips what is buried.

Under pressure people write long, apologetic enquiries and vague complaints — the opposite of what
works. Short numbered questions and dated denials feel blunt to write and read as ordinary and
businesslike to the person receiving them.

A formal reader reads a dated denial as a claim they must answer. *The parcel did not arrive on
the fifteenth* forces a check of their own records; *the delivery was not proper* invites a
sentence of apology and nothing else.
