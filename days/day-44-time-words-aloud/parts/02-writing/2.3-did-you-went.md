## One-line answer

Write the enquiry with the double past in every question on purpose, and write the complaint with
every denial left vague — then count how many of each you can find in your own real messages from
this week, because this is the error that survives everything and the only cure is catching your
own.

## The story

Two messages go out about the same missed delivery.

> Did the driver came on Tuesday? Did he called anybody? I didn't received any message.

The shop replies and answers all three, because the meaning is clear. Nothing in their reply
mentions the English.

> The delivery did not come properly and nobody informed me anything.

The shop replies to this one too: *we are sorry for the inconvenience.* Nothing else happens,
because nothing in the sentence named a day, a person or an action, so there was nothing to check.

Two different failures. The first marks the writer; the second wastes the writer's afternoon.
Today you build both on purpose and then go looking for them in what you have already sent.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **double past** — the past marked twice, in *did* and in the verb.
- **vague** — without the details that make something checkable.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Three pieces, about the same real matter.

- **Piece A — the double past.** Your enquiry from
  [part 2.2](2.2-write-the-questions-and-the-denials.md), rewritten with every question carrying
  the past twice: *did you went*, *did they served*, *I didn't saw*.
- **Piece B — the vague record.** Your denials rewritten with no dates, no names, no actions:
  *properly*, *anything*, *not good*.
- **Piece C — the correct pair**, as you wrote and sent them.

Then a real reader reads A and B and answers one question about each, and then you go hunting
through your own sent messages.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 4 is arguments and complaints, and phase 5 is formal letters. Both are made of dated
denials. A vague denial in a formal letter is the difference between a refund and an apology.

And the double past is the error most likely to still be in your English on day 120. Catching your
own instances, from your own phone, is the only thing that reliably moves it.

## The rule

**Write pieces A and B from your own sent pieces**, changing only the thing being tested.

**Then the reader test.** Give a real person piece B — the vague record — and ask exactly this:

> If you worked at the shop, what would you check?

Write down what they say. Then give them piece C's record and ask the same question.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader of the vague record
cannot name one thing they would check. It is **GREEN** when the dated record gives them at least
two: a day to look up and a person or an action to confirm.

**Then the hunt, which is the real work.** Go through your last twenty sent messages — any
messages, in any app, from this week — and find every past-tense question and negative in them.
List them:

| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

Count them. Write two numbers down: how many past questions and negatives you wrote this week, and
how many carried the past twice.

> Past questions and negatives written: `TODO(me)` · with the double past: `TODO(me)`

That ratio is the only honest measure of where this rule actually is in your English. A person
can get twenty exercise sentences right and still be at half in their own messages.

**Then the correction drill.** For every double past you found in your own messages, write the
correct sentence out, by hand, and then write one new true sentence of your own with the same
verb:

> I didn't received it.  →  I didn't receive it.  →  I didn't receive the second message either.

**And the vagueness drill.** Take three vague complaints from your own past messages — *not
properly*, *very bad service*, *nobody informed me* — and rewrite each with a date, a person and
an action.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a hunt with nothing in it**, because you only
looked at messages written in English to people who correct you. Look at the ordinary ones: the
fast replies, the messages sent while walking.

The second is **writing piece B so vaguely that it is obviously useless**:

> Something was wrong.

Nobody learns anything from that. The dangerous vague sentence is the one that sounds like a real
complaint — *the delivery was not done properly* — and produces nothing.

The third, in real writing, is **correcting the grammar and keeping the vagueness**. A rewritten
complaint with perfect verbs and no dates is still unanswerable, and grammar is the part that
feels like the work.

The fourth is **the double past appearing only in long sentences**. Many learners are accurate in
short sentences and lose the rule when the sentence has a clause in it: *I didn't received the
message that they said they sent on Tuesday.* Watch the long ones in your hunt.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Nobody corrects adults' English in a message. The shop answers, the office replies, the friend
tells you about the event. The error is invisible in its consequences and permanent in its
impression, which is exactly the combination that makes a habit last for years.

Under pressure both failures increase together: a complaint written while angry has more double
pasts and fewer dates, because the writer is describing how it felt rather than what happened.

A formal reader — an office, a company, a landlord — has a standard reply for a vague complaint,
and it costs them nothing. A dated one, with clear denials, goes into a different pile, because
somebody has to check a record and write an answer about it.
