# Stopping at the platform, not in the tunnel

`PR-14` · `FL-07` · Day 45

## The one-line answer

Fluent speech is not fast speech — it is speech that stops in the right places, and the same
silence reads as *thinking* when it falls between two chunks and as *stuck* when it falls inside
one.

## The model

Almost everybody learning English believes the same thing, and it is wrong: that fluent means
fast, and that the way to sound better is to get the words out sooner.

Here is where the belief comes from. Fluent speakers do sound quick. But what you are hearing is
not somebody producing more words — it is somebody producing them **in runs**. A chunk comes out
whole, joined up, with no gaps inside it, and then there is a silence, and then the next chunk
comes out whole. The runs are fast. The silences are ordinary, and there are plenty of them, and
some of them are longer than yours.

Think of a train. A train standing still at a platform is a service. Doors open, people get on and
off, nobody on board is worried, and everybody understands that this is what stopping is for. The
same train standing still in a tunnel is a fault. Nothing has happened except the standing still —
same train, same silence, same amount of not moving — and every passenger knows instantly which one
they are in. Nobody has to announce it.

Your pauses work exactly like that, and your listener reads them exactly that quickly.

**The platforms are the joins between chunks.** Day 4 gave you the chunks and Day 15 gave you the
places where words run into each other and cannot be separated. Those are the same map read two
ways. *To be honest with you* is one carriage. You do not stop between *to* and *be*. You stop
after *you*, and a stop there is a platform: the listener catches up, you choose the next thing,
and none of it sounds like difficulty.

**The tunnels are the grammar words.** A silence after *the*, *a*, *to*, *of*, *and*, *that* is a
different event altogether. Those words are promises — each of them says *a word is coming, right
now*. Stop after one and the promise fails in public. The listener does not think *they are
thinking*. They think *the word has gone missing*, because that is the only thing a gap in that
position ever means.

That single rule is today's whole audible content. **Not how long you stop. Where.**

**Two pauses worth putting in on purpose.**

The first goes in front of the important sentence. A gap before a word does what your voice would
otherwise have to get louder to do — Day 41 already showed you that volume is not the ingredient
for anything. The silence frames it.

> And this is the important part —
> One thing.

Say either of those, then stop properly, then say the thing. From the inside the gap feels like an
eternity and like you have lost your place. On the tape it is short and it sounds completely
deliberate.

The second goes after a sentence has landed. Day 26's fall drops the last word onto the floor; the
silence after it is what lets the landing be heard. Run straight on and you have thrown it away.

**Now the part that kills the myth.** Speeding up does not remove your pauses. It cannot — you
still have to decide what you are saying, and deciding takes what it takes. So the silences arrive
anyway. What changes is that they now land wherever you happen to be when you run out of plan,
which is the middle of a chunk. **Speed does not delete your pauses. It moves them into the
tunnels.** That is why speaking faster makes you sound less fluent rather than more, and why it is
possible to be genuinely quicker and audibly worse.

Slowing down is not the fix either, and this is worth being clear about, because it is the advice
everybody gives. Slowing down evenly just makes long tunnels. The fix is to keep the runs quick and
put the stops on the joins.

**Today's phrases** — each one of these puts a silence somewhere on purpose, which is the only
reason it is here. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| And this is the important part — | Framing the sentence you actually care about, with a gap not with volume |
| One thing. | A short landing where the silence after it does all the work |
| Right. So — | Closing one block and opening the next, with the stop on the join |
| Which is where it gets interesting. | Earning a gap by promising something on the other side of it |
| Before I answer that — | Taking a deliberate front pause instead of a stuck one |
| Two reasons, and the second one's the real one. | Setting up the gap you want before the second reason |
| Park that for now. | Putting something aside so you can stop cleanly and move on |

## Say it now

Each line has a stop written into it. Take the stop properly — long enough to be uncomfortable —
and never move the stop somewhere easier.

1. *And this is the important part —* then the gap, then *{{the point}}.*
2. *One thing.* then the gap, then *{{the thing}}.*
3. *{{a finished sentence}}.* Land the last word, then the gap, then *Right. So — {{the next
   block}}.*
4. *{{the setup}} — which is where it gets interesting.* then the gap, then *{{what happens}}.*
5. *Before I answer that —* then the gap, then *{{the answer}}.*
6. *Two reasons, and the second one's the real one. {{the first}}.* then the gap, then *{{the
   second}}.*
7. *Park that for now — {{what you are moving to}}.*

Then run all seven together as one continuous stretch about a single subject, with the runs between
the gaps as quick as you can make them and every gap in exactly the same place. That combination —
fast runs, stops only on the joins — is what the thing you are chasing actually sounds like, and
you have probably never made it on purpose before.

## The deliberate failure

Take a story you have told many times and say it as fast as you physically can. Do not allow
yourself a single deliberate stop.

**What breaking sounds like:** the stops happen anyway, and now you can hear exactly where they
went. After *the*. After *and*. Halfway through a phrase your mouth had already started. Every one
of them sounds like a word going missing rather than like a person thinking, and the whole thing
sounds less fluent than your ordinary speech even though it contains more words in less room.

Then do the opposite version. Say the same story with a gap after every third word, regardless of
where the third word falls. Count them out if you have to.

Now listen to the two together. **The silences in the second version are all the same length as
each other, evenly spaced, perfectly calm — and it sounds like a person whose language is failing.**
Nothing about the length of a pause made that happen. The only thing wrong with those silences is
where they are. Same standing still, one train at a platform and one in a tunnel, and you did not
need anybody to tell you which was which.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are in a meeting and you start to feel you have been talking for too long. So you speed up —
which is the reasonable thing to do and it makes everything worse. You outrun your own planning
within a couple of sentences, your gaps start landing mid-chunk, and then somebody offers you a
word, or takes the turn off you altogether. They were not being rude. A tunnel pause looks like a
person who is stuck, and helping somebody who is stuck is a kindness.

**The repair:**

> Right. So — {{the one point}}.

Finish the chunk you are already inside, let the voice drop, take the gap on the join, and start the
next block from a standing stop. It is the only place in the sentence where stopping costs you
nothing, and it puts you back in charge of a turn that was leaving without you.
