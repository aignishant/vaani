## One-line answer

When somebody asks about your childhood, answer with one place, one person and one thing that
happened there — because a list of facts about your early life tells a stranger nothing, and one
small scene tells them everything.

## The story

A long train journey, six hours, and the person opposite has run out of things to look at. They
ask where you grew up.

The first answer:

> I grew up in a small town. It was near a river. There were four of us. My father worked in a
> government office. We shifted when I was twelve.

Five facts. All true. The conversation stops, because there is nothing in any of those sentences
that anybody can pick up.

The second answer:

> I grew up in a small town near a river. Our house was the last one on the road, so every stray
> dog in the area slept on our steps. My mother fed them and then pretended she hadn't.

Now the person opposite is telling you about their grandmother and the cat. Six hours is going to
be fine.

Same childhood. The second answer had a place you can see, a person doing something, and one small
event.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **childhood** — the time when you were a child.
- **grew up** — spent your childhood somewhere. *I grew up in Pune.*
- **scene** — one place at one time, with somebody doing something.
- **general** — true about many years at once, and so about no particular moment.

Three pieces make a childhood answer that goes somewhere.

| Piece | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A place | gives the listener somewhere to stand | *a small town near a river* |
| A person | gives them somebody to watch | *my mother* |
| One thing that happened | gives them something to react to | *she fed the dogs and pretended she hadn't* |

The mistake is not giving too few facts. It is giving facts that cover ten years at once. *We
shifted when I was twelve* covers a decade and shows nothing; *the last house on the road* shows a
place.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story from your life of at least two hundred spoken words.
Childhood is where most people's best stories are, and this is the day that makes them reachable
in English.

And this question is unavoidable. Anybody who talks to you for more than ten minutes — on a
journey, at work, at a wedding — will ask where you are from and what it was like. The answer is
the first real thing they learn about you.

## The rule

**The page checked for the verb forms, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple),
whose model sentences are about exactly this kind of talk:

> When I was a boy, I **walked** a mile to school every day.
> I **met** my wife in 1983.

**The two shapes a childhood answer uses**, and the difference matters:

```text
   things that happened ONCE          →  past simple
       One year the river came up to the gate.

   things that happened AGAIN and AGAIN  →  past simple, with a time word
       Every summer we slept on the roof.
       We walked to school every day.
```

Both are the same verb form. The repeated one leans on a time word — *every summer*, *on
Sundays*, *in those days* — to tell the listener it happened many times.

**Start with the place, in one sentence.** Not two:

> I grew up in a small town near a river.

**Then a person, doing something**, not a person described:

> My grandmother sat on the steps most evenings and told everybody's business to everybody.

Compare: *my grandmother was a very kind lady*. Nobody can see that, and every grandmother in
every answer is a very kind lady.

**Then one thing that happened.** A single event, from a single day if you can:

> One year the water came up to the gate and my uncle rowed a boat down our road.

**The ages and years that keep an answer anchored:**

| What you say | When to use it |
| --- | --- |
| *when I was small* | vague, warm, any early age |
| *when I was about eight* | when the age matters to the story |
| *in those days* | for how things generally were |
| *by the time I was twelve* | for a change that happened |

**And the sentence that invites the other person in**, because a childhood answer is a
conversation and not a statement:

> Where did you grow up?
> Was yours a town or a village?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the childhood as a list of facts**:

> I was born in 1994. My father was in a bank. We were three brothers. We shifted twice.

This is a form, not an answer. Nothing in it can be asked about without effort.

The second is **the whole childhood in one breath**:

> My childhood was very nice, we were a joint family, everybody was together, we used to play a
> lot, those days were very good, now everything is changed.

All general, all decade-sized, and it ends on a conclusion about the present. A listener has
nothing specific to hold.

The third is **the person described instead of doing**:

> My mother was very hardworking and my father was very strict.

Two adjectives that a hundred other people would also use. One action shows more: *my father
checked the lock twice every night*.

The fourth is **the answer that skips the place entirely**:

> It was nice. We were happy.

Without a place, a listener cannot picture anything at all, and the conversation has to be rescued
by them asking the question again in a different way.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Ask a fluent speaker about their childhood and you get one image almost immediately — a street, a
shop, a grandmother, a dog. The general facts come later, if at all, and usually because you asked
for them.

Under pressure, in a second language, general sentences are safer: they need no specific
vocabulary, no dates, no names of things you may not know in English. That is exactly why they
come out first, and why this needs practising on purpose.

What a native listener does with a good childhood answer is give you theirs. That exchange is one
of the fastest ways two strangers become comfortable with each other, in any language, and it is
worth being able to start.
