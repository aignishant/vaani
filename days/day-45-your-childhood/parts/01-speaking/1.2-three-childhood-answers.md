## One-line answer

Prepare three childhood answers of different lengths for the three questions people actually ask —
where are you from, what was it like, what did you do — because these three questions arrive in
every long conversation and you should never have to invent an answer from nothing.

## The story

Six hours on the train, and the childhood questions arrive in a predictable order.

First, at the beginning, while the seats are still being sorted out:

> Where are you from?

Short answer. Place, one line, question back.

An hour later, over tea:

> So what was it like, growing up there?

Medium answer. Place, person, one thing that happened.

Much later, when it is dark and nobody is reading any more:

> Did you have a favourite place when you were a kid?

Long answer. One scene, stretched, with what people said in it.

Three questions, three lengths, and all three are the same childhood. Somebody who has only one
answer gives the long one at the beginning and has nothing left at ten o'clock.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **prepared** — thought about in advance, not written and memorised.
- **stretch** — to tell one small part slowly, with detail.
- **invite** — to ask a question that gives the other person a turn.
- **specific** — about one moment, one place, one person.

Three answers, three sizes, for the three questions:

| The question | The answer | What it must contain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Where are you from?* | two sentences | the place, one detail, a question back |
| *What was it like?* | five or six sentences | place, person, one thing that happened |
| *Tell me about …* | one stretched scene | what people said, what you saw, how it ended |

Prepared does not mean written out and learned. It means you have said each one aloud three or
four times, so the words exist somewhere other than in a plan.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story from your life of at least two hundred spoken words. The long
answer here **is** that length, which makes this the closest practice to the gate in the whole
phase.

And there is a plain practical reason. These three questions are the ones a stranger asks, and
they are usually asked when you are tired, or in a noisy place, or with somebody you want to make
a good impression on. Having the answers ready is worth more than more vocabulary.

## The rule

**The page checked for the verb forms, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple),
with its regular examples — *called*, *liked*, *wanted*, *worked* — and:

> When I was a boy, I **walked** a mile to school every day.

**The short answer — two sentences and a question.**

> I'm from a small town near the river, about two hours from here. It's the kind of place where
> the bus stops when somebody waves at it. Where are you from?

One detail, and it has to be a detail nobody could guess. *It's a small town* on its own gives the
listener nothing to answer.

**The medium answer — five or six sentences.** Use the three pieces from
[part 1.1](1.1-where-a-childhood-answer-starts.md), then one sentence about how it was in general,
then a question back:

```text
   1  the place, with one detail
   2  a person, doing something
   3  one thing that happened
   4  how it usually was — "every summer we …"
   5  what changed, or what is there now
   6  a question back
```

**The long answer — one scene, stretched.** Choose a single afternoon, not a year. Then slow down
around it with three things:

- **what somebody said**, in their words:
  > My grandmother said, "Don't come back before the lights come on."
- **what you could see**, in one line;
- **what you thought at the time**, which is the part learners leave out and listeners like most.

**Speech in a spoken story.** When you quote somebody, English speakers usually say *and she said*
or *and she's like* and then say the line as it was said. Keep the frame of the story in the past
and the quoted line exactly as spoken.

**The rule about not performing.** Say each answer three or four times, on different days if you
can, and let it change each time. An answer said the same way twice is on its way to being a
recitation, and a listener can hear the difference immediately.

**And one thing to prepare in advance**: the two or three English words for things in your
childhood that you do not yet have — a thing in your house, a game you played, a food. Look them
up before you record, and write them into your lab page with where you checked them.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the long answer to the short question**. Somebody asks where you
are from and gets six sentences, and they now have to find a way out of a conversation they only
opened politely.

The second is **the short answer to the long question**:

> What was it like growing up there?
> It was nice.

Two words where the listener has opened a door. This is the answer that makes people stop asking.

The third is **the memorised answer**. Word for word, the same every time, with a shape that does
not fit the conversation it is in. It is worse than a rough answer, because the listener can hear
that it was ready before they asked.

The fourth is **a long answer with no speech in it**:

> We went there every evening and played and came back and it was very enjoyable.

Nobody says anything, so nobody is really there. One sentence of somebody talking changes the
whole scene.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

People decide very early whether a conversation is worth continuing, and the childhood questions
are where that decision usually happens. A short answer with one real detail keeps it open; a list
of facts closes it politely.

Under pressure, the medium answer is the one that disappears. Nervous speakers have a short answer
and a story they have told many times, and nothing in between — so the conversation goes from two
words to five minutes with nothing between them.

What a native listener notices is whether an answer is about a place or about a period. *We shifted
in 2006* is a period. *The last house on the road* is a place, and places are what people ask
follow-up questions about.
