## One-line answer

Record the childhood answer with every person, place and event taken out — only facts and dates —
then play it and the real one to somebody who does not know you, and ask them to describe you,
because the facts version produces an accurate description of nobody.

## The story

Two people on a train give their childhood answer to the same stranger.

> I was born in 1994. My father was in a bank. We were three brothers. We shifted twice, once in
> 2002 and once in 2006. I did my schooling in two different schools.

> I grew up in the last house on a road where every stray dog in the area slept on our steps. My
> mother fed them and then said she hadn't.

Ask the stranger an hour later what they know about each person. About the first, they can recite
four facts and cannot tell you one thing about who he is. About the second, they cannot tell you
the year of anything and they will say *she's the one whose mother fed the dogs*.

The first answer is not wrong. It is a form that somebody read out, and a form is nobody.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **facts version** — an answer made only of dates, places and roles.
- **impression** — what a listener is left with about you.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will record the same childhood twice.

- **The RED take — facts only.** Dates, numbers, jobs, moves. No person doing anything, no place
  you can see, no event.
- **The GREEN take — the medium answer** from
  [part 1.2](1.2-three-childhood-answers.md): place, person, one thing that happened.

Then somebody who does not know you well hears one take each — two different people — and answers
one question a day later: **what do you remember about that person?**

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story from your life. A story assembled from facts passes every
grammar test and fails the gate's actual condition, which is that a listener can follow it and
care.

And this is the failure that does the most damage outside the course. The facts version is what
most learners give in an interview, at a new workplace, or meeting a partner's family — the three
situations where being remembered is the entire point.

## The rule

**Build the RED take from true facts only.** Six sentences, every one of them checkable: years,
places, jobs, numbers of people. Nothing anybody did.

**Then the GREEN take**, the same childhood, five or six sentences with the three pieces in it.

**Then the listener test, with a day in between.** Play one take to each of two people who do not
know your history. Say nothing about the exercise. The next day, ask:

> What do you remember about that person?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the listener of the facts take
cannot name a single thing that happened, or answers with a general sentence — *he's from a small
town, I think*. It is **GREEN** when the other listener names the person and the event a day
later, even if they have forgotten every date.

If both are remembered equally, an event slipped into the RED take. Take it out and run it again.

If the last column is bigger than the other three together in any answer, that answer is a form.

**Then the repair drill.** Take six of your own fact sentences and turn each into a sentence with
a person doing something:

> My father worked in a bank.
> → My father came home at six and counted the day's change onto the table before he ate.

Record all six pairs, the fact first and the repair after it, so you can hear the difference in
your own voice.

**And the honest limit.** Some facts matter and belong: where you are from, what your parents did,
how many of you there were. The rule is not *no facts*. It is that a fact with nobody in it cannot
be the whole answer.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take that is secretly warm**. Tone rescues
it: you say the dates fondly and the listener fills in a person. Say them flatly, as if reading a
form.

The second is **asking the listener immediately**. Anything is remembered after ten seconds. The
day's wait is the test.

The third, in real conversation, is **the facts version coming out under pressure**. In an
interview or in front of somebody's family, the answer that feels safe is the one made of
checkable facts, because nothing in it can be wrong. It is also the one nobody remembers.

The fourth is **over-correcting into a performance**. Told that facts are flat, some learners go
the other way and tell a five-minute story to somebody who asked a polite question. The short
answer still has to be short; it just needs one real thing in it.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

People remember one thing about a person they have just met, and they choose it themselves. The
only power you have is over what is available to be chosen, and dates are never chosen.

Under pressure the facts version is produced by almost everybody, in every language. Its appeal is
that it cannot fail. It also cannot succeed, and the price is being pleasantly forgotten.

What a native listener does with the answer that has a person in it is ask about that person.
Every follow-up question you get — *did she really?*, *what happened to the dogs?* — is the
conversation continuing, which was the whole purpose of the question they asked you.
