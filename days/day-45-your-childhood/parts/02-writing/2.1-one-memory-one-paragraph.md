## One-line answer

A memory written well is one moment, not one year — a single afternoon, with one place, one or two
people and three things you could see, hear or smell — and the paragraph's job is to put the
reader in that afternoon rather than to summarise a period of your life.

## The story

An old photograph is going into an album, and somebody has to write a few lines under it.

The first attempt:

> This photo is from our childhood days. Those days were very happy. We used to play a lot and
> all the neighbours were like family. Life was simple at that time. Now everything has changed.

Five sentences about ten years. The photo shows two children on a step with a bucket, and the
writing does not mention the step, the bucket or the children.

The second attempt:

> This is the summer the tap outside started running for one hour at six in the morning. My
> brother and I carried the blue bucket between us because neither of us could lift it alone, and
> we spilled half of it on the way back every single day. My mother never once mentioned the
> spilling.

Three sentences about one summer, one tap, one bucket, two children and a mother who noticed
everything and said nothing.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **memory** — something you remember that happened to you.
- **moment** — one short piece of time: an afternoon, an hour, one morning.
- **sense detail** — something you could see, hear, smell, taste or feel.
- **summary** — a short account covering a long time.

A memory paragraph has four jobs, in about five to seven sentences.

| Sentence | Its job |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | put the reader in the place and the time: *the summer the tap ran at six* |
| 2–3 | what happened, with one or two people doing something |
| 4 | one sense detail — what it looked, sounded, smelled or felt like |
| 5 | one line that says what it meant, without explaining it |

The last job is the one to be careful with. A memory paragraph earns one line of meaning. Two
lines and it turns into a lesson.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past, in paragraphs, with speech marks used once. A
memory paragraph is one of those paragraphs, and it is the hardest kind, because nothing external
forces it to be specific.

And this is a piece of writing people keep. A dated account is useful; a memory is the thing a
family reads again in twenty years. It is worth being able to write one.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[My last holiday](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/my-last-holiday),
whose model opens by placing the reader — "My last holiday was a five-day trip to Prague in the
Czech Republic. I know Prague well because I lived there when I was at university, more than ten
years ago." — and whose tips are:

> Try to make your writing interesting for the reader. To do this, you can make it personal with
> your own memories and experiences.
> Use adjectives to add detail to your descriptions.
> Write clear and simple sentences and organise your ideas in short paragraphs. Give each
> paragraph a different topic.
> Use *so, but, and, because* and other linking words.

**Choose the moment by asking one question**: can you say what time of day it was? If you cannot,
you have chosen a period, not a moment.

> *Our childhood days* — a period.
> *The summer the tap ran at six* — a moment.

**Open with the place and the time, in one sentence.** Not with what it meant:

> This is the summer the tap outside started running for one hour at six in the morning.

**Put people in and let them do something.** Two people is plenty:

> My brother and I carried the blue bucket between us.

**Then the sense detail — exactly one, chosen for being small.** Not a beautiful view: a sound, a
smell, the weight of something, the colour of a thing nobody would think to mention.

> The handle was too thin and left a line across our palms.

**Then one line of meaning, said flat.** No explanation:

> My mother never once mentioned the spilling.

Compare with the version that explains:

> My mother never mentioned the spilling, which shows how kind she was, and I understood only
> later how much she did for us.

The first line leaves the reader to feel it. The second tells them what to feel, and stops them
feeling it.

**Tense, which is where memory paragraphs slip.** The whole paragraph is in the past. Things that
happened repeatedly use the past too, with a time word:

> Every morning we carried the bucket back.     ← repeated, past
> One morning we dropped it on the steps.       ← once, past

**The shape:**

```text
   ▇▇▇▇▇        place and time — one sentence
   ▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇     people, doing something
   ▇▇▇▇▇▇       one sense detail, small
   ▇▇▇          one flat line of meaning
```

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the decade paragraph**:

> Our childhood days were very happy. We used to play a lot. Life was simple then.

Nothing here happened on any particular day, so there is nothing for a reader to picture.

The second is **the ending that explains itself**:

> That is why I still believe that family is the most important thing in life.

A memory that ends in a moral asks the reader to agree rather than to see. One flat line is
stronger and harder to write.

The third is **adjectives instead of detail**:

> It was a beautiful, wonderful, very happy morning.

Three adjectives and no bucket. One concrete noun does more than five adjectives — which is the
opposite of what the word *descriptive* suggests to most people.

The fourth is **the paragraph that changes moment halfway**:

> We carried the bucket every morning. Later we moved to another town and the school was far, and
> my brother started going by cycle.

Two memories in one paragraph. The second one is a different paragraph, on a different day.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

The memories that survive being written down are small. Nobody remembers a paragraph about happy
childhood days; everybody remembers the bucket and the line across the palm, including the person
who wrote it, twenty years later.

Under pressure — writing for a family album, a card, a message after a death — people reach for
the general and the noble, because it feels respectful. What it actually does is make the piece
interchangeable with everybody else's.

A reader who was there gets something else from the specific version: they are put back in the
place. That is why the small detail is not decoration. It is the only part that can carry somebody
else's memory as well as yours.
