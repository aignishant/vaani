## One-line answer

Write three memory paragraphs, give one to somebody who was there and one to somebody who was not,
and keep both reactions — because the person who was there tells you whether it is true, and the
person who was not tells you whether it is readable.

## The story

The paragraph under the photograph is finished, and it goes to two people.

The brother who carried the other side of the bucket reads it and says:

> It was green, not blue.

That is the first kind of reading, and it matters: a memory that is wrong in a checkable detail
loses its authority, even when nobody else would ever know.

A friend who has never been to that town reads it and says:

> Why was the tap only on for an hour?

That is the second kind. Nothing is wrong — something is missing, and only somebody outside the
memory can see the hole.

Two readers, two different repairs, and the paragraph after both of them is better than it could
have been from either one alone.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **draft** — a first version.
- **checkable** — able to be confirmed as right or wrong by somebody else.
- **hole** — something a reader needs that is not on the page.
- **revise** — to change a piece of writing after somebody has read it.

The work is three paragraphs and two readings.

| Paragraph | About |
| --- | --- |
| One | a place from your childhood — a room, a road, a shop |
| Two | a person from your childhood, doing one thing |
| Three | one day you remember for a reason |

Then **one reader who was there** and **one who was not**, each reading one paragraph, and one
revision from each.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in paragraphs. Three memory paragraphs is most of a page, and
the one about the day you remember is very often the page itself.

And these pieces are kept. Unlike the day's message or the day's complaint, a memory paragraph
gets read again — which is a reason to write it truthfully and to check the details that can be
checked.

## The rule

**The page checked for this, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[My last holiday](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/my-last-holiday),
whose tips this part applies:

> Try to make your writing interesting for the reader. To do this, you can make it personal with
> your own memories and experiences.
> Use adjectives to add detail to your descriptions.
> Write clear and simple sentences and organise your ideas in short paragraphs. Give each
> paragraph a different topic.

**Each paragraph gets the four jobs** from
[part 2.1](2.1-one-memory-one-paragraph.md): place and time, people doing something, one sense
detail, one flat line.

**Before writing each one, note the time of day.** One word at the top of the page: *morning*,
*after school*, *night*. If it will not come, the memory is a period and needs choosing again.

**Write them on different days if you can.** Three memory paragraphs in one sitting start to sound
like each other, because the same three sentences shapes come out.

**Then the two readings:**

| Reader | The question to ask | What it finds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Somebody who was there | *Is anything wrong?* | details that are untrue |
| Somebody who was not | *Is anything missing?* | holes only an outsider sees |

Write both answers down word for word:

| Paragraph | What the person who was there said | What the outsider said | What I changed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**When the two readers disagree, the outsider wins on clarity and the witness wins on fact.** That
is the rule that settles almost every disagreement about a memory piece.

**Then the one-detail test on each paragraph.** Underline your sense detail. Ask: could this
detail belong to anybody else's childhood? If yes, find a smaller one — the handle, the smell of
the bag, the sound the gate made.

**Then put one line of speech into one of the three.** A remembered sentence, in speech marks,
exactly as it was said:

> My grandmother said, "Don't come back before the lights come on."

Speech marks are part of the phase 3 gate, and one remembered line is the most natural place to
practise them. The words inside the marks are the words that were said; the full stop goes inside
the closing mark.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **three paragraphs about the same person**. They blur into one
piece and none of them gets its own detail. Different subjects: a place, a person, a day.

The second is **fixing everything the witness says**. Somebody who was there will want to add
their own memories — *and the neighbours also used to come* — which are true and are not your
paragraph. Take the corrections of fact; leave the additions.

The third is **writing for the person who was there**. The paragraph fills with names and details
that need explaining, and the outsider cannot read it. Write for the outsider and let the witness
check the facts.

The fourth is **the speech line that nobody actually said**:

> My mother said, "You must always help your brother."

Invented speech sounds invented, because real remembered lines are odder and shorter than the
ones people make up. If you cannot remember the words, write what she did instead.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Memory pieces get shared at exactly the moments when people are least able to write — a birthday,
an anniversary, a death, somebody leaving. Having written three in an ordinary week is what makes
one possible on a hard day.

Under pressure, writers reach for what everybody says: *those were the best days*, *she was always
there for us*. Those sentences are true and empty, and they are the ones a reader's eye slides
over.

A reader who was there is doing something different from checking you: they are testing whether
their memory and yours are the same. That is why a wrong colour matters more than it should. Get
the checkable things right and the rest is yours.
