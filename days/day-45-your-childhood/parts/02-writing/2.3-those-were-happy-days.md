## One-line answer

Write the memory as five sentences that could be about anybody's childhood, then give it and the
real one to two readers and ask each to draw what they read — because a paragraph nobody can draw
is a paragraph that put nobody anywhere.

## The story

Two paragraphs go under two photographs in the same album.

> Those were happy days. We used to play together all the time. The neighbours were like our own
> family. Life was simple and everyone helped each other. We were very lucky.

> This is the summer the tap outside ran for one hour at six in the morning. My brother and I
> carried the green bucket between us because neither of us could lift it alone.

Somebody is asked to draw each one with a pencil.

For the second, they draw two children, a bucket, a tap. It takes thirty seconds and it is roughly
right.

For the first, the pencil does not move. There is nothing in it to draw — not one object, not one
person doing anything, not one place. Every sentence is a feeling about a decade.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **interchangeable** — could be about anybody, so it is about nobody.
- **concrete** — able to be seen, touched or counted.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Two paragraphs about the same real childhood.

- **The RED paragraph.** Five sentences, every one a general feeling: *happy days*, *simple life*,
  *everybody was like family*, *we used to play a lot*, *now everything has changed*. No object,
  no person doing anything, no time of day.
- **The GREEN paragraph.** Your real one from
  [part 2.2](2.2-write-the-memory.md).

Then two readers, one paragraph each, a pencil, and one instruction: **draw what you just read.**

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story. A page of RED paragraphs is the commonest failed version of
that gate: correct, warm, and impossible to picture from beginning to end.

And this is the paragraph almost everybody writes on the first attempt, in any language. Seeing a
pencil not move is worth more than any amount of being told that writing should be specific.

## The rule

**Write the RED paragraph about your own real childhood**, so the two paragraphs cover the same
ground. Keep it warm and true. The point is not that it is a bad paragraph; the point is that it
is anybody's.

**Then the drawing test.** Give one paragraph to each reader with a pencil and a blank page and
say:

> Draw what you just read. Stick figures are fine.

Watch what happens, and write it down.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader of the general paragraph
cannot draw anything, or draws something generic — a sun, a house, two stick figures with no
objects — and has to ask you what it was about. It is **GREEN** when the reader of the specific
paragraph draws at least two things that are actually in the writing.

If the general paragraph gets drawn, some concrete noun survived in it. Find it, take it out, and
run the test again — and notice that removing one noun was enough to empty a whole paragraph.

Concrete nouns are things you could photograph: *bucket*, *tap*, *steps*, *bag*, *bicycle*.
*Happiness*, *life*, *time* and *childhood* are not.

**Then the conversion drill.** Take five sentences from the RED paragraph and turn each into a
sentence with at least one concrete noun and one person doing something:

> The neighbours were like family.
> → The woman next door kept our spare key on a nail behind her door for eleven years.

**And the honest limit**, because this can be over-applied: one stated feeling in a paragraph is
allowed, and it usually belongs at the end. What fails is a paragraph made **only** of stated
feelings. The flat line in
[part 2.1](2.1-one-memory-one-paragraph.md) is the one sentence that earns it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who draws from what they know about
you**. If they were there, or know the town, they will draw their own memory. Use readers with no
connection to the place.

The second is **explaining while they read**. The paragraph has to do the work alone.

The third, in real writing, is **the general paragraph that arrives when the feeling is strongest**.
The harder a memory is to write about — somebody who died, a place that no longer exists — the
more the writing turns to *those were good days*. That is exactly when one concrete noun does the
most work.

The fourth is **stripping out all the feeling**. A memory with nothing but objects is a list. The
shape that works is objects and people, with one line of feeling at the end, said flat.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

The pieces of writing that people keep and re-read are full of specific objects. That is not a
literary rule; it is how memory works. A reader cannot enter a decade, and they can enter a
morning with a bucket in it.

Under pressure — a card, a speech, a message on a sad day — everybody writes the general
paragraph, and everybody who receives one feels the same mild nothing. One remembered object
changes it completely, and it does not have to be an important object. The smaller, the better.

A reader who was there will forgive a clumsy sentence and will not forgive a paragraph that could
be about anybody's family. The specific version is what tells them you were actually present.
