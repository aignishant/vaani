## One-line answer

A spoken account of a trip has four moves — where and when, how you got there, one thing that
happened, and how it ended — and the third move is the whole account, because nobody wants your
itinerary.

## The story

Monday morning, in the doorway of an office, and somebody asks how the trip was.

> It was good. We left on Friday night. We reached at about six in the morning. Then we went to
> the hotel. We rested. Then we went out in the afternoon. Next day we visited two places. Then
> Sunday evening we came back.

Every sentence is true, and what has been described is a timetable. The person in the doorway
waited politely through eight sentences to learn that a trip happened.

> It was good, but we nearly didn't get there — the bus left an hour late and the driver decided
> to make it up on the ghat road. There was a woman behind us who prayed the entire way, out loud.
> By the time we arrived, half the bus was friends with each other.

Three sentences. One journey, one person, one thing that happened. The person in the doorway is
now late for a meeting and does not mind.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **trip** — a journey somewhere and back, usually short.
- **itinerary** — the list of where you went and when.
- **highlight** — the one part worth telling.
- **move** — one step in the shape of an account.

Four moves, and the third one is where almost all the words go.

| Move | What it carries | How long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | where, when, who with | one sentence |
| 2 | how you got there — only if something happened on the way | one sentence, or none |
| 3 | **one thing that happened** | most of it |
| 4 | how it ended, or what you brought back | one sentence |

The trap is that a trip contains a hundred true sentences, and the more of them you say, the less
the listener gets. One thing that happened beats nine places visited, every time.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story from your life with a beginning, a middle and an end. A trip
is the easiest material for it — everybody has one, it has natural edges, and it contains
somebody you did not know.

And this is the everyday question. Any absence from work, from home, from a group produces *how
was it?*, and the answer is one of the most-used pieces of spoken English there is.

## The rule

**The page checked for the verb forms, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple),
which gives *–ed* for most verbs and shows the irregular ones in use:

> We **went** to Spain for our holidays.
> They **got** home very late last night.

**Move one — where, when, who, in one sentence:**

> We went up to my aunt's place on Friday night, four of us.

**Move two — the journey, only if it earns a sentence.** A journey where nothing happened is one
of the hundred true sentences to leave out. A journey with something in it is often the whole
story.

**Move three — one thing that happened.** Choose it before you start speaking. The test: it has a
person in it who is not you, and something that changed. A view is not a thing that happened; a
woman praying on a ghat road is.

Then stretch it, exactly as a story is stretched:

```text
   where you were       We were three rows from the back.
   what somebody said   The driver said, "Sit properly, we'll be there by five."
   what you thought     I remember thinking it was still dark and he was already lying.
```

**Move four — the end.** How it finished, or what came home with you:

> We got back Sunday night and slept all Monday.

**Two sentences that do a lot of work in a trip account**, and are worth having ready:

> It was the first time I'd been anywhere that cold.
> There were about forty of us and one bathroom.

Those describe a whole situation in one line, which is what a trip account needs — you cannot
describe everything, so you describe one thing well and put a frame around it.

**And the answer to the question people ask next**, which is always some version of *would you go
again?* Have one honest sentence for it:

> I'd go again, but not by that bus.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the itinerary**:

> Friday night we left. Saturday morning we reached. Then hotel. Then lunch. Then we went to the
> lake. Sunday we went to the temple and then we came back.

Eight moves of the wrong kind. Nothing went wrong, nobody spoke, and the listener has been given a
schedule.

The second is **the review**:

> It was very beautiful. The weather was nice. The food was good. The people were friendly.

Four general judgements, no events. This is what people say when they did not choose one thing
that happened before they started talking.

The third is **the trip with no people in it**:

> The mountains were very big and the air was clean and there were a lot of trees.

Landscape without anybody in it is a postcard. One person doing one thing changes it into a story.

The fourth is **the account that never ends**. Each day gets its turn, and the account stops when
the days run out rather than when the story does.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Ask anybody fluent how their trip was and you get one thing that happened, almost always — the
delay, the argument with the driver, the family they ate with. The places come out afterwards, if
you ask.

Under pressure, in a second language, the itinerary is safer: the sentences are short, the verbs
are ones you know, and nothing can be wrong. It is also the version nobody asks a second question
about, which means the conversation ends and you get less practice.

What a native listener notices is whether there is a person in the account. Trips with people in
them get questions — *what did she say?*, *did you talk to them?* — and every one of those
questions is another turn you did not have to invent.
