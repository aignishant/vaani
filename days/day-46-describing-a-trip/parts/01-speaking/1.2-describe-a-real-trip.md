## One-line answer

Give a real trip account to a real person and count the questions they ask — because questions are
the only measure of a spoken account that matters, and an account that produces none has been
received as a report.

## The story

The same Monday morning, the same doorway, a different colleague.

You give the three-sentence version: the late bus, the ghat road, the woman praying.

They say:

> Praying out loud? For how long?

And then, after you answer:

> Was the driver actually that fast, or was everyone just scared?

Two questions in thirty seconds, and now you are in a conversation rather than delivering an
answer. Each question is also a free rep: you are speaking about your own life, unprepared, with
somebody genuinely interested.

That is what this part counts. Not whether the account was correct — whether it was answerable.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **account** — what you say about something that happened.
- **follow-up question** — a question about something you just said.
- **hook** — a detail in your account that invites a question.
- **measure** — a way of counting whether something worked.

The work is three accounts to three different people, and one number from each: **how many
questions did they ask?**

| Questions | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| none | it was a report; nothing in it was open |
| one | polite interest, one hook found |
| two or more | it worked; they are in the conversation |

The hooks are what produce questions, and there are only three kinds: a person, a number that
sounds odd, and something unfinished.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story of at least two hundred spoken words. This part is where you
find out which of your trips has two hundred words in it, and which one runs out at sixty.

And the questions are the practice. An account that produces questions turns one prepared minute
into five unprepared ones, and the unprepared minutes are the ones that actually build speech.

## The rule

**The page checked for the verb forms, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-simple),
with *called*, *liked*, *wanted*, *worked* and the irregular models:

> We **went** to Spain for our holidays.
> They **got** home very late last night.

**Prepare three trips, one sentence each**, before speaking to anybody:

> The one where `TODO(me)` happened.

If a trip cannot be named that way, it is not ready. Choose another or find the thing that
happened inside it.

**Then build in the three hooks.** Each one is a place where a listener can reach in:

| Hook | How it sounds | Question it usually gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a person | *there was a woman behind us who prayed the whole way* | *for how long?* |
| an odd number | *there were forty of us and one bathroom* | *forty?* |
| something unfinished | *we never did find out whose bag it was* | *whose was it?* |

Two hooks in a three-sentence account is plenty. Three and it starts to sound arranged.

**Then give the account to three different people** and count:

| Trip | Who I told | Questions they asked | Which hook they took |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**Record at least one of the conversations**, including their questions and your answers, because
your unprepared answers are the most useful thing on the recording.

**Then the long version.** Take whichever of the three produced the most questions and record it
alone, at about two hundred spoken words, adding length only in move three — the thing that
happened. Use what the listeners asked about as your guide: whatever they wanted more of is
exactly what the long version stretches.

**And the two-way rule.** A trip account is a conversation, so it ends with a question:

> Have you been up that way?
> Where did you go last, actually?

An account that ends with a full stop and a silence gets fewer questions, however good it was.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the account with no hooks**:

> We went to my aunt's place on Friday. It was nice. We came back on Sunday.

There is nothing to reach for, so the listener says *oh, good* and the conversation is over. This
is not about grammar — it is about what was offered.

The second is **answering the question and stopping**:

> For how long?
> The whole way.

A one-word answer to a follow-up question closes the conversation you just opened. Answer, then
add one sentence.

The third is **counting questions from somebody who was on the trip with you**. They ask nothing,
because they were there, and the count is meaningless.

The fourth is **changing the story to get more questions**. The moment an account is improved with
things that did not happen, you are practising fiction — and you will be caught by the second
question, which is always about a detail.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Conversations run on hooks. People are not being polite when they ask about the praying woman;
they are taking the thing you held out. A speaker who offers nothing to take is described later as
hard to talk to, which is almost never about their English.

Under pressure — a new workplace, a group of people who all know each other — hooks disappear
first. Accounts become short, safe and general, which produces no questions, which produces less
speaking, which makes the next account shorter still.

What a native listener does with a good hook is remember it. Weeks later somebody will ask
whatever happened about the bag, and that is what it looks like when a small piece of your life
has actually reached another person.
