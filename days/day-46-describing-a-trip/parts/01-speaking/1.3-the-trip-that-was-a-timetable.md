## One-line answer

Give the trip as a pure timetable on purpose — every day, every stop, in order, with nothing that
happened — and count the questions a real listener asks, because a timetable is correct English
that produces zero conversation, and zero is a number you can act on.

## The story

Monday morning, and a third colleague gets the timetable version.

> We left Friday night at nine. We reached at six. We went to the hotel and rested. In the
> afternoon we went to the lake. Saturday we went to the temple and in the evening to the market.
> Sunday morning we left and we reached at eight at night.

They say:

> Nice. Good you got a break.

And they go into the meeting. That exchange took forty seconds, used eleven correct past-tense
sentences, and produced no questions, no follow-up and nothing either of you will remember by
Wednesday.

It is worth building this on purpose once, because it is the version most learners produce by
accident, and it never feels like a failure while it is happening. Nothing goes wrong. Nothing
happens at all.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is
not one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **timetable** — a list of times and places in order.
- **hook** — a detail a listener can ask about.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will give the same real trip twice, to two different people.

- **The RED take — the timetable.** Every day, every movement, in order. No person who is not in
  your group, no odd number, nothing unfinished, nothing that went wrong.
- **The GREEN take — four moves**, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-a-trip-in-four-moves.md), with two hooks in it.

Then you count the same thing for each: **how many questions came back**, and how long the
conversation lasted afterwards.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate is a recorded story of at least two hundred spoken words. A timetable cannot reach two
hundred words without becoming a list of nine places, and a list of nine places has no beginning,
middle or end — so this failure fails the gate on its own terms.

And the ledger row here is a number, which is rare and valuable: *timetable version, zero
questions; four-move version, four questions.*

## The rule

**Use one real trip for both takes**, so only the shape changes.

**Build the RED take properly.** Eight to twelve sentences, all true, all in order, all about
movement and time. Nobody outside your group appears. Nothing goes wrong.

**Give the two takes to two different people**, on the same day if you can, and count:

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the timetable take produces no
questions at all and ends in a closing line — *nice*, *good you got a break*, *sounds lovely*. It
is **GREEN** when the four-move take produces at least two questions and the conversation
continues past your account.

If the timetable version gets questions, a hook survived in it. Find it — usually a number or a
person — take it out, and notice how one detail was carrying the whole account.

**Then the conversion drill.** Take five sentences from the timetable and turn each into a
sentence that could produce a question:

> In the afternoon we went to the lake.
> → At the lake there was a man renting out one boat, and he had a list with everybody's name on
> it.

Record all five pairs, timetable sentence first.

**And the repair for real conversation**, for when you hear a timetable coming out of your own
mouth: stop the list and hand over one thing.

> — anyway, the actual story is the bus. Do you want that one instead?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a timetable with one good sentence in it**. It
rescues the take and you learn nothing. Strip it back to movements and times only.

The second is **giving both takes to the same person**. The second one gets questions because they
already know you went somewhere, or no questions because they have heard enough about the trip.

The third, in real speech, is **the timetable given to somebody senior**. In front of a manager,
an official or a new group, learners give the safest version, which is the timetable — and the
safest version is the one that ends the conversation soonest, exactly where more conversation
would have helped.

The fourth is **believing the timetable is more accurate**. It is not more truthful; it is a
different selection. Both versions are true, and one of them was received.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

A timetable answer is not rude and does not sound foreign. It sounds like somebody who does not
want to talk, which is the opposite of what a learner intends. People adjust: they ask you fewer
things, and they give you shorter answers back, and both of you end up speaking less.

Under pressure the timetable is the default, because it needs no adjectives, no speech, no
opinions and no unfinished stories. It is the safest English in the world and it buys nothing.

What a native listener does with a timetable is close it politely. *Sounds lovely.* If you hear
that sentence at the end of your accounts often, this is why — and it is entirely fixable with one
person, one odd number, or one thing you never found out.
