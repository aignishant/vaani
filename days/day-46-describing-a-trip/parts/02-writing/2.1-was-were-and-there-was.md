## One-line answer

*Be* is the one English verb with two past forms — *was* for *I*, *he*, *she* and *it*, *were* for
*you*, *we* and *they* — and the same two words carry *there was* and *there were*, which are how
you say what existed in a place at a time that has finished.

## The story

A message to a cousin who is going next month to the place you went last year. She wants to know
what it is like.

> There is one shop near the bus stand. There are two hotels but they are full in season. The
> water is only in the morning.

Everything in that message is about **now**, and you have not been there for a year. Your cousin
will plan around a shop that may have closed and hours that may have changed.

> There was one shop near the bus stand when I went. There were two hotels, and both were full.
> The water was only in the morning, but that was last November.

Same facts, moved into the past, and now the message is honest about what it is: a report from a
year ago rather than a description of today.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **be** — the verb *am*, *is*, *are* and, in the past, *was* and *were*.
- **subject** — the person or thing the sentence is about.
- **existed** — was there, was present.
- **singular** — one. **Plural** — more than one.

Two tables. The first is the verb itself:

| Subject | Past of *be* | Negative | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | was | I wasn't | Was I? |
| you | were | you weren't | Were you? |
| he / she / it | was | he wasn't | Was he? |
| we | were | we weren't | Were we? |
| they | were | they weren't | Were they? |

The second is the *there* pattern, which uses the same two words and is chosen by what comes
after:

| What follows | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| one thing | there was | *There was one shop.* |
| more than one thing | there were | *There were two hotels.* |
| a thing you cannot count | there was | *There was no water.* |

Note what decides it: not the subject, but **the thing that comes after**. That is the difference
from the first table, and it is where most of the errors live.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3 writes about the past, and every description of a place in the past runs on these two
words. A trip, a memory, a childhood home, a complaint about a room — all of them are *there was*
and *there were*.

And these forms carry a fact that matters practically: that what you are describing was true
**then**. A message that says *there is one shop* claims the shop exists now, and if it does not,
the mistake is not grammatical — it is a wrong instruction to somebody making plans.

## The rule

**Two pages were checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**

The first, for the verb:
[The verb 'be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/verb-be).
Its table gives the past as "I was", "He/She/It was", "You were", "We were", "They were"; the
negatives as "I was not, I wasn't" and "You were not, You weren't"; and the questions as "Was I?",
"Were you?", "Was he/she/it?", "Were we?", "Were they?" Its example sentences include:

> The children **were** good.

The second, for the *there* pattern:
[Using 'there is' and 'there are'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/using-there-there-are),
which explains that *there is* says something exists or is in a place, singular, and *there are*
does the same for plurals — and which gives the past forms directly:

> **There was** a storm last night. (Past simple)
> **There were** a lot of cars on the roads yesterday. (Past simple)

**The pattern, laid out:**

```text
   subject + was/were            The room was cold.  ·  The beds were hard.
   there  + was/were + thing     There was a fan.    ·  There were two beds.
   negative                      It wasn't clean.    ·  There weren't any towels.
   question                      Was it far?         ·  Were there any shops?
```

**Questions with *there*** put *was* or *were* first and keep *there*:

> Was there hot water?
> Were there any shops nearby?

**The negatives are the sentences that do the real work in a description:**

> There wasn't a lift.
> There weren't any lights on the stairs.

**And the contraction on paper.** *Wasn't* and *weren't* are ordinary in a message to a cousin and
out of place in a formal complaint, which wants *was not* and *were not*. That choice is the same
one you make with every contraction.

**One thing that is not this rule.** *Be* never takes *did*:

> Was it far?          — not *did it was far*
> It wasn't ready.     — not *it didn't was ready*

Every other verb uses *did* for questions and negatives. *Be* does it alone, with these two words.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***there was* with a plural**:

> There was two hotels. There was many people.

The word after decides: two hotels and many people need *there were*. This is the single most
common error in written descriptions of places.

The second is ***we was***:

> We was late. They was already inside.

*We* and *they* take *were*. This one is common in speech among native speakers too, in some
places, and in writing it is read as an error every time.

The third is **the present left in a past description**:

> The hotel was nice. There is a shop near the bus stand and the water is only in the morning.

Half the description has slipped into now, which is the failure from the story above, and it
changes what the reader will do with the message.

The fourth is ***did* used with *be***:

> Did there was any water? It didn't was clean.

Understandable and not English. *Was there any water?* and *It wasn't clean.*

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Descriptions of places are among the most practical things anybody writes: what a room was like,
what was missing, what was there and what was not. Insurance claims, complaints about
accommodation, reports to a family about a hospital — all of them are built from these forms.

Under pressure the tense slips into the present, because a place you can see in your head feels
present. In a complaint this matters: *there is no hot water* invites the reply *it is working
now*, while *there was no hot water on the fourteenth* is a claim about a date.

A formal reader reads *there were no towels on the fourteenth and fifteenth* as a fact to check. It
is the same sentence you would write to a cousin, with the contraction removed and a date added.
