## One-line answer

Describe a real place you stayed in as it was when you were there — what the place was like, what
there was and what there wasn't — for somebody who is about to go, and then let them read it and
say, sentence by sentence, whether each one tells them about *then* or about *now*.

## The story

Your cousin is going next month to the guest house where you stayed last year. She asks for a
proper description, not a single line.

You write eight sentences. She reads them on her phone and replies with three questions:

> Is the shop still there, or was it there?
> You said the room has a fan. Did it have a fan, or does it have one now?
> Were there towels, or do I bring my own?

Every one of her questions is about the same thing. She cannot tell which of your sentences are a
report from last year and which are a promise about next month. Where you wrote *was* and *were*,
she knew. Where you slipped into *is* and *are*, she did not.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **description** — writing that tells a reader what something looks like or what is in it.
- **report** — writing that says what was true at a time that has finished.
- **past of *be*** — *was* and *were*, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-was-were-and-there-was.md).
- **negative** — a sentence with *not* in it: *there wasn't*, *there weren't*.

A description of a place you visited is a report. It says what you found on the days you were
there. The two words that keep it honest are *was* and *were*: *the room was small*, *there were
two beds*.

The trouble is that a place you remember feels present. You can see the room in your head right
now, so your pen writes *the room is small*. To a reader who is going there, *is* is a promise
about today. That is the mistake this part is built to catch.

A good description has three kinds of sentence:

| Kind | What it tells the reader | Example shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| what the place was like | the size, the feel, the state | *The room was small but clean.* |
| what there was | the useful things that were there | *There was a fan. There were two beds.* |
| what there wasn't | the things they should bring or plan for | *There weren't any towels.* |

The third kind is the one people leave out, and it is usually the most useful.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past. Every story puts people somewhere, and the
sentences that build the place — *there was a bench outside*, *the gate was locked* — are this
part's sentences. A story whose setting slips into the present reads as if the writer lost track
of when it happened.

And this is a piece of writing you will actually send. Somebody is always going where you have
been: a hospital ward, a hostel, an office, a relative's town. A description that keeps *then* and
*now* apart is one they can plan from.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.** For the verb:
[The verb 'be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/verb-be),
whose table gives "I was", "He/She/It was", "You were", "We were", "They were". For the *there*
pattern:
[Using 'there is' and 'there are'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/using-there-there-are),
which gives the past forms:

> **There was** a storm last night.
> **There were** a lot of cars on the roads yesterday.

**Step one — the anchor sentence.** Say when you were there, first, so every later *was* has a
time to belong to:

> I stayed there for three nights last November.

**Step two — what it was like.** Two or three sentences with *was* or *were* after a subject:

> The room was on the first floor. The beds were hard but clean.

**Step three — what there was.** Choose by the word after *there was* or *there were*:

> There was a fan and one small window.
> There were two shops near the bus stand.

**Step four — what there wasn't.** At least two negatives. These are the sentences your reader
will act on:

> There wasn't any hot water after nine in the morning.
> There weren't any towels, so take your own.

Notice the second one. The negative is in the past, and the advice after it is not. *Take your
own* is an instruction for next month. That is allowed, and it is the only place the present
belongs: **in advice, clearly separated from the report**.

**Step five — the honest now-line.** One sentence that says what you do not know:

> That was last year, so it may be different now.

The whole message has this shape:

```text
   anchor           I stayed there ... last November.
   like             The room was ...   The beds were ...
   there was        There was ...      There were ...
   there wasn't     There wasn't ...   There weren't ...
   advice           Take ...  Ask for ...          ← the only present tense
   now-line         That was last year, so ...
```

**Then the reader's test.** Give the message to someone and ask them to put a mark beside every
sentence: **T** if it tells them about then, **N** if it tells them about now. Every sentence
should be **T** except the advice. Any sentence they mark **N** by mistake is a place where your
verb slipped.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the description that starts in the past and drifts**:

> I stayed there last November. The room was small. There is a fan and the beds are hard. There
> are two shops near the bus stand.

The first two sentences are a report. The rest are promises. The reader cannot see where the
change happened, because nothing in the meaning changed, only the verb.

The second is **the missing negatives**:

> The room was nice. There was a fan. There were two beds. The staff were friendly.

All true, and the reader arrives without towels. A description that lists only what was there is
half a description.

The third is **advice mixed into the report without a break**:

> There weren't any towels bring your own and there was no hot water after nine.

The instruction is hidden in the middle of a run-on sentence. Put a full stop after *towels* and
give the advice its own sentence.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A fluent writer describing a place they visited does exactly this without thinking: past for what
they found, present only for advice, and one line admitting that things may have changed. The
admission is not politeness. It tells the reader which facts to check.

Under pressure — a quick message from a bus, a reply typed with one thumb — the present creeps in,
because the place is vivid in your head. The fix is not to write slowly. It is the reader's test
above, done once on your own message before you send it.

In formal writing the same sentences become evidence. A complaint to a guest house or a report to
an employer about a site visit uses *there was no hot water on the fourteenth* and never *there is
no hot water*, because the first is a fact about a date and the second is an argument about today.
