## One-line answer

Put four real *was* and *were* errors into your own description on purpose, give it to a reader
who has not seen the clean version, and find out which errors they catch and which ones slide past
— because the error a reader does not notice is the one that changes what they do.

## The story

Your cousin's reply comes back. She has read your description of the guest house twice.

> There was two beds, ok. We was going to take our own sheets anyway. But you said there is a shop
> near the bus stand — so I don't need to carry snacks?

She noticed *there was two beds* — it looked odd to her, and she understood it anyway. She did not
notice that she copied your *we was* into her own reply. And the one error that changed her plan
was the one that looked perfectly correct: *there is a shop*, which told her about today when you
only knew about last November.

Three errors. The loudest one did no harm. The quietest one sent her off without food.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **plant** — put a mistake somewhere on purpose so that you can see if it is found.
- **reader** — the person who reads your writing, not you.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The past of *be* goes wrong in four ways. Two of them look wrong to almost any reader. Two of them
look fine and change the meaning. This part plants all four in your own description and finds out
which is which for a real reader.

| Error | What it looks like | What a reader does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *there was* + plural | *There was two beds.* | notices, understands, trusts you a little less |
| *we was*, *they was* | *We was late.* | often notices in formal writing; may not in a message |
| present in a past description | *There is a shop near the bus stand.* | does not notice; believes it is true now |
| *did* with *be* | *Did there was any water?* | notices, and has to work out what you meant |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past, read by somebody else. A page with *there was
two* on it gets read; a page whose setting has slipped into the present gets misread, and nobody
tells you.

And this is the habit the rest of the phase depends on: knowing which of your errors are the
harmless ones and which are the dangerous ones, so your second read looks for the right thing.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[The verb 'be'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/verb-be)
gives "You were", "We were", "They were", and questions such as "Was he/she/it?" and "Were they?",
with no *did*.
[Using 'there is' and 'there are'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/using-there-there-are)
gives the past pair:

> **There was** a storm last night.
> **There were** a lot of cars on the roads yesterday.

**Step one — make the RED copy.** Take your real description from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-the-place-as-it-was.md). Copy it out and plant exactly four errors, one of
each kind in the table above. Write a list of the four for yourself and keep it hidden.

**Step two — give the RED copy to a reader** who has not seen the clean one. Say only:

> This is about a place I stayed. Is there anything you'd check with me before you went?

Do not say there are errors. You want to see what a real reader does, not what a proofreader does.

**Step three — then tell them** there are four mistakes in *was*, *were* and *there*, and ask them
to find them.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if any planted error was not found by the
reader when asked, *or* if the present-in-the-past error changed what they would do. It is
**GREEN** when you can then fix all four without looking at your hidden list, and the reader agrees
that the fixed copy says what you meant.

**Step five — the fix, smallest first.** Change only the verb:

> There was two beds. → There were two beds.
> We was late. → We were late.
> There is a shop near the bus stand. → There was a shop near the bus stand.
> Did there was any water? → Was there any water?

Nothing else in the sentence moves. If you find yourself rewriting the whole sentence, you are
fixing something that was not broken.

**Step six — your own read-back list.** Write two lines at the top of your notebook for every
description you write from now on:

> Read the word after *there was*. One thing, or more than one?
> Read every *is* and *are*. Then, or now?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **planting errors that are too easy**: *there were
one bed*, which any reader stops at. Plant the version you would really write, in a sentence you
would really send.

The second is **telling the reader first**. Once they know it is a test, they read like a teacher,
and you learn nothing about the reader who will actually get your message.

The third, in real writing, is **fixing the loud error and missing the quiet one**. Almost everyone
who re-reads a description finds *there was two* and leaves *there is a shop* alone, because it is
correct English. It is only wrong about time.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A native reader forgives *there was two beds* in a message without a pause. Many native speakers
say it themselves. In a formal complaint or a job application it reads as careless, and the reader
starts to doubt the facts too.

What nobody forgives is being misled. A description that promises a shop, a lift or hot water
*now*, when you only knew about *then*, is the one that gets a complaint back. Under pressure the
present slips in most easily, so the second line of your read-back list is the one to keep.

The honest fix a fluent writer uses when they are not sure is the now-line: *that was last year,
so it may have changed*. One sentence, and every *was* before it is safe.
