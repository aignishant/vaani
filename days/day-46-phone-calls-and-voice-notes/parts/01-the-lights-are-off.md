# Speaking in a room with the lights off

`IN-09` · Day 46

## The one-line answer

On a call there's no nod coming back at you, so everything the other person's face was doing has
to be done out loud instead — and a voice note is worse again, because there's no other person in
it at all.

## The model

Think of every conversation you've had in a room with the lights on. You never noticed how much
work the other face was doing, because you never had to do it yourself. It nodded. It leaned in
when it wanted a turn. It went slightly still when it lost you. You read all of that without ever
deciding to, and you steered by it.

Now the lights go off. That's a call. The room is the same size, the other person is still in it,
and you can't see a thing.

What people do badly in the dark is go quiet. What fluent speakers do is make noise — small,
constant, deliberate noise, so the other person always knows where you are.

**The nod becomes a sound.** This is the single biggest one. In the light, you nod through
somebody's sentence and they keep going. In the dark, you have to say it.

> Mm — right, yeah.
> Oh, did they?

These go on their pauses, not on their words. They carry no content at all and that's the point:
you're not adding anything, you're only saying *still here, still with you, carry on*. Leave them
out and the other person starts to wonder. Two or three unanswered sentences into the dark and
they'll stop and check on you, and the checking costs both of you more than the noise would have.

**You say who you are, even when they know.** The first thing out of your mouth on a call is your
own name and one line about whether now is alright.

> Hi, it's {{your name}} — is now alright?

You feel silly saying your name to somebody who has your number. Say it anyway. It gives them a
beat to place your voice before they have to do anything with it, and that beat is worth more than
the half second it costs.

**The frown becomes a question.** In the light you'd see them not following. In the dark you have
to ask, and you have to ask on a schedule you choose rather than waiting for a signal that isn't
coming.

> Still with me?

Short, cheap, and repeatable. You can say it three times in one call and nobody minds. What you
cannot do is guess.

**The overlap.** The line has a lag in it. Not much, but enough that the end of their sentence
reaches you after they've stopped, so you start, and by then they've started too. Now you're both
talking and both stopping, and then both starting again into the same gap.

The fix isn't politeness. Politeness is what causes it — two people deferring into the same
silence, over and over. The fix is that **one of you gives it away immediately and says so out
loud**:

> Sorry, you go —

Say it fast, on the first collision, before the little dance starts. Day 30 gave you *Go on — you
first* for exactly this on a laggy line, and it's the same move: one turn spent, four turns saved.
The thing that costs you is treating the overlap as an accident to be sorted out politely rather
than a thing you can end in two words.

**Then the other half: voice notes.** A voice note has none of this, because it has no turn-taking
at all. Nobody can interrupt you, nobody can ask, nobody can nod. It's one turn, sent whole, and
whatever's wrong with it stays wrong.

Which means the shaping has to happen before you press record, not during. Three moves:

**Say what it's about, first.** Before any of the content. The listener is going to hear this
while doing something else, and they need to know within a sentence why they're listening.

> Just so you know what this is about — it's the {{thing}}.

**Then the content, and only the content that serves the ask.**

**Then say what you want back.** A voice note that ends without an ask is a broadcast. It gets a
thumbs up and nothing else.

> Let me know either way.

**And close it.** Land a last sentence and stop. A voice note that trails off leaves the listener
genuinely unsure whether it cut out, and they'll play the end again to check.

> So — that's the whole thing.

Same at the end of a call, where there's no door to walk out of and nothing to look at, so somebody
has to say the thing that means *we're finished*:

> I'll let you go.

## Say it now

Say each of these on real material — a real call you owe somebody, a real thing you'd send a note
about.

1. *Hi, it's {{your name}} — is now alright? It's about {{the thing}}.*
2. *Mm — right, yeah.*
3. *Can you hear me alright? You're {{breaking up / very quiet}} at this end.*
4. *Still with me? I'll go back a step if not.*
5. *Sorry, you go — I'll wait.*
6. *I'll let you go. I'll {{what you'll do next}}.*
7. *Just so you know what this is about — {{the subject, in five words}}.*
8. *Let me know either way, and I'll {{what you'll do with the answer}}.*
9. *So — that's the whole thing.*

Now run them again with your eyes shut, facing away from the room, getting nothing back off
anything. That's not a gimmick — it's the actual condition. You're checking whether the lines
still arrive when there's no face in front of you feeding you a rhythm, because the rhythm is
exactly what you lose on a call.

  talking over you once on purpose.

## The deliberate failure

Send yourself a voice note with no shape at all. Press record and start thinking out loud — begin
in the middle, work out what you mean while you're talking, and stop when you run out.

Then play it back and try to write down, from the note alone, exactly what you're being asked to
do.

**What breaking sounds like:** it doesn't sound broken. That's the finding, and it's an
uncomfortable one. It's fluent. The sentences are fine. Nothing in it would make anybody ask you
to repeat yourself. It just never says what it's about and never asks for anything, so there's
nothing for the listener to do with it except nod at the screen — which is precisely why this kind
of note gets a thumbs up and no answer, and why you assumed the other person was being slow.

Now record the same note again with the head on the front and the ask on the end. It'll be
shorter. Play them back one after the other and notice which one you could act on without playing
it twice.

## When it breaks in the wild

You're on a call and the other person is explaining something long. You're listening properly —
concentrating, in fact, which is why you've gone completely quiet. Halfway through they stop and
say *hello? are you still there?* You say yes, and now they've lost their thread and you've both
got a small awkwardness to clear up before either of you can go on. You were paying more attention
than you'd have paid in a room, and it read as absence.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — I'm here, I was just listening. Go on.

Then start making the noise. Not because you weren't listening, but because on a call, listening
that can't be heard isn't listening as far as the other person is concerned. It's the one place
where being quiet is worse than being slightly in the way.
