# The three rungs down to where they are

`FN-14` · Day 47

## The one-line answer

You explain a complicated thing in three moves, in this order — a whole sentence saying what it
is, a comparison to something ordinary, and one example — and jargon isn't the enemy, unexplained
jargon is.

## The model

Here's the picture to hold all day. The thing you know is at the top of a ladder. You've been up
there so long you've forgotten climbing it. The person asking is on the floor. And what everyone
does, without exception, is reach down and offer the top rung — because from up there the top rung
is the obvious thing to hand over.

Nobody can take hold of a rung that isn't attached to anything below it.

So you come down three rungs and hand over from there.

**Rung one: the whole sentence.** Not the name of the thing. A sentence, with a verb in it and a
person in it.

> It's a way of {{doing what}}, for {{who}}.

The reason a name doesn't work is that a name is a handle on a box the other person hasn't got. If
you say *it's a scheduler*, the only people who understand you are the people who already knew.
The sentence has to carry its own weight: what it does, and who it does it for. Say the sentence,
then say the name if you want to. That order round.

Test it in your mouth right now. Take the thing you spend most of your day on and say one sentence
about it that contains a verb and a person. If what comes out is a noun phrase, you're still on
the top rung.

**Rung two: the comparison to something ordinary.** Ordinary means something in a kitchen, on a
street, in a queue. Day 23 gave you the machinery for this — *it's a bit like a …*, *think a …,
but …* — and today you're aiming it at the hardest possible target, which is the thing you know
best.

> The closest everyday thing is {{the ordinary thing}}.
> Same idea as {{the ordinary thing}}, except {{the one difference that matters}}.

The comparison doesn't have to be accurate. It has to be *nearly right and immediately available*.
An almost-right comparison the other person already owns beats a perfect description they have to
build from scratch, every time. And say the one place it breaks, because a comparison with no
stated limit quietly becomes a wrong belief.

**Rung three: one example.** Exactly one. Not two, and never a list.

> So, for instance — {{one thing that actually happened}}.

Two examples is the sound of somebody who doesn't trust the first one. The moment you add a second
you've turned an explanation into a survey, and the listener stops holding the thing and starts
sorting the things. One concrete case, from real life, with a time and a person in it.

Then stop.

> That's the whole of it, really.

Stopping is a move. There's always more, you always know it, and the pull to add the qualification
is enormous. The qualification is for people who asked a second question. Nobody's asked one yet.

**Now the thing about jargon.** The instinct is to strip it out and speak in plain words only.
That's the wrong lesson and it makes you sound vague. A specialist word does real work — it's
precise and it's short — and taking it away means you now need six words where you had one.

Keep it. Glue a gloss to it, in the same breath, first time only:

> …the {{term}} — which just means {{plain words}} — and once that's set up, …

Said in one breath, with the dashes doing the work, this costs almost nothing and it hands the
listener the word. They now own it. Two sentences later you can use it bare and they'll follow.
That's the difference between a word that teaches and a word that walls somebody out, and it is
entirely a matter of whether you paused to hook it up.

**And one line that keeps you off the ladder:**

> Shall I go a level down?

Ask it rather than deciding for them. Both answers are useful — a yes tells you to keep going, and
a no tells you they've got what they came for and you can stop, which is information you would
otherwise never have had.

**Today's phrases** — the three rungs, plus the two lines that stop you climbing back up. All of
these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| It's a way of {{doing what}}, for {{who}}. | Rung one — the whole sentence, before the name |
| The closest everyday thing is … | Rung two — reaching for the ordinary comparison |
| Same idea as {{ordinary thing}}, except … | Rung two, when the comparison is close but not exact |

## Say it now

Pick one thing you genuinely know and most people don't. The same thing for all eight lines.

1. *It's a way of {{doing what}}, for {{who}}.*
2. *The closest everyday thing is {{the ordinary thing}}.*
3. *Same idea as {{the ordinary thing}}, except {{the one difference}}.*
4. *So, for instance — {{one thing that actually happened}}.*
5. *…the {{term}} — which just means {{plain words}} — {{carry the sentence on}}.*
6. *That's the whole of it, really.*
7. *Shall I go a level down?*
8. *Where it gets interesting is {{the one part}}.*

Then run lines 1 to 4 as one continuous run, twice, on the same thing, with no gaps — sentence,
comparison, example, stop. That run is the whole day compressed. When it comes out in one breath
without you choosing the next move, it's yours.

> does exactly what you do. **Then** to somebody bright who works in a completely different field.

The three versions must be different, and if you find they're not, that's the finding rather than
a mistake.

- **Solo form:** as written. Do all three without stopping the recorder, and don't rehearse the
  second and third while you're saying the first.
- **With a partner:** they're the outsider for version two. One instruction for them — every time
  you use a word they couldn't define, they say the word back to you, and you gloss it and carry
  on without starting the sentence again.

## The deliberate failure

Explain the same thing from the top rung. Start with its name, use every specialist word you'd use
with a colleague, gloss nothing, assume everything, and keep going for as long as you can. Don't
slow down and don't check on anybody.

**What breaking sounds like:** it's the most fluent thing you'll record this week. No hesitation,
no groping for words, no pauses that go anywhere. It's smooth because it's the version you actually
say, several times a week, to people who already understand it — so your mouth has had years of
practice at it and none at all at the other one.

That's the uncomfortable bit. The unusable version *sounds better*. The good version — sentence,
comparison, one example — comes out slower and rougher, with visible seams, and you'll want to
believe the fluent one was better English. It wasn't better English. It was easier English, and it
was easier because it was aimed at somebody who was already at the top of the ladder with you.

## When it breaks in the wild

You're somewhere social and somebody asks what you do. You give them the name of it. They say *oh,
right* in the tone people use when they've got nothing, and you both stand there for a beat, and
then one of you changes the subject. You've had this exact exchange dozens of times and each time
you've concluded, quietly, that your work is hard to explain.

It isn't. You handed over the top rung and they had nothing to hold.

**The repair:**

> Let me back up a bit — it's a way of {{doing what}}, for {{who}}.

No apology in front of it and no *sorry, it's quite technical*. That line does nothing but tell
them the failure is theirs. Back up, say the sentence, and let them decide whether they want the
next rung.
