## One-line answer

When you correct somebody or set one thing against another, you make the one word that carries the
difference the loudest word in the sentence — *I said TUESday* — and let every word after it go
quiet, so the listener hears which part of their picture to change.

## The story

You booked a delivery for Tuesday. On the phone, the man at the depot reads it back:

> Okay, so Thursday, between ten and one.

You answer:

> No, I said Tuesday.

You said every word at the same strength. He hears a complaint, not a correction. He says:

> Sorry, sorry. Thursday, ten to one. Done.

You try again, and this time one word is bigger than the others:

> No — TUESday. Not THURSday.

He stops. He changes one word on his screen. He heard exactly which part of what he said was
wrong, because your voice pointed at it.

Same words, the second time. One of them was pushed.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **stress** — saying a part of a word louder and longer. You met it inside words on
  [day 7](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-loud-syllable.md).
- **syllable** — one beat of a word. *Tues-day* has two.
- **loud words and quiet words** — in a normal sentence the meaning words are loud and the small
  words are quiet, from
  [day 13](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/01-speaking/1.1-loud-words-and-quiet-words.md).
- **emphasis** — making one word stand out more than all the others, on purpose.
- **contrast** — setting one thing against another: *Tuesday, not Thursday*.

On day 13 you learned that a normal English sentence has several loud words. That is the everyday
beat.

Today is different. When you are **correcting** something, or **choosing** one thing over another,
one word jumps above all the others. It becomes the biggest word in the sentence. Everything after
it drops down and goes quiet.

That one big word tells the listener: *this is the word that is different from what you thought*.
They keep the rest of their picture and change only that word.

You do not choose the loud part of the word. The word keeps its own stress from day 7. *TUESday* is
pushed on *TUES*, because that is where its stress already lives. You only choose **which word**.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every correction you make on the phone, at a counter or at work needs this: a wrong date, a wrong
number, a wrong name. Without it, a correction sounds like a repeat, and the listener repeats their
mistake back to you.

And the phase 3 gate is a story from your life told to a stranger. Stories are full of contrast —
*I thought it was the small bag, but it was the BIG one* — and a story with no pushed words sounds
flat even when every word is right.

**The page checked for the rule, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Stress (linguistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_(linguistics)). Its section *Prosodic
stress* says that stress "is also often used pragmatically to emphasize (focus attention on)
particular words or the ideas associated with them", and gives one sentence with two meanings,
decided only by which word is pushed:

> I didn't take the test **yesterday**. (I took it some other day.)
> I didn't **take** the test yesterday. (I did not take it.)

**The three things your voice does to the pushed word:**

| What changes | On the pushed word | On the words after it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| loudness | louder | quiet |
| length | longer | short |
| pitch — how high your voice is | a clear jump up, then down | low and flat |

Here is the shape of *No, I said TUESday* in your voice:

```text
                  TUES
                 /    \
   No, I said  /        day.
   ─────────────            ─────  (low, quiet)
```

**The pushed word is the one that is different.** Ask yourself one question before you speak:
*which word is wrong in what they said?* That is the word.

> They said Thursday. → No, **TUES**day.
> They said two-thirty. → No, **ONE**-thirty.
> They said the red bag. → No, the **BLUE** one.

**Say the contrast when it helps.** A second pushed word, after *not*, makes the correction
impossible to miss:

> **TUES**day, not **THURS**day.

**Everything after the pushed word goes quiet.** This is the part most people forget. If the words
after it stay loud, the push disappears into them:

> I want the **BLUE** one, please. — *one, please* are low and quick.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the correction said flat**:

> No, I said Tuesday.

Every word the same size. The listener hears *I said something*, not *you got this one word wrong*.
Very often they read their mistake back to you again.

The second is **pushing the word in the wrong place inside it**:

> No, tues-DAY.

The word's own stress moved. *DAY* is the part Tuesday and Thursday share, so pushing it tells the
listener nothing, and *tues-DAY* is harder to recognise as a word.

The third is **pushing and then pushing again**: the pushed word is loud, and so are the next three.

> No, TUESday, BETWEEN TEN and ONE.

Now nothing stands out, and it sounds like shouting.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers push a word many times a day without deciding to. *I said the BIG bag.* *Not this
one — THAT one.* *She's my SISter, not my wife.* A listener follows these without effort, because
the push points at the part to change.

Under pressure — a noisy line, a busy counter, somebody who has already got it wrong twice — the
push gets bigger and the rest gets quieter. It does not get louder all over. A calm, clear push on
one word is more powerful than raising your voice on the whole sentence.

Many languages mark the important word with a separate little word, or by moving it to the front.
English does it mostly with the voice. If your first language works the other way, your English
corrections may sound like simple repeats to a native listener, and this is why.
