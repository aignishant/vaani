## One-line answer

Correct a real person's real mistake about you by saying only the part that is wrong, with that
part pushed, and count how often they fix it the first time — because a short, pushed correction
gets fixed and a long, flat one gets argued with.

## The story

The same depot, a week later. A different person reads your booking back:

> Flat two-oh-five, Block C, Tuesday morning.

Two things are wrong: it is flat two-oh-**six**, and it is Block **D**. You could say the whole
address again. Instead:

> Two-oh-SIX. And Block D, not C.

She says:

> Six, D. Got it.

Two corrections, one breath, fixed first time. She did not have to listen to your whole address
again and find the difference herself. Your voice found it for her.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **correction** — saying the right version after somebody said the wrong one.
- **detail** — one small piece: a number, a letter, a day, a name.
- **pushed word** — the one word you make the loudest, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-push-the-word-you-mean.md).
- **read back** — when somebody says your details back to you to check them.

When somebody gets one detail wrong, you have two choices.

| Choice | What you say | What the listener has to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| say it all again | *Flat two-oh-six, Block D, Tuesday morning.* | compare your whole line with theirs and find the difference |
| say only the detail, pushed | *Two-oh-SIX. Block D, not C.* | change one thing |

The second is shorter, and it is also clearer. It does the listener's work for them.

This part is practice with real details from your life, said to real people, until the push comes
without thinking.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the sound you use every time a form, a booking, a delivery or a name goes wrong. Phase 5
takes you to offices, banks and landlords, where a detail read back wrongly costs real money. The
push is how you fix it in one line.

And every story in phase 3 has at least one moment of *I thought it was this, but it was that*. The
push is what makes that moment land for a listener.

## The rule

**The rule, from [part 1.1](1.1-push-the-word-you-mean.md):** the word that is different gets
louder, longer and higher, and every word after it goes quiet. The words keep their own stress
inside them.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **repeating the whole thing, louder**:

> NO, FLAT TWO-OH-SIX, BLOCK D, TUESDAY MORNING.

Everything is pushed, so nothing is. The listener hears anger and still has to find the difference.

The second is **the correction that starts with an apology and never arrives**:

> Sorry, sorry, actually, I think maybe it was, the flat is two-oh-six.

By the time the detail comes, the listener has stopped listening for it. The detail should come
first, pushed.

The third is **pushing the wrong digit** in a long number, usually the last one, out of habit. The
listener changes the wrong digit and now two are wrong.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

A fluent speaker on the phone corrects like this all the time, and adds nothing: *SIX. D.* It does
not sound rude, because the push itself is polite. It saves the other person effort.

Under pressure — a call centre, a queue behind you, a person reading from a screen — the short
pushed correction is what gets through. The long polite version is what gets *sorry, can you say
that again?*

What a native listener notices is not whether you pushed the right syllable perfectly. It is
whether they can tell **which** detail you mean. Get the word right and they will forgive a lot.
