## One-line answer

Record one correction four ways — the right word pushed, the wrong word pushed, every word pushed,
and nothing pushed — and ask a listener what each one means, because a push on the wrong word does
not sound wrong; it sounds like you meant something else.

## The story

The depot again, and you are tired of calling. The booking is still on Thursday.

> I SAID Tuesday.

You pushed *said*. The man on the phone hears something you did not intend:

> Okay, okay, you said it. I'm sorry. I'll ask my supervisor.

He heard *I already told you* — an argument about whether you said it. He did not hear which day
you wanted. The booking stays on Thursday, and now he is also upset.

The words were right. The push was on the wrong word, and a push always means *this is the part
that matters*.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **pushed word** — the loudest word in a sentence, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-push-the-word-you-mean.md).
- **meaning** — what the listener understands you want.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A push is never neutral. Whatever word you push, the listener thinks that word is the point. So a
push on the wrong word does not come across as a mistake. It comes across as a different message.

Four versions of one correction:

| Take | What you push | What a listener tends to hear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | *No, I said TUESday.* | the day is wrong — change the day |
| B | *No, I SAID Tuesday.* | *I already told you* — irritation |
| C | *No, I said Tuesday* — all flat | a repeat; no clear correction |
| D | *NO, I SAID TUESDAY* — all pushed | shouting; the day is lost in it |

## Why Vaani needs it

Pushing the wrong word is how a polite person accidentally sounds rude in English. It happens most
on the phone and at counters, which is where phase 5 lives. Hearing it on purpose, once, is the way
to stop it happening by accident.

It also matters in every story you tell. A story where the push lands on *said* or *was* instead of
on the surprising thing sounds like a complaint rather than a story.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Stress (linguistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_(linguistics)), section *Prosodic
stress*: the pushed word "focus[es] attention on" that word, and the same sentence changes its
meaning with the push:

> I didn't take the test **yesterday**. (I took it some other day.)
> I didn't **take** the test yesterday. (I did not take it.)

**Step one — choose one real correction** from your own life, from
[part 1.2](1.2-correct-the-detail-not-the-sentence.md), with a sentence of four or more words.

**Step two — record the four takes A to D** from the table above, using your sentence. Record them
in a shuffled order and write the order down, hidden.

**Step three — play them to a listener** who does not know the plan. After each take, ask one
question only:

> What do you think I want you to change?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the listener cannot name the right
detail from take A, or names it from take B or C as easily as from A — which means your pushed word
was not standing out. It is **GREEN** when take A, and only take A, gets the detail named straight
away.

If it went RED, the usual cause is the words after the push. Say take A again with everything after
the pushed word low and quiet, and play it to the same listener.

**Step five — the repair drill.** Take three sentences you say often in real life. For each,
record the version pushing the word you mean, then ask yourself out loud: *if I pushed a different
word, what would I be saying instead?* Say that version too.

> I wanted the SMALL one. → I WANTED the small one. — *but I didn't get it*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a listener who hears the script**. If they have
watched you prepare, they will name the day from any take. Use somebody who has not seen the page.

The second is **four takes that sound the same**. If your takes B, C and D are close to A, the push
in A is too small. Make it much bigger than feels natural; a push that feels too big to you usually
sounds about right to a listener.

The third, in real speech, is **pushing *said*, *told*, *was* or *did***. These words push easily
when you are annoyed, and every one of them turns a correction into an argument.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Native speakers push *said* on purpose sometimes — *I SAID no* — and it is always a warning. If
you do it by accident, the listener hears the warning, and they react to that, not to your detail.

Under pressure, when you are correcting somebody for the third time, the push drifts to *said* and
*told* on its own. That is the moment to slow down and push the detail instead. It is calmer and it
works better.

A listener forgives a small push on the right word. They do not easily forgive a big push on the
wrong one, because they cannot know it was a mistake.
