## One-line answer

The topic sentence is the first line of a paragraph, and it makes a promise: it names the topic
*and* says what you are going to tell the reader about it — so that somebody who reads only that
line already knows what the paragraph is for.

## The story

The noticeboard by the lift in your building. People read it while they wait, which means most of
them read the first line of each notice and nothing more.

Two notices go up on the same day.

> Our building has many things to think about. Some people have said different things. The water
> tank will be cleaned on Saturday, so there will be no water from morning until evening.

> There will be no water on Saturday from morning until evening, because the tank is being
> cleaned. Please fill buckets on Friday night.

On Saturday morning, half the building is surprised. They all walked past the first notice. Its
first line promised nothing, so they did not read on. The second notice worked, because its first
line *was* the notice.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph** — several sentences about one thing, written together as a block. You met it on
  [day 13](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-paragraph-is.md).
- **topic** — what the paragraph is about, from the same part.
- **the spine** — the sentence every other sentence holds up, from
  [day 33](../../../day-33-your-job-or-studies/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-paragraph-with-a-spine.md).
- **topic sentence** — the spine, when it comes first.
- **the point** — what you are saying *about* the topic.

On day 13 you learned that a paragraph is about one thing, and that the first sentence says what
the thing is. On day 33 you learned that one sentence holds the paragraph up.

Today goes one step further. A good first line has **two halves**:

| Half | What it does | In the second notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the topic | says what this is about | *water on Saturday* |
| the point | says what you are telling the reader about it | *there will be none, from morning until evening* |

A first line with only the topic — *This is about the water tank* — is a label. A label tells the
reader what the paragraph is about, but not why they should care. A first line with the topic and
the point is a **promise**: the rest of the paragraph will explain it or prove it.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in paragraphs. A reader moving through a page decides at the
first line of every paragraph whether to keep going and what to expect. Paragraphs that open with a
promise can be followed; paragraphs that open with *there are many things* get skimmed.

Phase 4 is about arguing on paper, and its gate is two paragraphs, each with a topic sentence. This
is where that begins.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Topic sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic_sentence). It defines a topic sentence as "A
sentence that summarizes the main idea of a paragraph", says "It is usually the first sentence in a
paragraph", and says it should be "general enough to cover the support" that follows.

**Test one — the half test.** Can you point at the topic and at the point?

> Our lift has broken three times this month. — topic: *our lift*; point: *broken three times*
> The lift. — topic only; no point
> It broke again on Tuesday. — a single fact; see test two

**Test two — the size test.** The first line must be big enough to cover every sentence after it,
and no bigger.

```text
   too wide     Our building has many problems.
                → could be followed by anything; promises nothing

   right        Our lift has broken three times this month.
                → the next sentences give the three times and what happened

   too narrow   The lift stopped on the fourth floor on Tuesday at noon.
                → one fact; where do the other two times go?
```

A sentence that is too narrow is not wrong. It is usually a good **second** sentence.

**Test three — the cover test.** Cover everything below the first line with your hand. Ask: *what
will the next sentences say?* If you can answer, the promise is there. If the answer is *anything*,
it is too wide.

**Three first lines that look like topic sentences and are not:**

> In this paragraph I will write about the lift.

This is an **announcement**. It names the topic and hides the point. The reader has to read on to
find out what you think.

> Have you ever been stuck in a lift?

A **question** opener. It can work in a magazine. In a notice, a message or a letter, the reader
wants your answer, not your question.

> The lift is a machine that carries people between floors.

A **dictionary line**. Everybody knows this. It promises nothing about *your* lift.

**Where it goes.** First, in everything you write in this course. Stories and essays sometimes put
it later, for effect, but a reader who is busy should never have to hunt for it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the warm-up line**:

> There are many things in life that are important.

The writer is warming up. The reader has already stopped.

The second is **the topic with no point**:

> This paragraph is about my neighbour.

The reader knows who, but not what. Is the neighbour kind, noisy, leaving, ill?

The third is **the fact that is too small to hold the paragraph**:

> My neighbour moved in last March.

Then the next four sentences are about how helpful she is. The first line promised a moving story
and the paragraph delivered a helpful neighbour. Swap in: *My neighbour, who moved in last March,
has helped everyone on our floor at least once.*

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

People who write for busy readers — notices, work emails, news — put the point first almost every
time. They know most readers stop after one line, so the first line has to be enough on its own.

Under pressure, writers do the opposite: they write their way towards the point and put it last,
because that is the order they thought of it in. The fix is to write the paragraph, find the
sentence that says the point, and move it to the top.

A formal reader — an examiner, a manager, an office — expects the first line of every paragraph to
tell them what the paragraph does. A paragraph that makes them hunt reads as unclear, even when
every sentence in it is correct.
