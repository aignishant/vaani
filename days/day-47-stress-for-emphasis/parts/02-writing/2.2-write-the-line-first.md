## One-line answer

Write the topic sentence before you write anything else, then write only sentences that keep its
promise, and test it by showing a reader the first line alone and asking what comes next — if they
guess right, the line is doing its job.

## The story

Your building is collecting money for a new gate. The committee asks you to write one paragraph for
the noticeboard.

The first time, you just start writing. Seven sentences later you have covered the old gate, the
thefts last year, the cost, a meeting, and a thank-you. It is honest and it goes everywhere.

The second time, you write one line first and stop:

> Every flat is asked to give five hundred rupees by the fifteenth for the new gate.

Now every sentence you add has a job. It explains why, or how much, or where to pay. The meeting
and the thank-you do not fit, so they go in their own notices. The paragraph is shorter and
everybody who reads the first line knows what to do.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **topic sentence** — the first line of a paragraph, which says the topic and the point, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **support** — the sentences after the first line that explain it or prove it.
- **draft** — a first version you expect to change.
- **the cover test** — hiding everything after the first line and asking what comes next.

Most people write a paragraph in the order they think of it. The point arrives at the end, because
that is when they found it.

This part turns the order round. You find the point **first**, write it as one line, and then
every other sentence has to earn its place by supporting that line. A sentence that does not
support it goes into a different paragraph, or out.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in paragraphs. Writing each paragraph's first line before its
body is how a page stays in order, because you can read your first lines alone and see the whole
story.

It is also the habit phase 4 is built on. An argument on paper is a set of promises, each one kept
in its paragraph.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Topic sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic_sentence): a topic sentence "summarizes the main
idea of a paragraph" and is "usually the first sentence".

**Step one — ask yourself the reader's question.** Before writing, finish this sentence in your
head: *after reading this, I want the reader to know that …* Whatever finishes it is your point.

**Step two — write the topic sentence alone.** One line. Check it has a topic and a point. Stop.

**Step three — the cover test with a real person.** Show them the first line only, and ask:

> What do you think the next few sentences will say?

Write their answer down word for word.

**Step four — write three to five support sentences.** Each one must do one of these jobs:

| Job | Example after *Every flat is asked to give five hundred rupees by the fifteenth for the new gate.* |
| --- | --- |
| why | *The old gate does not lock, and two bicycles were taken last month.* |
| how much, how many | *The gate costs forty thousand rupees in total.* |
| how, where | *Please give the money to the guard in an envelope with your flat number.* |
| what happens next | *The gate will be fitted in the first week of next month.* |

**Step five — the fit check.** Read each support sentence and ask: *does this keep the first line's
promise?* Put a tick or a cross beside it. Every cross comes out.

**Step six — compare.** Put the reader's guess from step three beside the paragraph you actually
wrote.

the first line is too wide, or the support drifted. Fix whichever it is.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the first line written last and left at the bottom**:

> The old gate does not lock. Two bicycles were taken. The new one costs forty thousand. So every
> flat must give five hundred rupees by the fifteenth.

Every sentence is right. The point is in the last line, and the reader standing at the lift never
got there.

The second is **the support sentence that starts a new topic**:

> Also, the lift is making a noise again.

It is true and it matters. It belongs in a different paragraph. The word *also* at the start of a
sentence is often the sign that a new topic has walked in.

The third is **a first line that changes after the reader sees it**. Some writers, when the reader
guesses wrong, change the paragraph to match the guess. Change the first line instead, so it
promises what you actually wanted to say.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often draft in the thinking order and then move the point to the top before sending.
Either order works, as long as the paragraph that the reader sees starts with the promise.

Under pressure, the easiest way to get it right is this part's method: one line first, stop, check
it, then the rest. It feels slow the first few times, and it saves the rewrite.

A reader who only reads first lines — and at a noticeboard, on a phone, in a busy office, most do —
should be able to act correctly from your first line alone. If they cannot, the paragraph failed
them, however good the rest is.
