## One-line answer

Write a paragraph on purpose whose first line promises one thing and whose body delivers another,
give a reader only the first line and ask them what to do, then give them the whole thing — because
a broken promise is invisible to the writer and misleading to every reader who stops early.

## The story

A notice by the lift:

> The meeting on Sunday is cancelled. Some residents asked about the parking. We have spoken to the
> builder about the cracks on the third floor. The meeting will now be on Monday at seven, in the
> hall, and it is important that one person from each flat comes.

The first line says *cancelled*. Most people read it, think *good, no meeting*, and walk into the
lift. On Monday at seven the hall is nearly empty.

Nothing in the notice is false. The meeting *was* cancelled, and it *was* moved. But the first line
promised an ending — *no meeting* — and the paragraph delivered something else: *a different
meeting, and you must come*. Only the people who read to the last line got the real message.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not
one we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **topic sentence** — the first line, which says the topic and the point, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **broken promise** — a first line whose point is not what the paragraph is really for.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A paragraph can go wrong in two quiet ways. Neither makes any sentence wrong.

| Break | What it looks like | What a first-line reader does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **the drift** | the first line is right, then the paragraph slides to another topic | acts on the first line and misses the new topic entirely |
| **the buried point** | the first line is true but minor, and the real point is at the end | acts on the minor thing and misses the one that matters |

The notice in the story is a buried point. The real message — *come on Monday* — is the last
sentence.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story, and a story paragraph that opens with one thing and slides
into another is the commonest reason a reader loses the thread. In phase 4 an argument paragraph
that breaks its promise does worse: it argues against itself.

And a real reader, at a noticeboard or in an inbox, will act on your first line. This part shows you
what they do when that line is the wrong one.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Topic sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topic_sentence): it "summarizes the main idea of a
paragraph", so a first line that does not summarise the paragraph is not doing that job.

**Step one — write the RED paragraph about something real.** Choose a real message you might
send: a change of plan, a request, a warning. Write it with the real point in the **last**
sentence, and a true but smaller fact as the first line.

**Step two — the first-line test.** Show a reader the first line only. Ask:

> If this was all you read, what would you do?

Write down their answer.

**Step three — show them the whole paragraph.** Ask:

> Now what would you do?

**Step four — rewrite it GREEN.** Move the real point to the top and make it a proper topic
sentence. Cut or move anything that does not support it. Show the new first line alone to a
**different** reader and ask the same question.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the first-line reader of the RED
paragraph would do something different from the whole-paragraph reader. That is the broken promise,
seen. It is **GREEN** when, in your rewrite, the first-line reader and the whole-paragraph reader
would do the same thing.

**Step six — the drift audit.** Take the three paragraphs you wrote in
[part 2.2](2.2-write-the-line-first.md). For each, read the first line, then the last line, and ask:
*are these about the same thing?* If not, the paragraph drifted. Mark the sentence where it turned.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the paragraph that follows the order things happened**:

> The meeting on Sunday is cancelled. … The meeting will now be on Monday.

Events happened in that order, so the writer wrote them in that order. The reader needed the
newest one first.

The second is **the drift into a second topic**:

> The new gate will be fitted next week. It will lock at eleven at night. The lift is also making a
> noise and somebody should look at it.

The last sentence is a new paragraph pretending to be part of this one.

The third is **a rewrite that just adds a line at the top** without taking anything out. Now the
paragraph has two first lines. Move the point up and cut what no longer fits.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Change-of-plan messages are where this bites hardest. *The trip is cancelled … we'll go next week
instead* is read as *cancelled* by everyone in a hurry. A fluent writer puts the new plan first:
*We're going next week instead of this Saturday.*

Under pressure the writer's mind follows the story of what happened, and the reader's mind wants the
result. The paragraph has to follow the reader.

A formal reader who meets a buried point reads it as a trick, even when it was an accident. In a
complaint, a request or a report, the first line is what they will quote back to you.
