## One-line answer

A problem story has four beats — what you were doing, what went wrong, **what you did about it**,
and how it ended — and the third beat is the one a listener is waiting for, because a problem is
only a story once somebody does something.

## The story

The tea break at work. A colleague asks why you were late for the wedding on Sunday.

The first answer:

> The scooter got a puncture. It was very bad. Then I was late. It was a bad day.

Your colleague says *oh no* and drinks their tea. There is nothing else to say. Something happened
to you, and that is all.

The second answer:

> I was riding to the hall in my good clothes when the back tyre went flat, right on the flyover.
> So I pushed it down to the bottom, found a boy at a cycle shop who said he could fix it, and
> held my shirt away from the grease the whole time. I walked in during the photos.

Now your colleague is laughing and asking how much the boy charged. The problem was the same. The
second answer has **you** in it, doing something.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **problem story** — a short story about something that went wrong and what happened next.
- **beat** — one step in the shape of a story.
- **background** — what was already going on when the problem started.
- **action** — what you did.
- **the three parts** — where we are, what went wrong, how it ended, from
  [day 43](../../../day-43-story-with-three-parts/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-three-parts-a-listener-waits-for.md).

On day 43 you learned the three parts a listener waits for. A problem story uses those three and
adds one more in the middle: **what you did about it**.

| Beat | What it carries | How long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · background | what you were doing when it started | one sentence |
| 2 · the problem | what went wrong | one sentence |
| 3 · **your action** | what you did about it — the choice, the attempt, the person you asked | most of it |
| 4 · the ending | how it came out | one sentence |

Beat three is where the listener gets interested. Without it, a problem story is a complaint.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story from your life, at least two hundred spoken words, with a
beginning, a middle and an end. Most real stories people tell are problem stories: the bus that did
not come, the key left inside, the form that was wrong. The middle of that gate story is beat three.

And beat three is also what an interviewer wants in phase 5 when they ask *tell me about a problem
you solved*. The same shape, at work.

## The rule

**The page checked for the verb forms, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-continuous),
which shows how a background action and a sudden event sit together in one sentence:

> The children **were doing** their homework when I **got** home.

That is exactly beat one and beat two joined.

**Beat one — the background, with *was* or *were* and *-ing*:**

> I was riding to the hall …

This tells the listener *something was already going on*. You do not need a grammar name for it to
use it: *was* or *were*, then the verb with *-ing*. In speech, one sentence of this is enough.

**Beat two — the problem, in the ordinary past:**

> … when the back tyre went flat.

The word *when* joins the two, and the problem lands like a bump.

**Beat three — your action. Stretch this.** Three kinds of action make good beat threes:

| Kind of action | Example |
| --- | --- |
| a choice | *I could go back home or find a shop, so I pushed it to the bottom.* |
| an attempt that half worked | *I tried to call my brother, but his phone was off.* |
| a person you asked | *I asked a boy at a cycle shop, and he said give me ten minutes.* |

Inside beat three, say what somebody said. One line of speech makes a scene:

> He said, "Sir, this tyre is finished."

**Beat four — the ending, in one sentence.** Often a small, funny or honest detail:

> I walked in during the photos.

**The shape, in one breath:**

```text
   1  I was riding ... when          ← background
   2  the tyre went flat.            ← problem
   3  So I ...   I tried ...   He said ...     ← your action: most of it
   4  I walked in during the photos.  ← ending
```

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the problem with no action**:

> The scooter got a puncture. Then I was late.

Two beats. The listener hears bad luck and has no way into the story.

The second is **the story that is all feeling**:

> It was so bad, I was so angry, what to do, it was a very bad day.

The feeling is real, and it is the ending of a story, not the whole of it. The listener needs to
see what happened to share the feeling.

The third is **starting with the ending**:

> I missed the photos because of the scooter.

This works as a first line in some stories, but a beginner who starts here often stops here. If
you start with the ending, the next sentence must go back to beat one.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers tell problem stories all day, and they are mostly beat three. *So I …, and then I
thought …, and he said …* A listener leans in when you start weighing a choice, because they are
making the choice with you.

Under pressure, in a second language, beat three is the hardest part, because it needs more verbs
and more people. That is why it gets dropped, and why the story then shrinks to *it was bad*.
Practising it out loud is the only way it stays in.

What a native listener responds to is not the size of the problem. A lost pen with a good beat three
beats a car accident told as a list. What they remember is what you did.
