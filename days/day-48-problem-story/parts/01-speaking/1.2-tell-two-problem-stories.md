## One-line answer

Tell two real problem stories to two real people — one where your action worked and one where it
did not — and notice which one gets more questions, because the story where you failed and tried
again is usually the better one.

## The story

The next tea break, and it is your colleague's turn. They tell you about the day their phone fell
in a bucket of water.

> So I put it in a bag of rice, like everyone says. Two days. Nothing. Then my nephew said, "Just
> take it to the shop, uncle." The man at the shop opened it, shook it, and it worked.

You ask three questions: how long was it in the water, which shop, how much did he charge. The
rice did not work. That is exactly why the story is good. An action that fails and a second action
that works is a story with two beat threes.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **beat three** — what you did about the problem, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-went-wrong-and-what-you-did.md).
- **attempt** — something you tried.
- **turn** — the moment the story changes direction.
- **follow-up question** — a question the listener asks about something you said.

You will prepare two real stories and give them to two real people:

| Story | Beat three | What it teaches you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A — it worked | one action, and it solved the problem | the simple shape |
| B — it didn't, at first | an attempt that failed, then a second action | the turn |

Then you count the follow-up questions each one gets. That number tells you which of your stories
people actually want to hear.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate recording is at least two hundred spoken words. A problem story with one attempt
that failed and one that worked reaches that length naturally, without padding, because each
attempt is its own small scene.

And it is the story you will be asked for most in daily life: *what happened?*, *why were you
late?*, *how did you sort it out?* Having two ready means you are never stuck with *it was bad*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **hiding the failed attempt** because it feels embarrassing:

> I took it to the shop and he fixed it.

The rice is gone, and with it the part people enjoy. The failed attempt makes you human.

The second is **a list of attempts with no reaction between them**:

> I tried rice, I tried a hair dryer, I tried the sun, I took it to the shop.

Four actions, no turn. Say what happened after one of them: *the hair dryer made it hot and it
still didn't turn on*.

The third is **telling the story the same way word for word** each time, so it sounds read out. The
four beats stay the same; the words should change a little every time.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

A fluent speaker almost always includes the thing that did not work, and often laughs at it. The
listener joins in, because they have done the same thing. That shared moment is what turns a story
into a conversation.

Under pressure, in a second language, speakers tend to jump to the solution to get the story over
with. The listener then has no questions, and the conversation ends early. Slowing down at the turn
— *but it didn't work* — is the fix.

What a native listener asks about is usually the small odd detail inside beat three: the price, the
person, the thing somebody said. That is where to add detail next time.
