## One-line answer

Tell a real problem story on purpose as pure bad luck — things happening *to* you, one after
another, with nothing you did — and compare what a listener says back with what they say to the
version with your action in it, because a story with no *you* in it gets sympathy and nothing else.

## The story

A third colleague, the same tea stall. You tell the scooter story again, this time as a list of
things that happened to you:

> The tyre went flat. On the flyover. It was raining also. My phone battery was low. The shop was
> far. Then I was late for the wedding.

They say:

> Arre, bad luck. Anyway, did you see the match?

Six sentences, all true, all correct English, and the conversation moved on in one line. Every
sentence had the problem as the subject: *the tyre*, *my phone*, *the shop*. You appear only at the
end, and only as somebody who was late.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not
one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **subject** — who or what a sentence is about, the one doing the verb.
- **sympathy** — feeling sorry for somebody.
- **beat three** — what you did about the problem, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-went-wrong-and-what-you-did.md).
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Listen to who the sentences are about.

| Version | The subjects of the sentences | What a listener tends to say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **RED — bad luck** | *the tyre*, *it*, *my phone*, *the shop* | *oh no*, *bad luck*, then a new topic |
| **GREEN — your action** | *I*, *I*, *he*, *I* | a question about something you did |

The difference is not the problem. It is who is **doing** things in the story. When every sentence
starts with the problem, the listener can only feel sorry. When some sentences start with *I* doing
something, they can follow you.

## Why Vaani needs it

A phase 3 gate story told as bad luck will be correct and short and will not reach two hundred
words, because a list of things going wrong runs out fast. Beat three is where the length comes
from.

In phase 5, *tell me about a problem you faced* is a standard interview question. The bad-luck
version is the answer that fails it.

## The rule

**Use one real problem for both takes**, so only the shape changes. Use the one from
[part 1.2](1.2-tell-two-problem-stories.md) that got more questions.

**Build the RED take properly.** Six to eight sentences. The problem, the things that made it worse,
and the ending. Every sentence has the problem, a thing or the weather as its subject. You do
nothing.

**Build the GREEN take** in the four beats, with beat three stretched and at least one line of what
somebody said.

**Give each take to a different person** who has not heard the story. Write down exactly what they
say first, and whether they ask a question.

| GREEN — your action | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the bad-luck take gets no question
about the story and the listener changes the topic. It is **GREEN** when the action take gets at
least one question about something you did.

**Then the subject audit.** Play back both takes and write the first word of every sentence:

| Take | Sentences starting with *I* or another person | Sentences starting with a thing or *it* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RED | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| GREEN | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**Then the conversion drill.** Take four sentences from the RED take and turn each into a sentence
where you do something:

> My phone battery was low. → I switched my phone off to save the battery for one call.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take that sneaks an action in** — *so I
called my brother*. Once one action is in, it is not the pure version. Take it out.

The second is **testing on somebody who knows the story**. They will ask questions from memory.

The third, in real life, is **the story where the action belongs to somebody else**:

> The mechanic came and fixed it.

Somebody acted, and it was not you. That can be a good story, if you say what *you* did to find
him or what you said to him. If you only watched, it is still bad luck.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Everybody tells the bad-luck version sometimes, usually when tired. It is fine as a quick answer to
*why are you late?* It is not a story, and a fluent speaker does not expect it to start a
conversation.

Under pressure, in a second language, *the tyre went flat* is easier than *I pushed it down the
flyover*, because the thing needs fewer words than your action. That is why the RED version comes
out by itself, and why you practise the GREEN one on purpose.

A native listener is kind about the problem and bored by the list. The first *so I …* is the moment
they start listening properly.
