## One-line answer

*Was* or *were* plus a verb with *-ing* — *I was cooking* — says that something was already going
on at a moment in the past, and when you join it to an ordinary past verb with *when*, the reader
sees one action in progress and another one breaking into it.

## The story

Water came through your kitchen ceiling last night. The landlord wants it in writing.

You write:

> I cooked dinner. Water came through the ceiling.

The landlord reads it and replies:

> So you finished dinner and then the water came? Or did the cooking cause it?

Your two sentences put the events one after the other, like steps. That is not what happened.

You write again:

> I was cooking dinner when water came through the ceiling.

Now the landlord sees it: you were in the middle of cooking, and the water arrived during it. The
cooking had no part in it. One small change to the verb, and the reader stops guessing about cause.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **verb** — a doing word: *cook*, *go*, *eat*.
- **-ing form** — a verb with *-ing* on the end: *cooking*, *going*. You met it with *am*, *is*
  and *are* on
  [day 26](../../../day-26-making-plans/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are-plus-ing.md).
- **past of *be*** — *was* and *were*, from
  [day 46](../../../day-46-describing-a-trip/parts/02-writing/2.1-was-were-and-there-was.md).
- **in progress** — started and not yet finished.
- **past continuous** — *was* or *were* plus an *-ing* form. The name for today's pattern.
- **past simple** — the ordinary past: *cooked*, *came*, *went*.

On day 26 you wrote *I am cooking* for something happening right now. Today you move the same
shape into the past. Change *am*, *is*, *are* to *was* and *were*, and the sentence is about
something that was in progress **then**.

The past simple says a thing happened, whole, like a dot. The past continuous says a thing was going
on, like a line. Put them together and the reader sees a dot landing on a line:

```text
   was cooking   ─────────────────────────────►
                              ●
                  water came through the ceiling
```

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past, and every story needs a background — what people
were doing when the thing happened. Without the past continuous, a story is a list of dots, one
after another, and the reader cannot tell what was already going on.

It also matters in the practical writing phase 5 asks for: a complaint, an incident report, an
insurance claim. *I was driving slowly when the other car hit me* says something very different
from *I drove slowly. The other car hit me.*

## The rule

**Two pages were checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**

[Past continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-continuous)
says "The past continuous is made from **the past tense of the verb** *be* and the *–ing* **form**
of a verb", and gives:

> The children **were doing** their homework when I **got** home.

[Past continuous and past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/past-continuous-past-simple)
shows how the choice changes the meaning:

> When the guests arrived, Jane **was cooking** dinner.
> When the guests arrived, Jane **cooked** dinner.

In the first, she had started before they came. In the second, they came first and then she cooked.

**The pattern:**

| Subject | was / were | verb + -ing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I, he, she, it | was | cooking, sleeping, waiting |
| you, we, they | were | cooking, sleeping, waiting |

**The spelling of *-ing*** follows day 26: *make* → *making* (drop the *e*), *sit* → *sitting*
(double the last letter after one short vowel), *cook* → *cooking*.

**Negative and question:**

> I wasn't sleeping. They weren't listening.
> Were you driving? What was she doing?

**The two jobs it does on paper:**

1. **The background, with an interruption.** The longer action takes *was/were + -ing*. The short
   one takes the past simple.

   > I was cooking dinner when water came through the ceiling.

2. **A moment in the past, and what was going on around it.**

   > At nine o'clock we were all watching the news.

**Verbs that do not take *-ing*.** The first page says: "We do **not** normally use the past
continuous with **stative verbs**. We use the **past simple** instead", with the example:

> When I got home, I really **needed** (NOT was needing) a shower.

A **stative verb** is about a state, not an action: *need*, *know*, *want*, *like*, *believe*,
*own*. You cannot see somebody *knowing* something the way you can see them cooking.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **both halves in the past simple**, which turns the line into steps:

> I cooked dinner when water came through the ceiling.

The reader may think the water came *because* you cooked, or *after* you finished.

The second is **both halves in the past continuous**:

> I was cooking when water was coming through the ceiling.

Now there is no dot. Two lines run side by side and nothing happens. The interruption loses its
bump.

The third is **the missing *was***:

> I cooking dinner when the water came.

It reads like a note, and the reader has to supply the time.

The fourth is **a state verb in *-ing***:

> I was knowing the pipe was old.

It should be *I knew*.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Written accounts of accidents and incidents lean heavily on this pattern, because the person
reading them — a landlord, an office, an insurer — is trying to work out what was happening and
what caused what. *I was standing at the bus stop when the scooter hit the pole* puts you outside
the event. *I stood at the bus stop. The scooter hit the pole* leaves room to wonder.

Under pressure writers use only the past simple, because it is shorter. Every event becomes a step,
and the reader reads it as a sequence. In a complaint, that can accidentally make you sound
responsible.

A formal reader notices the missing *was* at once and reads the whole message as rushed. The wrong
choice between the two tenses they may not notice as grammar at all — they simply understand a
different order of events.
