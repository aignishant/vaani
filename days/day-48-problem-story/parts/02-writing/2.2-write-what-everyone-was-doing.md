## One-line answer

Write one real moment from your own life as a still picture — what each person in the place was
doing, in the past continuous — and then let one thing happen in the past simple, so the reader
sees the whole room at the instant it changed.

## The story

The landlord asks one more question about the leak:

> Was anyone else home?

You write back:

> My mother was sleeping in the front room. My son was doing his homework at the table. I was
> cooking dinner. The fan was making its usual noise. Then water came through the ceiling above the
> stove.

The landlord replies in one line: *Okay, I understand, I'm sending a plumber.* He can see the flat
at that moment. Four things were going on, and one thing happened. Nobody has to ask what order
anything was in.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **past continuous** — *was* or *were* plus a verb with *-ing*, for something in progress in the
  past, from [part 2.1](2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **past simple** — the ordinary past: *came*, *stopped*, *rang*.
- **still picture** — a description of one moment, as if the world had stopped.
- **the event** — the one thing that happened and changed the moment.

This part uses the past continuous for a whole scene, not just one sentence. The shape is:

| Part of the paragraph | Tense | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the time | — | fixes the moment: *last Tuesday, just after dark* |
| the picture | past continuous, several sentences | who was doing what |
| the event | past simple, one sentence | what happened |
| what came next | past simple | one or two sentences |

The picture sentences are all lines. The event is the dot that lands on all of them at once.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story. The strongest place to start a story is often exactly this:
a still picture, and then the thing that broke it. A reader who can see the room before the event
cares about the event.

It is also the most useful paragraph in any written account of something that went wrong at home,
at work or on the road, where the reader needs to know what everybody was doing.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Past continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-continuous)
gives the pattern and uses such as:

> My head **was aching**. Everyone **was shouting**.

[Past continuous and past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/past-continuous-past-simple)
gives the interruption:

> We **were watching** television when the power **went** off.

**Step one — choose a real moment.** One instant when something happened and other people or things
were around: a power cut, a phone call with bad news, a knock at the door, a sudden storm, a child
falling, a visitor arriving early.

**Step two — the time line.** One short phrase that fixes when:

> Last Sunday, just after lunch,

**Step three — the picture, three to five sentences, past continuous.** Each about a different
person, animal or thing. Include at least one thing that is not a person — the rain, the television,
a dog:

> My father was reading the paper on the steps.
> The pressure cooker was hissing.

**Step four — the event, one sentence, past simple:**

> Then the lights went out.

or joined with *when*:

> We were all eating when the lights went out.

**Step five — what came next, one or two sentences, past simple.** Stop after that. This paragraph
is the moment, not the whole story.

**Step six — the tense check.** Underline every verb. Write **L** (line) above each *was/were +
-ing* and **D** (dot) above each past simple. The picture should be all L, the event should be D, and
what came next should be D.

**Step seven — the reader's test.** Give it to someone and ask:

> What was each person doing when it happened?

If they can answer for every person, the picture works.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the picture written in the past simple**:

> My father read the paper. The cooker hissed. My sister watched TV. The lights went out.

Now it reads as four things that happened one after another. The reader sees your father finish the
paper *before* the lights went out.

The second is **the event also in the past continuous**:

> The lights were going out.

The dot turns into a line, as if the lights went out slowly for a while. It should land.

The third is **a picture that goes on too long**. Eight sentences of what everybody was doing, and
the reader forgets to expect an event. Three to five is enough.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Written stories, news reports and witness statements often open like this: a still picture in the
past continuous, then the event. It is one of the most common shapes in written English, because it
lets a reader stand in the place before anything changes.

Under pressure, writers drop the picture and give only the event: *the lights went out*. The reader
knows what happened and has no idea what it interrupted. One sentence of picture is always better
than none.

A formal reader, such as a landlord or an office, uses the picture sentences to decide responsibility.
*I was cooking when water came through the ceiling* tells them you were not doing anything to the
pipe.
