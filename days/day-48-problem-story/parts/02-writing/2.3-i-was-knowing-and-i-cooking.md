## One-line answer

Take your real paragraph and break its tenses on purpose — the picture in the past simple, the
event in the past continuous, a missing *was* and a state verb with *-ing* — then give it to a
reader and ask what happened first, because the tense errors that look worst are not the ones that
change the order of events.

## The story

The landlord forwards your message about the leak to the building owner, who reads a different
version, typed in a hurry:

> I cooking dinner. My son did his homework. Water was coming through the ceiling. I was knowing
> the pipe was old.

The owner replies:

> Your son did his homework and *then* the water came? And you knew the pipe was old and said
> nothing?

Two of the four errors — *I cooking* and *I was knowing* — look odd, and the owner understood them
anyway. The other two looked like normal English and changed the story: the homework became a step
before the leak, and *was coming* made the leak sound like it went on quietly for a while.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **plant** — put a mistake somewhere on purpose to see if it is found.
- **past continuous** and **past simple** — the line and the dot, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **stative verb** — a verb about a state, not an action: *know*, *need*, *want*, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Four tense errors, in two groups:

| Error | Example | Looks wrong? | Changes the order of events? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| missing *was* | *I cooking dinner.* | yes | no |
| state verb with *-ing* | *I was knowing the pipe was old.* | yes | no |
| picture in the past simple | *My son did his homework.* | no | **yes** — it becomes a step before |
| event in the past continuous | *Water was coming through the ceiling.* | no | **yes** — the moment disappears |

The first two a reader forgives. The last two a reader believes.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past, and it will be read by somebody who does not know
what happened. The tense errors that change the order of events are the ones that make your story
say something you did not mean, and you will not find them by looking for what *looks* wrong.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Past continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/past-continuous):
"We do **not** normally use the past continuous with **stative verbs**", with:

> When I got home, I really **needed** (NOT was needing) a shower.

[Past continuous and past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/past-continuous-past-simple):

> When the guests arrived, Jane **was cooking** dinner.
> When the guests arrived, Jane **cooked** dinner.

**Step one — make the RED copy.** Take one real paragraph from

one each. Keep a hidden list.

**Step two — the order test.** Give the RED copy to a reader who has not seen the clean one. Ask:

> What happened first? What was already going on when the main thing happened?

Write down their answer word for word.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **planting only the loud errors**. A copy with *I
cooking* and *I was knowing* in it teaches you nothing new, because you would have caught them
yourself. The quiet two are the reason this part exists.

The second is **asking the reader to find errors first**. Once they are hunting, they stop reading
for meaning, and you lose the order test.

The third, in real writing, is **the whole story in the past continuous**, because it feels more
vivid:

> We were going to the market and we were buying vegetables and it was starting to rain.

Every verb is a line and nothing ever happens.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Native readers read *I was knowing* as a learner's error and move on. Some regional varieties of
English use state verbs with *-ing* freely, and in a message nobody minds. In a formal statement it
reads as careless.

What matters in real life is the order. A witness statement, an accident report or a complaint
where the picture is in the past simple gives the reader a sequence that never happened, and the
reader will act on it.

Under pressure writers reach for whichever tense they learned first. Marking lines and dots before
sending — a few seconds — catches the errors that the eye alone never will.
