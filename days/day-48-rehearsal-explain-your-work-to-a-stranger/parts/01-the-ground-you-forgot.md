# The ground you forgot you were standing on

`CF-11` · Day 48

## The one-line answer

A colleague shares your words, your assumptions, and a face you've learned to read — a stranger
hands you none of the three, and tonight's recording is what you sound like standing on ground you
didn't know was there.

## The model

You've explained your work hundreds of times. Almost always to people standing on the same patch
of ground as you: the same words underfoot, the same set of things nobody needs to say out loud.
It's solid, you've never had to look at it, and you can move across it fast.

A stranger is standing somewhere else entirely. Three things go missing at once, and they go
missing in order of how hard they are to notice.

**The shared vocabulary goes first, and it's the easy one.** There are forty or fifty words you
use every day that mean nothing outside your building — or worse, mean something *else* outside
it. Day 47 gave you the fix: glue the gloss to the word in the same breath, first time only. Today
you find out how many words there actually are, which is always more than you'd guess, because
half of them don't sound technical to you at all.

The rule is blunt and you can hear whether you've kept it: **any word you'd only use at work gets
a gloss the first time it comes out.** One flag at the top buys you some room to be imperfect:

> I'll try and keep this out of the jargon.

And when you know a term is coming and it can't be avoided, walk into it rather than hoping:

> This'll mean nothing to you yet, but —

**The shared assumptions go second, and they're much harder,** because vocabulary is at least
audible and an assumption is invisible from the inside. You don't say *we do this because
{{reason}}*. You say *so obviously we {{do the thing}}* — and the word **obviously** is the tell.
Every time it comes out of your mouth, something underneath it has gone unsaid.

A stranger needs the why before the what. Not after. If you tell them what happens first and the
reason second, they spend the whole of the what wondering why, and they aren't listening.

> The reason anyone does that is {{the why}}.
> You'd have no reason to know this, but {{the assumption}}.

That second one matters more than it looks. There's a way of supplying missing background that
sounds like you think the person is slow, and a way that sounds like you're handing them something
— and it's entirely in whether you attribute the gap to them or to the situation. *You'd have no
reason to know this* puts it on the situation. Nobody has ever taken offence at it.

**And the third thing is the one nobody expects: you can't read the face.** Not because it isn't
there — it's right in front of you — but because you've never seen it before.

You know exactly what a colleague looks like when they've lost you. You've watched that face for
years and you read it without deciding to. A stranger's polite face and a stranger's following
face are the same face. Both of them nod. Both of them say *mm*. Neither of them means anything
you can use.

So a nod is not evidence. And *does that make sense?* is not a question — it's a request for
reassurance, and everybody on earth answers it yes. Ask something that can't be answered with a
nod:

> Which bit of that would you want more of?
> Am I making it sound more complicated than it is?

Those come back with information. The first one gets you a specific place to go next. The second
one is the only version of *are you following* that people answer honestly, because it offers them
an out that isn't an admission.

And hand them the brake, out loud, once:

> Stop me if I'm going too deep.

Then when you catch yourself already too deep — which you will, mid-sentence, with the thing
half-built — say so and back up without ceremony:

> Right — you've probably got no context for that.

**What today spends.** Day 47's three rungs, whole. You're not learning them again; you're finding
out which ones you skip when there's a real person in front of you being polite. That's the entire
difference between yesterday and tonight, and it's a large one.

**Today's phrases** — the ones for a listener who shares nothing with you and won't tell you so.
All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I'll try and keep this out of the jargon. | Flagging, once, at the top, that you know you've got some |

## Say it now

All eight on your own work, out loud, on real material.

1. *I'll try and keep this out of the jargon. It's a way of {{doing what}}, for {{who}}.*
2. *This'll mean nothing to you yet, but — {{the term}}.*
3. *You'd have no reason to know this, but {{the assumption}}.*
4. *The reason anyone does that is {{the why}}.*
5. *Which bit of that would you want more of?*
6. *Am I making it sound more complicated than it is?*
7. *Stop me if I'm going too deep.*
8. *Right — you've probably got no context for that. {{Back up one step}}.*

Now run lines 1, 3 and 4 as one continuous stretch, twice — flag, assumption, why — and then stop
dead on line 5 and say nothing after it. The stopping is the rep. A question about whether they've
followed, with your next sentence already tumbling out behind it, isn't a question and won't get
answered.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d48-free.m4a`.**

> Take one sentence you'd say to a colleague without thinking twice — a real one, the kind you say
> weekly. Say it out loud as-is. Then ask yourself **why** three times over, out loud, answering
> each one before you ask the next. Do this on three different sentences.

- **Solo form:** as written, and say your answers out loud rather than thinking them. The point is

- **With a partner:** they ask the whys, and they ask them in the flattest voice they can manage,
  with no help and no encouraging noises. Three sentences, three whys each.

## The deliberate failure

That's the rep, and it's built to break. Somewhere in the second or third *why* you'll run out of
ground.

**What breaking sounds like:** a pause. Then something like *because — well, that's just how it's
done*, said slightly faster than the sentences either side of it, sometimes with a small laugh
attached. That laugh is the sound of somebody standing on something they can't see.

It isn't a gap in your knowledge, which is why it's uncomfortable. You know your work. What you've
hit is a place where the reason has been assumed for so long that it stopped being stored as a
reason. With a colleague it never surfaces, because they're standing on the same patch and neither
of you needs it. A stranger stands on nothing, and every one of those spots is a place tonight's
recording can fall through.

Write down each one. They're not errors, but they go in `docs/ERRORS.md` anyway, as the actual
sentence that ran out — because tonight, one of them is going to get asked about.

## When it breaks in the wild

You're at a table with people you've just met and somebody asks what you do. You give the good
version — sentence, comparison, one example. They nod, and it's a real nod, and you relax. Then
they ask a follow-up question, and the question reveals they've mapped the whole thing onto
something completely different that happens to share a word with it. They've been following
attentively. They've just been following the wrong thing, and the nod told you nothing.

**The repair:**

> Ah — no, that's a fair guess, but it's closer to {{the ordinary thing}}.

*That's a fair guess* is doing the work. Their reading was reasonable given what you gave them,
and saying so out loud costs nothing and keeps them in the conversation. What loses people is
correcting the misunderstanding as though it were an odd thing to have thought.
