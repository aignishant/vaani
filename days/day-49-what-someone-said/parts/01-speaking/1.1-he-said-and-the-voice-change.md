## One-line answer

To pass on what somebody said to you, name who spoke — *he said*, *she asked*, *he told me* — then
give their words as they said them, with a small pause before and a slight change in your voice, so
the listener can hear where your words stop and theirs begin.

## The story

You come home from the bank. Your father wants to know what happened with the form.

> I went to the counter, he checked the form, the address is wrong, come back Monday with the
> electricity bill, I said okay.

Your father looks confused.

> Who said the address is wrong? You or him? Who has to come back?

Every word you said was true. But the man's words and your words ran together, and nothing told
your father where one stopped and the other started.

You try again:

> The man at the counter looked at the form and said, "The address is wrong." Then he told me,
> "Come back on Monday with your electricity bill." And I said, "Okay, I'll come."

Now your father knows exactly who said what, and who has to do what.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **quote** — to say somebody's words again, the way they said them.
- **reporting verb** — the verb that tells the listener somebody spoke: *said*, *asked*, *told*.
- **the three parts** — where we are, what went wrong, how it ended, from
  [day 43](../../../day-43-story-with-three-parts/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-three-parts-a-listener-waits-for.md).
- **tune** — how your voice goes up and down.
- **pause** — a short stop in your speech.

When you pass on a conversation, the listener has one big question the whole time: *who is talking
now?* You answer it in two ways at once.

| What you do | What it tells the listener |
| --- | --- |
| say a reporting verb first: *he said*, *she asked* | who is about to speak |
| a tiny pause, then their words in a slightly different voice | where their words start and stop |

You do not need to act. A small change is enough: a little higher or lower, a little slower, the
tune of a question if it was a question. Your listener hears the edges of the quote.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story from your life. Almost every good story has somebody saying
something in it. A story where you cannot hear who spoke is a story the listener gives up on.

In daily life you pass on conversations all the time: what the doctor said, what the landlord said,
what the teacher said about your child. Getting *who said what* wrong can send somebody to the wrong
office on the wrong day.

## The rule

**Two pages were checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**

[Reported speech](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/reported-speech)
shows a person's words quoted directly, then the reporting verbs that pass them on, including:

> Peter **told** her not to worry.
> She **asked** if they could stop for a rest.

[tell](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tell) and [say](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/say) on the
free dictionary give *tell* as "To convey by speech; to say", with "Please tell me how to do it" and
"Tell him to go away", and *say* as "To tell, either verbally or in writing", with "He said he would
be here tomorrow."

**The three reporting verbs you need most:**

| Verb | What comes after it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *said* | their words straight away | *He said, "The address is wrong."* |
| *told me*, *told her* | a person, then their words | *He told me, "Come back on Monday."* |
| *asked* | their question, in a question tune | *She asked, "Do you have the bill?"* |

Look at the examples on the two dictionary pages: *tell me*, *told him*, *tell him* — every time
*tell* has a person straight after it. *Said* in the page's example has none: *he said he would be
here*. Keep that pattern: **told** + a person, **said** + the words.

**The sound of a quote:**

```text
   And he said  |  "Come back on MONday."   |  So I said  |  "Okay."
   (your voice)  ↑  (a little different)    ↑  (yours)    ↑  (a little different)
               pause                      pause         pause
```

The pause is short. The change in voice is small. Together they draw the quotation marks in the
air.

**Keep a question a question.** If the person asked, say their words with their tune:

> She asked, "Do you have the bill?" — the voice goes up at the end, as she said it.

**Keep the person's own words, including *I* and *you*.** Inside the quote, *I* means the person
speaking, not you:

> He said, "I can't do it today." — *I* is the bank man.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **words with no reporting verb**:

> The address is wrong, come back Monday, I said okay.

The listener cannot tell which sentence was the bank man and which was you.

The second is **mixing their words and yours halfway through**:

> He said the address is wrong so you come back Monday.

Who is *you*? The bank man said *you* to you. When you repeat it to your father, *you* now sounds
like your father.

The third is ***said me***:

> He said me come back on Monday.

The listener understands, and it marks the English as a beginner's. *He told me* or *He said to me*.

The fourth is **the quote in your own flat voice**, with no pause, so it melts into your next
sentence.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers pass on conversations with a lot of quoting, and often with very casual reporting
verbs you will hear everywhere: *he goes, "No way"*, *she was like, "Seriously?"*. Understand these
when you hear them; *said* and *asked* are always safe for you to use.

Under pressure, when the conversation you are passing on was upsetting, the quotes run together
and the voice goes flat. That is exactly when your listener most needs to know who said what. Slow
down at every *he said*.

What a native listener notices is the edges. A quote with a clear start and end is easy to follow
even with small grammar errors. A quote with no edges is hard to follow even when every word is
right.
