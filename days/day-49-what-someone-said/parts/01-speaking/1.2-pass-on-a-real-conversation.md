## One-line answer

Pass on a real conversation of your own to somebody who was not there, then ask them to tell you
back who said what — because the only proof that your quotes had clear edges is a listener who can
return them to the right person.

## The story

A week later, your father goes to the bank himself. When he comes back, he tells you about it:

> The lady said, "Your form is fine now." I asked her, "When will the card come?" She said, "Ten
> days." And then the manager came and told me, "Sir, next time bring your passbook also."

You say it back to him, to check:

> So the lady said the form's fine, you asked about the card, she said ten days, and the manager
> said bring the passbook.

He nods. Four quotes, three people, and you got every one back in the right mouth. That is what a
well-passed conversation sounds like from the other side.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **quote** — somebody's words said again as they said them, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-he-said-and-the-voice-change.md).
- **reporting verb** — *said*, *told*, *asked*, from the same part.
- **say it back** — the listener repeats what they understood, so you can check it.
- **turn** — one person's piece of talk before the other person speaks.

This part is practice on real conversations. You will pass on three of them, each to somebody who
was not there, and then ask for a say-back.

The say-back is the check. If the listener gives a line to the wrong person, you know exactly where
your edges were unclear.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story of at least two hundred spoken words. A story with two or three
people speaking in it reaches that length and holds a listener, but only if the listener can follow
who said what the whole way through.

And in daily life, passing on a conversation correctly is sometimes the whole job: the doctor's
instructions, the office's list of documents, a teacher's message about your child.

**Step two — the frame.** Before the first quote, one sentence that sets the scene: where, who.

> I was at the chemist, and the man behind the counter was new.

**Step three — the turns.** A reporting verb before each quote, a small pause, a slight voice
change. Vary the verbs so the listener hears who is speaking:

> He asked, "Do you have the doctor's paper?" I said, "No, I forgot it." He told me, "Then I can
> only give you half."

**Step four — the say-back.** After each conversation, ask your listener:

> Can you tell me who said what?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **forgetting to name the speaker after the first two turns**:

> He asked, "Do you have the paper?" I said, "No." "Then half." "Okay, give half." "That's fifty."

By the third line, nobody knows who is talking.

The second is **using *he* for two different men**. If the chemist and your brother are both in the
story, *he said* is not enough. Use *the chemist said* for one of them.

The third is **passing on only your own lines** — *I said this, then I said that* — so the other
person's words, which were the reason for the story, disappear.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often drop the reporting verb in a fast back-and-forth, but only once the two voices
are clearly different. They keep the voices apart with tune and pace instead. You can do the same
once your pauses are reliable; until then, name every speaker.

Under pressure — passing on bad news, or instructions you did not fully understand — speakers
summarise instead of quoting, and the exact words that mattered get lost. When the words matter,
quote them.

A native listener asks *who said that?* without any embarrassment. If you hear that question, your
edges slipped, and the fix is a name, not a longer explanation.
