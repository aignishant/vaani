## One-line answer

Pass on one real conversation on purpose with no reporting verbs, no pauses and one voice, then
ask a listener to hand each line back to its speaker — because the lines they give to the wrong
person show you exactly what the edges of a quote were doing.

## The story

Your neighbour asks what happened at the parent-teacher meeting.

You tell it fast, in one voice:

> The teacher is saying he's not doing homework, I said he does it every night, she said then
> he's not bringing it, I said I will check his bag, she said good, next week test.

Your neighbour says:

> Wait — who said he does it every night? And who's checking the bag?

Six turns between two people. Every word is right. With no *she said* and no change in your voice,
it came out as one person talking to themselves, and your neighbour had to guess who owned each
line. She guessed two of them wrong.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not
one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **quote** — somebody's words said again as they said them, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-he-said-and-the-voice-change.md).
- **the edges** — where a quote starts and stops: the reporting verb, the pause, the voice change.
- **misattribute** — give words to the wrong person.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will pass on the same real conversation twice.

- **The RED take.** No reporting verbs after the first one. No pauses. One flat voice. Their words
  and yours run together.
- **The GREEN take.** A reporting verb before every quote, a small pause, a slight voice change, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-he-said-and-the-voice-change.md).

Then a listener tries to give every line back to the right person. You count the mistakes.

## Why Vaani needs it

A phase 3 gate story with a conversation in it depends entirely on this. The listener cannot stop
the recording to ask *who said that?*, so a misattributed line in a recording is lost for good.

In daily life, a misattributed line can do real harm: *the doctor said stop the tablets* when it
was your aunt who said it.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Reported speech](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/reported-speech),
which passes on each person's words with a verb that names who spoke — *told*, *asked*,
*complained*, *suggested*.

**Step one — choose one real conversation** with two people and at least six turns.

**Step two — write the turns as a list for yourself only**, one line each, with who said it:

| 4 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| 5 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| 6 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**Step three — record both takes.** RED first, then GREEN.

**Step four — the hand-back test.** Play the RED take to one listener and the GREEN take to a
different one. Give each a copy of your turn list with the *who* column hidden, and ask them to fill
it in.

**Step five — count.**

| Take | Lines given to the right person | Lines given to the wrong person | What they asked you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RED | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| GREEN | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the RED take's listener gives at least
one line to the wrong person. It is **GREEN** when the GREEN take's listener gives every line to the
right person.

If the GREEN take also lost a line, find the turn. It is almost always one where two lines in a row
belong to the same person, or where *he* could mean two people. Add a name there and play it to a
third listener.

**Step six — the edges drill.** Say four real lines from your conversations this week, each three
ways: with no edges, with only the reporting verb, and with the verb, the pause and the voice change.
Listen to which way you can hear the edges.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take that is secretly clear**, because your
voice still changes a little. Make it truly flat, as if reading a list.

The second is **testing on somebody who was there**. They know who said what already.

The third, in real speech, is **the *is saying* habit** from the story: *the teacher is saying he's
not doing homework*. The listener cannot tell whether she is still saying it, said it once, or said
it about someone else. *She said, "He's not doing his homework"* fixes all three.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A native listener will interrupt a flat retelling with *who said that?* — and in a busy conversation
they may not. They will simply walk away with the wrong version.

Under pressure, when the conversation was tense, the edges vanish first, because you are thinking
about what it meant rather than who said it. That is exactly the conversation where who said it
matters most.

The fix a fluent speaker uses is not a longer explanation. It is one short *and she said* at the
place the listener might get lost.
