## One-line answer

Put the exact words somebody spoke inside speech marks — *'Where to?' asked the conductor.* — with a
capital letter at the start of the words, the comma or question mark *inside* the closing mark, and
a new line every time a different person speaks, so a reader can see who said what without hearing
a single voice.

## The story

You are writing down a small moment from a bus journey, for a story about your week.

> The conductor said where to I said the market he said the market is closed today I said since when
> he said since the fire.

Your friend reads it and gets lost at *the market*. Is that you saying it or him? Is *since when*
a question? There is nothing on the page to show where anybody's words start or stop.

You write it again:

> 'Where to?' asked the conductor.
>
> 'The market,' I said.
>
> 'The market's closed today.'
>
> 'Since when?'
>
> 'Since the fire.'

Now your friend reads it in one go and says, *what fire?* The words are the same. The marks, the
capitals and the new lines are doing the work your voice would do.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **speech marks** — the small marks around spoken words: *'like this'*. Also called *quotation
  marks* or *inverted commas*.
- **direct speech** — the exact words somebody said, written inside speech marks.
- **reporting verb** — the verb that says somebody spoke: *said*, *asked*.
- **sentence** — words that start with a capital letter and end with a full stop, from
  [day 3](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md).
- **paragraph** — a block of writing that starts on a new line.

Spoken words on a page need three signals, because the reader cannot hear a change of voice.

| Signal | What it tells the reader |
| --- | --- |
| speech marks | these are somebody's exact words |
| the reporting verb | who said them: *I said*, *asked the conductor* |
| a new line | a different person is speaking now |

Once the first two speakers are clear, you can often drop the reporting verb and let the new lines
alone show who is speaking, as in the story.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past "with speech marks used once". That is this
day's rule, and the gate checks it. One line of speech, marked correctly, is the thing that turns an
account into a story a reader can hear.

It is also how you write down any exact words that matter later: what a doctor told you, what an

## The rule

**Three pages were checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**

[Reported speech: statements](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/reported-speech-statements)
gives direct speech in single speech marks, with the reporting verb after the words:

> 'I work in a bank,' said Daniel.
> 'We want our jobs back!' we said.

[Quotation marks in English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotation_marks_in_English) says they are
"also known informally as quotes, talking marks, speech marks", that "Single quotes are more usual in
the United Kingdom, Ireland and South Africa, though double quotes are also common there", and that
"Double quotes are preferred in the United States and Canada". It also describes the style most
common in the United Kingdom: "to include within quotation marks only those punctuation marks that
appeared in the original quoted material".

[Quotation Marks with Fiction, Poetry, and Titles](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/quotation_marks_with_fiction.html)
says: "Write each person's spoken words, however brief, as a separate paragraph."

**Choose single or double, and keep it.** This course uses single marks, as on the first page. Double
marks are not wrong. Switching halfway through a page is.

**The shape of one line of speech:**

```text
   'The market,'   I said.
   ↑           ↑↑  ↑      ↑
   capital     ,'  small  full stop
   first word  comma      letter
               inside     after the
               the mark   closing mark
```

Four things to see:

1. **Open the marks before the first spoken word**, and start that word with a capital letter.
2. **End the spoken words with a comma inside the closing mark** when the reporting verb comes after.
   The full stop belongs to the whole sentence, so it goes at the very end, after *said*.
3. **A question mark or an exclamation mark stays inside**, because it is part of the words, as in
   *'We want our jobs back!' we said.* Nothing else is added:

   > 'Since when?' I asked.

4. **The reporting verb after the words starts with a small letter**, because the sentence has not
   ended: *said Daniel*, *I said*, *asked the conductor*.

**Speech with no reporting verb** keeps its own ending inside the marks:

> 'Since the fire.'

**A new speaker, a new line.** Every time the person speaking changes, start a new line, even if they
say one word.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **no marks at all**, as in the story. The reader has to guess where
each person's words are.

The second is **the full stop inside, and then the reporting verb**:

> 'The market.' I said.

Now *I said* is a sentence on its own, and a strange one. Use the comma: *'The market,' I said.*

The third is **the comma outside the mark, and a capital on the verb**:

> 'The market', I said. · 'The market,' I Said.

The fourth is **two speakers on one line**:

> 'Where to?' asked the conductor. 'The market,' I said.

It can be read, but it is the first place a reader loses track of who is speaking.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Printed stories and newspapers follow these signals so exactly that readers never notice them. They
only notice when one is missing, and then they stop and re-read.

Under pressure — a quick message, an informal note — people drop the marks and write *he said the
market is closed*. That is fine in a message. It is not direct speech any more, and it loses the
exact words.

A formal reader, such as an office writing down a complaint, uses speech marks for exactly one reason:
to show these were the words, not a summary. *The officer said, in these words, 'Come back next
month'* is evidence. Your version of what he meant is not.
