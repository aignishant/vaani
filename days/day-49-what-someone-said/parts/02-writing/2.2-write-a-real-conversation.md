## One-line answer

Write a real conversation from your week as a short scene — one line setting the place, then the
turns in speech marks on new lines — and give it to somebody to read aloud in two voices, because if
they give a line to the wrong voice, your page did not show who was speaking.

## The story

The bus again, the next morning. The conductor remembers you.

You write it down that evening:

> The same conductor was on the morning bus, leaning on the door.
>
> 'Market again?' he asked.
>
> 'No, the hospital,' I said. 'Is the market still closed?'
>
> 'Two more weeks. They're rebuilding the whole back side.'
>
> 'Were many shops burned?'
>
> 'Eleven.' He tore my ticket. 'My cousin had one of them.'

You hand it to your sister and ask her to read it aloud, doing the conductor in a deep voice. She
does it without one mistake. Every line went to the right person, and she stopped at *my cousin had
one of them* and said, *oh*. The page carried the scene by itself.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **direct speech** — exact spoken words inside speech marks, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-the-marks-around-the-words.md).
- **scene** — one place at one time, with people doing and saying things.
- **action line** — a short sentence of what somebody *did* between the words: *He tore my ticket.*
- **turn** — one person's piece of talk before the other person speaks.

A written conversation has three kinds of line:

| Kind | Example | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the setting | *The same conductor was on the morning bus, leaning on the door.* | where we are, who is there |
| speech | *'Market again?' he asked.* | the words |
| action | *He tore my ticket.* | what somebody did, which also shows who is speaking |

The action line is a quiet tool. When it sits in the same paragraph as the words, it tells the reader
who is talking without a *he said*.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Reported speech: statements](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/reported-speech-statements),
for the shape of each line:

> 'I'll phone you tomorrow,' he said.

[Quotation Marks with Fiction, Poetry, and Titles](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/quotation_marks_with_fiction.html):
"Write each person's spoken words, however brief, as a separate paragraph."

**Step one — choose a real conversation** from the last week, two people, six to ten turns. Short
and ordinary is best: a shop, a bus, a neighbour, a phone call.

**Step two — the setting line.** One sentence in the past: where, who, one detail you could see.

**Step three — the turns.** Each turn on its own line. The first two turns carry a reporting verb so
the reader learns the two voices. After that, add a reporting verb only where the reader might lose
track.

**Step four — one or two action lines**, placed in the paragraph of the person who did the action:

> 'Eleven.' He tore my ticket. 'My cousin had one of them.'

**Step five — the punctuation pass.** Go through every line and check the four things from
[part 2.1](2.1-the-marks-around-the-words.md): the opening mark and capital, the comma or question
mark inside the closing mark, the small letter on *said* or *asked*, and the full stop at the end.

**Step six — the two-voice test.** Give the page to somebody and ask them to read it aloud, doing
one person's lines in a different voice. Do not help. Write down every line they gave to the wrong
voice.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **all the turns in one paragraph**:

> 'Market again?' he asked. 'No, the hospital,' I said. 'Is the market still closed?' 'Two more
> weeks.'

Correct marks, and the reader still has to count to know who is speaking.

The second is **a reporting verb on every line**, *he said*, *I said*, *he said*, *I said*. Nothing is
wrong, and it reads like a list. Use them for the first two turns and where needed.

The third is **the action line in the wrong paragraph**: *He tore my ticket* placed next to your words.
The reader then thinks you said the line that follows.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Printed conversations are mostly short lines, very few reporting verbs, and small action lines that
carry who is talking. That is why they read fast.

Under pressure writers put every turn in one paragraph to save space. It saves space and costs the
reader. A new line costs nothing.

A reader forgives an odd comma. They do not forgive losing track of who is speaking, because then the
conversation stops meaning anything.
