## One-line answer

Take your real conversation and write it again with no speech marks and no new lines, then with the
marks in but the punctuation wrong, and ask a reader who said each line — because a conversation
without its signals is one voice talking to itself, and the wrong comma turns a speaker into a
sentence.

## The story

A friend asks to see your bus scene. You send it from your phone, typed quickly:

> The conductor asked market again I said no the hospital is the market still closed two more weeks
> theyre rebuilding the whole back side were many shops burned eleven he tore my ticket my cousin
> had one of them.

Your friend replies:

> So you asked if the market is closed, and then you said two more weeks? And who had a cousin?

Everything is in there, in the right order. Your friend gave *two more weeks* to you and could not
say who had the cousin. The meaning was carried by your voice when you told it, and on the page
there was no voice.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **speech marks** — the marks around spoken words, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-the-marks-around-the-words.md).
- **signal** — a mark or a new line that tells the reader something your voice would have told them.
- **misattribute** — give words to the wrong person.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will write the same real conversation three ways:

| Copy | What is missing | What a reader loses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A — no signals | no marks, no new lines, few capitals | who is speaking, and where each line ends |
| B — marks, wrong punctuation | the full stop inside before *said*, commas outside, capitals on *Said* | little meaning, a lot of trust |
| C — correct | nothing | nothing |

Copy A shows the big loss. Copy B shows what a careful reader notices even when they understand you.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story "with speech marks used once", and it is checked. A gate page
with the speech run into the paragraph fails that line of the gate, however good the story is.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Reported speech: statements](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/reported-speech-statements):

> 'I work in a bank,' said Daniel.

[Quotation Marks with Fiction, Poetry, and Titles](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/quotation_marks_with_fiction.html):
"Write each person's spoken words, however brief, as a separate paragraph."

**Step one — write copy A** of your conversation from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-conversation.md): one paragraph, no speech marks, no reporting verbs after
the first, capitals only where a sentence plainly starts.

**Step two — write copy B**: speech marks and new lines in, and four punctuation errors planted, with a
hidden list:

> 'Market again?' He asked.
> 'No, the hospital.' I said.
> 'Is the market still closed'?
> 'Two more weeks', he said.

**Step three — the who-said-it test on copy A.** Give copy A to a reader who has not seen the scene.
Give them a list of the spoken lines and ask them to write *me* or *the other person* beside each.

**Step four — the error hunt on copy B.** Give copy B to a second reader. Ask: *is anything wrong with
the punctuation?* Write down what they find.

> 'Two more weeks', he said. → 'Two more weeks,' he said.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **copy A with secret help**: you still put a new line
at each speaker because the habit is strong. Keep it one paragraph.

The second is **a reader who knows the conversation**. They will read your voice into it.

The third, in real writing, is **the question mark outside the mark**: *'Is it closed'?* It looks
small, and it shows a reader that the question was yours, not the speaker's.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

In a text message nobody expects speech marks, and *he said the market is closed* is normal. The
moment you write a story, a statement or anything somebody will read carefully, the signals are
expected, and their absence reads as a draft.

Under pressure the full stop before *said* is the error that survives longest, because it looks like a
finished sentence. A reader who knows the convention sees it at once.

A native reader forgives a stray comma. What they cannot recover is who said what, and a story where
that is lost has lost its point.
