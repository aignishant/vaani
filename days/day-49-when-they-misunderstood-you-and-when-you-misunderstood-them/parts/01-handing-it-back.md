# Handing it back without making a thing of it

`IN-10` · Day 49

## The one-line answer

Two halves that look symmetrical and aren't: when they took the wrong thing from what you said,
and when you took the wrong thing from what they said — and the second one is much harder, because
you're the one who has to say so.

## The model

A conversation is a series of small handovers. You hold something, you pass it across, they take
hold of it. Most of the time what they take hold of is roughly what you passed. Sometimes it
isn't, and the whole rest of the conversation gets built on the wrong object.

Both halves of today are about the same moment: the moment you notice. What changes is who's
holding the wrong thing.

### Half one — they took something else

The tell arrives before the words do. Two shapes, and once you've heard them you'll never miss
them again:

**They answer a question you didn't ask.** Perfectly reasonable answer. Just not to your thing.

**They agree with something you didn't say.** This one is worse, because agreement feels like
success and you'll want to bank it and move on. Listen to *what* they agreed with. If it's a
slightly different claim from the one you made, they've taken the wrong object and now you both
think you're finished.

The instinct is to say the same sentence again. Day 44 already dealt with that: a second attempt
made out of the same words isn't a second attempt.

Today's addition is smaller and it's about who takes the blame for the handover.

> That's on me — I said that badly.

You don't say this because it's true. Half the time it isn't, and it doesn't matter. You say it
because it lets the other person put down the wrong thing without having been wrong, and a person
who's been told they misheard you will defend their reading for another two turns before they let
go of it. Four words buy you all of that. It's the cheapest thing in this whole document.

Then two more moves, depending on how far off they are.

If they're nearly right:

> Almost — the bit I'd change is {{the one part}}.

Take the ninety per cent. Fix the ten. Re-explaining the whole thing to correct one part tells
them nothing they got was any use.

If they're a long way off, ask for the floor back rather than just taking it:

> Can I have another go at that?

And prevention, which belongs in half one because it stops most of half two as well: before you
answer anything you're not certain of, say it back.

> Just so I've got it — {{their point, in your own words}}?

**In your own words.** Repeating their words proves nothing — you can echo a sentence you haven't
understood, and people do it constantly. Putting it in different words is a real test and it fails
loudly when it fails, which is the point.

### Half two — you took something else

Here's the asymmetry. Half one, you're generous and it costs you nothing. Half two, you're the one
who's been holding the wrong object, possibly for a while, possibly while talking confidently
about it — and now you have to set it down in front of everybody.

Everything hard about this day is in that sentence.

The tells: something they say stops fitting, halfway through your own reply. Or they say *no, I
meant —* and you realise the last thing you said was an answer to a question nobody asked.

The move is small, flat, and early:

> Hang on — I've been answering the wrong thing.
> Ah — so you were talking about {{the other thing}}.
> Right, that changes it.

Then carry straight on. That's the whole move.

**And here is the rule, which you can hear:** the correction is one clause long. Not two. You do
not explain how you came to misread it. You do not say why it was a reasonable thing to have
thought. You do not apologise twice.

The audible consequence is exact. An explanation of your own mistake makes the other person manage
your embarrassment for you, and while they're doing that the conversation has stopped. Everybody
in the room now has a small job that isn't the conversation. One clause and a carry-on leaves
nothing for anyone to do, which is precisely why nobody remembers it happening.

And when the thing you already said needs withdrawing:

> Ignore what I just said, then.

Said lightly, with no wince in it. It's a completely ordinary sentence and it sounds like
confidence, which is the opposite of what you'd expect from a sentence that admits a mistake.

**Today's phrases** — four for the handover they got wrong, four for the one you did. All of these
go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| That's on me — I said that badly. | Taking the wrong reading back without blaming them for it |
| Almost — the bit I'd change is … | Their version is nearly right and one part isn't |
| Can I have another go at that? | Asking for the floor back, instead of repeating the first attempt |
| Just so I've got it — {{their point}}? | Playing their point back in your own words before you answer |
| Hang on — I've been answering the wrong thing. | You are the one who misread it, and you've just noticed |
| Ah — so you were talking about {{the other thing}}. | Landing on what they actually meant, out loud and plainly |
| Right, that changes it. | Accepting the correction and moving, with no speech attached |
| Ignore what I just said, then. | Withdrawing an answer you gave to the wrong question |

## Say it now

Take one real thing you'd say about your week. The same thing for all eight.

1. *That's on me — I said that badly. What I meant was {{the thing}}.*
2. *Almost — the bit I'd change is {{the one part}}.*
3. *Can I have another go at that?*
4. *Just so I've got it — {{their point, in your own words}}?*
5. *Hang on — I've been answering the wrong thing.*
6. *Ah — so you were talking about {{the other thing}}.*
7. *Right, that changes it. {{Carry straight on}}.*
8. *Ignore what I just said, then.*

Now run lines 1 to 4 as one block, then lines 5 to 8 as one block, back to back. Listen to
yourself. The second block will be slower than the first, and it'll have a longer run-up into it —
a breath, a *sort of*, a small laugh. Run the two blocks again and again until they come out at
the same speed. That's the target, and it's a target you can hear: **the line where you were wrong
should arrive exactly as fast as the line where they were.**

## The deliberate failure

Take one of the times you misread something, and apologise for it properly. Explain how you came
to misread it. Say why it was a reasonable thing to think, given what you'd been told. Add that
you'd assumed, and that you should have checked. Give it the full treatment.

**What breaking sounds like:** it sounds *good*. It's polite, it's articulate, it's the most
considerate paragraph on the whole tape — and that's the uncomfortable finding, because it means
nobody has ever told you it was the problem. Nobody interrupts a graceful apology.

Listen to what it's actually made of. Every sentence in it is about you. The topic has stopped
moving. And if there were another person there, they'd now be saying *no, no, it's fine* — which
is work, done by them, on your behalf, in the middle of something else.

Then say the same correction as one clause and carry on. Play both back. The short one sounds more
confident, and the strange part is that the short one is the one that admits more.

## When it breaks in the wild

You're in a group and somebody asks a question. You answer it. Two other people build on your
answer, and the conversation moves on quite happily — and it's a couple of turns later that you
realise the question wasn't the one you answered. Your wrong answer is now load-bearing. Three
people are standing on it and none of them knows.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — I answered the wrong question back there. Can I have another go?

Say it as soon as you notice, not at a polite moment. There isn't going to be a politer moment
than this one, and every turn you wait adds another thing built on top. What people actually mind
is finding out at the end. Nobody has ever minded being told halfway through.

wrong is completely inaudible from the inside and completely obvious on the tape, and it's the
thing that makes a small correction sound like a confession.
