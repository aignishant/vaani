## One-line answer

When somebody asks about a film, a show or a book, answer in three moves — what it's about in one
or two sentences, what you thought and one reason why, and who would enjoy it — because what they
really want to know is *should I spend my evening on this?*

## The story

Lunch at work. A colleague has seen you watching a series on your phone on the bus.

> Is that any good?

The first answer:

> Yes, it's very nice, very good, you should watch, everybody is watching.

Your colleague nods. They have learned nothing they could not guess, and they will not watch it.

The second answer:

> It's about a family who run a small hotel in the hills, and everything goes wrong in the first
> week. I liked it more than I expected — the grandmother is really funny. If you like slow, gentle
> stuff, you'll love it. If you want action, don't bother.

Now your colleague knows what it is, what you thought, and whether it is for them. They say they
will try the first episode tonight.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **plot** — what happens in a film, a show or a book.
- **episode** — one part of a show that comes in parts.
- **spoiler** — telling somebody what happens at the end before they have seen it.
- **recommend** — say that somebody should try something.
- **the ladder of liking** — *I love it, I like it, it's all right, I don't like it, I can't stand
  it*, from [day 16](../../../day-16-likes-and-dislikes/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-ladder-of-liking.md).

Three moves, and each one answers a question the listener has.

| Move | The listener's question | How long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · what it's about | *What kind of thing is it?* | one or two sentences, no ending |
| 2 · what you thought, and why | *Did you like it, really?* | one sentence and one reason |
| 3 · who it's for | *Would I like it?* | one sentence |

Move three is the one people forget, and it is the one the listener most wants.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is one of the commonest conversations between people who are getting to know each other:
*seen anything good?* It comes up at work, on a journey, with neighbours. Having a real answer
means you can join it.

It also builds the skill the phase 3 gate needs: telling somebody about something that happened
in a clear order, with an opinion at the end. And in phase 4, giving an opinion with a reason is
the whole subject.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[A restaurant review](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/b1/restaurant-review).
It is about a meal, not a film, and its advice fits any recommendation: "Write about the important
parts of the experience, not every detail." and "Write about the good and the bad."

**Move one — what it's about, in the present.** A second page was opened on 2026-09-23 for this:
[Historical present](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_present), whose section *In
describing fiction* says "Summaries of the narratives (plots) of works of fiction are conventionally
presented using the present tense, rather than the past tense." So:

> It's about a family who run a small hotel.
> It's about a boy who finds a phone on a train.

Start with *It's about* and a person. One or two sentences. **Stop before the ending.**

**Move two — what you thought, and one reason.** Choose a rung from the ladder of liking, and give a
real reason — a person, a moment, a feeling:

> I liked it more than I expected. The grandmother is really funny.
> It was all right, but the middle is slow.
> I couldn't stand it. Everybody shouts all the time.

**Move three — who it's for.** This is where you help the listener decide:

> If you like slow, gentle stuff, you'll love it.
> It's good for watching with kids.
> Don't watch it if you're tired — you have to follow it closely.

**The honest *but*.** Most good answers have one good thing and one bad thing:

> The story's great, but it's too long.

**The words that tell a listener you are about to give the ending,** so they can stop you:

> I won't tell you the ending, but …
> Do you want to know what happens?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the answer with no information**:

> It's very nice, very good, you should watch.

Every film is *very nice* to somebody. The listener cannot decide anything.

The second is **the whole plot**:

> First he goes to the city, then he meets a girl, then her father doesn't like him, then …

This is the failure part [1.3](1.3-the-whole-plot-start-to-finish.md) builds on purpose.

The third is **giving away the ending** without asking.

The fourth is **no move three**. The listener knows what it is and that you liked it, and still has
to ask *would I like it?*

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers answer *is it any good?* in about three sentences, and almost always include who it
is for: *you'd like it*, *not really your thing*, *my kids loved it*. That line is what makes it a
recommendation rather than a report.

Under pressure, in a second language, *very nice* is safe and needs no vocabulary. It also closes
the conversation. One real reason, even a simple one — *the old man is funny* — opens it.

What a native listener notices is honesty. *It's all right, but the middle is slow* is more
trusted than *it's the best film ever*, and it makes them more likely to try it.
