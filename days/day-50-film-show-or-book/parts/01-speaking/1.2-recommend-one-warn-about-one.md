## One-line answer

Recommend one real film, show or book to one real person and warn another real person off one you
did not like, each chosen for that person — and afterwards ask whether they will watch it, because
a recommendation that changes nothing has not done its job.

## The story

The same lunch, the next week. Your colleague did watch the first episode, and loved it. Now they
ask:

> What else? Something like that?

And someone else at the table says:

> Don't tell me to watch the one about the hospital. Everyone says it's good.

You have two jobs in one conversation: find the right thing for the first person, and be honest with
the second. For the first you say:

> Try the one about the tailor's shop. Same kind of slow and funny, and it's short.

For the second:

> Honestly? I stopped after two episodes. It's very loud. If you didn't like the trailer, you won't
> like the show.

Both people get something they can act on.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **recommend** — say that somebody should try something, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-its-about-and-what-you-thought.md).
- **warn** — tell somebody about a problem before they meet it.
- **the three moves** — what it's about, what you thought, who it's for, from the same part.
- **honest** — saying what you really think, politely.

A recommendation is not about the film. It is about **the person in front of you**. The same film is
a good recommendation for one person and a bad one for another.

| Job | What changes in the three moves |
| --- | --- |
| recommend | move three is about *them*: *you'd like it because you liked …* |
| warn | move two is honest and has a reason; move three says who *might* still like it |

A good warning is fair. It gives the reason, so the listener can decide the reason does not matter
to them.

## Why Vaani needs it

Recommending and warning are small daily opinions with reasons, which is exactly what phase 4 is
built on. Doing them with real people now makes phase 4's formal version much easier.

And they keep conversations going. A recommendation almost always gets a question back: *how long
is it?*, *is it sad?*, *where can I watch it?* Each one is unprepared speaking practice about
something you know well.

> Honestly? · To be fair, … · It's just not for me. · Maybe it gets better, but I stopped.

> Do you think you'll try it?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **recommending your own favourite to everybody**, whatever they like.
The listener hears enthusiasm and no reason to believe it applies to them.

The second is **the warning with no reason**:

> It's bad. Don't watch.

It sounds like a personal opinion with nothing behind it, and many people will watch it anyway.

The third is **a warning that is rude about the people who like it**: *only stupid people watch
that*. The person you are talking to may be one of them.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers almost always tie a recommendation to the listener: *you'd like this, it's got that
same kind of humour*. That link is what makes the listener trust it.

Under pressure — a group conversation, several people talking — the reason drops out first. Keep one
short reason, even if the rest is short too.

A native listener responds well to honest warnings with a *to be fair* in them. It shows you are
being fair to the thing, and it makes your good recommendations more believable.
