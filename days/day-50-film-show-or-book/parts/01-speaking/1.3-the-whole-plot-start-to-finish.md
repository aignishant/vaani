## One-line answer

Answer *is it any good?* on purpose by telling the whole story from the first scene to the last, and
then ask the listener whether they now want to watch it and what you thought of it — because a
retold plot answers every question except the one they asked.

## The story

A neighbour at the gate asks about the film you saw at the weekend.

> First there is a village, and a boy, his father is a farmer. Then the rain doesn't come, so the
> father goes to the city. In the city he works in a factory. Then the boy also goes to find him.
> On the train he meets a girl. Then …

It goes on. Your neighbour listens politely, nodding at each *then*. At the end, they say:

> So the father comes back? Oh. Okay. And — was it good?

After all those sentences, they still did not know your opinion. They also no longer need to watch
it, because you have told them the ending.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not
one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **plot** — what happens in the story, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-its-about-and-what-you-thought.md).
- **spoiler** — telling the ending before somebody has seen it, from the same part.
- **retell** — tell a story again, event by event.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will answer the same question about the same real film, show or book twice.

- **The RED take — the plot.** Every event, in order, with *then* between them, to the very end. No
  opinion and no *who it's for*.
- **The GREEN take — the three moves** from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-its-about-and-what-you-thought.md), with no ending.

Then you ask each listener the same two questions: *do you want to watch it now?* and *what did I
think of it?*

## Why Vaani needs it

Retelling the whole plot is the most common way a beginner answers *was it good?*, because it is the
safest: you only need the past tense and *then*. This part makes you hear, once, what it gives the
listener, so you notice when you start doing it by accident.

Retelling a story properly is a later skill, with its own day. This part is about the difference
between retelling and recommending.

**Build the RED take properly.** At least eight events, joined with *then*, from the first scene to the

> Do you want to watch it now?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take that stops before the end**, because you do
not want to spoil it. For this exercise, include the ending, with somebody who does not mind.

The second is **a GREEN take that slides into the plot** after the first sentence. If you hear yourself
say *then*, stop and give your opinion.

The third, in real life, is **the plot told because you could not find the words for an opinion**. One
simple reason is enough: *it's funny*, *it's sad*, *it's too long*.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Retelling plots does happen between friends who have both seen something. For somebody who has not,
a fluent speaker gives one sentence of plot at most and spends the rest on why.

Under pressure, a plot is easy to produce and hard to stop, because each event leads to the next. The
exit is always the same sentence: *Anyway, I really liked it, because …*

A native listener will wait politely through a plot, and many will quietly decide not to watch it. The
three-move answer gets people to try things.
