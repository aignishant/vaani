## One-line answer

An adjective ending in *-ing* describes the thing that causes a feeling — *the speech was boring* —
and one ending in *-ed* describes the person who has the feeling — *I was bored* — so swapping them
changes who you are talking about.

## The story

After a cousin's wedding reception, you write in the family group:

> The food was amazing. The speeches were very long. I was very boring.

Your aunt replies with a laughing face:

> Don't be so hard on yourself! You were not boring at all, you danced very well!

You meant *I was bored* — the speeches made you feel that way. What you wrote was that *you* made
other people feel that way. One ending, two letters, and the whole family now thinks you were
apologising for yourself.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **adjective** — a word that tells you more about a noun, from
  [day 28](../../../day-28-at-the-doctor/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-an-adjective-does.md).
- **noun** — a word for a person, place or thing.
- **cause** — the thing that makes something happen.
- **feeling** — what happens inside a person: *bored*, *excited*, *worried*.

Many feeling words come in pairs:

| The thing that causes it (*-ing*) | The person who feels it (*-ed*) |
| --- | --- |
| *The speech was boring.* | *I was bored.* |
| *The news was worrying.* | *My mother was worried.* |
| *The trip was tiring.* | *We were tired.* |

Ask one question before you choose: **am I describing the cause or the person who feels it?**

- The cause — a film, a speech, a journey, a person who makes others feel something — gets *-ing*.
- The person who feels it gets *-ed*.

A person can be either. *He was boring* means he made other people feel bored. *He was bored* means he
felt bored. Both are about him, and they say opposite things.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story from your life, and stories are full of feelings: *I was
frightened*, *it was surprising*, *we were exhausted*. Every one of them is this choice. Choosing wrong
turns a line about how you felt into a line about what you are like.

And these words come up every time you write about an experience: a message after an event, a review,
feedback on a course.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Adjectives with '-ing' and '-ed'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adjectives-ing-ed).
It says: "If you say something is *interesting*, you mean it *interests* you" and "If something
*bores* you, you can say you feel *bored*", with the examples:

> I read a very **interesting** article in the newspaper today.
> We had nothing to do. We were really **bored**.

It lists common *-ing* adjectives — "amusing, annoying, boring, disappointing, exciting, interesting,
frightening, tiring, shocking, surprising, worrying" — and common *-ed* ones — "annoyed, bored, closed,
delighted, disappointed, excited, frightened, tired, worried".

**The rule in one picture:**

```text
      the cause                  the person who feels it
   ┌──────────────┐    makes     ┌──────────────────┐
   │ the speech   │  ─────────►  │  I               │
   │ was boring   │              │  was bored       │
   └──────────────┘              └──────────────────┘
        -ing                            -ed
```

**The ten pairs to know by heart**, all taken from the page's two lists:

| -ing | -ed |
| --- | --- |
| amusing | amused |
| annoying | annoyed |
| boring | bored |
| disappointing | disappointed |
| exciting | excited |
| frightening | frightened |
| interesting | interested |
| shocking | shocked |
| surprising | surprised |
| tiring | tired |

The page's *-ed* list does not name every partner of the *-ing* list. For *amused*, *interested*,
*shocked* and *surprised* here, the *-ed* form follows the same pattern as the others; check any word
you are unsure of in a dictionary.

**Where they stand.** Like any adjective, after *was* or *were*, or in front of a noun:

> The film was frightening. · It was a frightening film.
> The children were excited. · The excited children ran to the door.

**The spelling.** Every pair in the table is spelled as the page lists it. For a new pair, the
endings go on the same way they go on verbs: the *-ing* spelling from
[day 26](../../../day-26-making-plans/parts/02-writing/2.1-am-is-are-plus-ing.md) and the *-ed*
spelling from
[day 41](../../../day-41-what-you-did-yesterday/parts/02-writing/2.1-add-ed-and-what-happens-to-the-spelling.md):
*tire* → *tiring*, *tired*; *annoy* → *annoying*, *annoyed*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***-ing* about yourself when you mean the feeling**:

> I was very boring. · I am so exciting for the trip.

The reader reads what you say you *are like*, not what you felt.

The second is ***-ed* for the thing**:

> The film was bored. · It was a tired journey.

The reader understands and notices. A film cannot feel anything.

The third is **the pair split across the sentence the wrong way**:

> I was interesting in the talk because it was interested.

Both halves swapped. Ask the cause-or-feeler question for each word separately.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Native readers make this choice without thinking, and they notice a swap instantly — often with a
smile, as the aunt did. In a family message it is funny. In a job interview answer, *I am very
interesting in this job*, it says something you did not intend.

Under pressure, the *-ing* form comes out more often by mistake, because it is the one heard most in
descriptions of things. The check is quick: point at the word and ask *who or what?*

A formal reader, such as an employer reading a cover letter, reads *I am interested in* as normal and
*I am interesting in* as a mistake that makes them re-read the whole line.
