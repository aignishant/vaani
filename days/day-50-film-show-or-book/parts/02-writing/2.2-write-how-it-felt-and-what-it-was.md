## One-line answer

Write a short message about a real event you went to, using the *-ing* word for what the event was
like and the *-ed* word for how the people felt, in pairs — and then mark every one with **C** for
cause or **F** for feeler, because the mark is what catches the swap before the reader does.

## The story

The next family event is a long drive to see your grandmother. Afterwards your cousin, who could not
come, asks how it went.

You write:

> The drive was tiring — six hours in the heat. The children were bored by the second hour and
> annoyed with each other by the third. But Nani was so excited to see them. The best part was a
> surprising visit from her old neighbour, who is ninety and still walks to the market. We were all
> amazed.

Your cousin writes back: *Wish I'd been there.* Every feeling word sits on the right thing. The drive
and the visit caused feelings; the children, Nani and the family felt them. A reader can see the day
and everybody in it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **-ing adjective** — describes the cause of a feeling, from [part 2.1](2.1-bored-and-boring.md).
- **-ed adjective** — describes the person who feels it, from the same part.
- **pair** — the *-ing* and *-ed* words from the same verb: *tiring* and *tired*.
- **C and F** — your marks: C for cause, F for feeler.

A message about an event is full of both kinds, and it is strongest when they come in pairs:

> The drive was **tiring**, and we were all **tired** by the end.

The first half tells the reader about the event. The second tells them about the people. Together the
reader has the whole picture.

what things were like and how people felt about them. This part is that practice on a real event.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **using only *-ing* words**, because they are the ones that describe the
event:

> The drive was tiring, the children were boring, the visit was surprising.

*The children were boring* is now a comment on the children, and not a kind one.

The second is **stacking three feeling words on one person**:

> We were tired, bored and annoyed.

Correct, and flat. One feeling with a reason is stronger: *by the third hour the children were annoyed
with each other*.

The third is **a pair that uses two different verbs**, *the drive was tiring and we were exhausted*,
counted as a pair. It is a fine sentence; it does not practise the pair. Use the same verb for the
four pairs in this part.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Messages about events are one of the things people write most, and a reader reads them for the
feelings: were people happy, was it worth going. Getting the right word on the right thing is what
makes that clear.

Under pressure — a quick reply on a phone — people use one word for everything: *it was nice*. A pair
of *-ing* and *-ed* words tells a much fuller story in the same space.

A native reader reads the swap as a slip and moves on in a message. In a review, a report or a
feedback form, the swap makes them unsure what you meant, and they may take it the wrong way round.
