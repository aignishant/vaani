## One-line answer

Swap every *-ing* and *-ed* word in your real message on purpose, give it to a reader who has not seen
the original, and ask them what they now think of each person — because this swap does not look like
a grammar mistake to most readers; it looks like you said something about somebody.

## The story

Your first draft about the drive to your grandmother's, typed too fast, goes to your cousin:

> The drive was tired. The children were boring by the second hour. Nani was so exciting to see them.
> We were all amazing.

Your cousin writes back:

> Haha, the children were boring? Harsh! And Nani is very exciting, true. You were all amazing — modest!

Four swaps. The reader did not see four grammar mistakes. They saw you call the children dull, praise
your grandmother's personality, and praise yourselves. Three of the four came across as opinions you
never had.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **swap** — put the *-ing* word where the *-ed* word belongs, or the other way round.
- **-ing** and **-ed adjectives** — the cause and the feeler, from [part 2.1](2.1-bored-and-boring.md).
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A swap does one of two things:

| Swap | Example | What the reader reads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *-ed* on a thing | *The drive was tired.* | a mistake — a drive cannot feel |
| *-ing* on a person | *The children were boring.* | an **opinion** — you think the children are dull |

The first kind is harmless; everybody sees it is a slip. The second kind is the dangerous one, because
it is correct English with a different meaning.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story about real people. Every *-ing* word on a person in that story is
a judgement of them. You want only the judgements you meant.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Adjectives with '-ing' and '-ed'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/adjectives-ing-ed):
*-ing* describes what something does to you — "If you say something is *interesting*, you mean it
*interests* you" — and *-ed* how you feel — "If something *bores* you, you can say you feel *bored*".

**Step one — make the RED copy.** Take your real message from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-how-it-felt-and-what-it-was.md) and swap **every** *-ing* and *-ed* adjective.
Keep the original hidden.

**Step two — the opinion test.** Give the RED copy to a reader who has not seen the original. Ask:

> What do you think I think of each person in this?

Write their answers down.

**Step three — the mistake hunt.** Now ask:

> Do any words look wrong to you?

Write down which ones they point at.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader reads at least one swapped
*-ing* word on a person as an opinion of that person, rather than as a mistake. It is **GREEN** when you
can restore every word without looking at the original, and a second reader, given the fixed copy,
says only what you meant about each person.

**Step five — the fix.** Change only the ending:

> The children were boring. → The children were bored.
> Nani was so exciting. → Nani was so excited.

**Step six — your read-back line**, for every message about people from now on:

> Every *-ing* word on a person: do I really mean they made others feel this?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who knows you well**. They will guess what
you meant and read past the swap. Choose somebody who has to take your words as they are.

The second is **swapping only the easy ones**, the *-ed* words on things. They teach nothing new. The
swaps on people are the point.

The third, in real writing, is ***interesting* for *interested*** in an application: *I am very
interesting in this course.* It is the most common version of this error in formal writing, and the
reader may quietly take it as a boast.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A native reader usually works out the intended meaning — after a moment of surprise. That moment is
the cost: a laugh in a family group, a raised eyebrow in an email to a manager.

Under pressure people swap most often with *interested*, *excited* and *bored*, because the *-ing*
forms are so common in descriptions. Those three are worth a second look every time.

The fix is always the smallest one — two letters — and the read-back line finds it in seconds.
