# The corner the story turns

`FN-15` · Day 50

## The one-line answer

A sequence is a list of things that happened; a story is a sequence with one moment where it
changes direction — and that corner is the only thing today is about.

## The model

Picture two journeys. The first is a straight road. Things go past. Then more things go past. It
goes on for as long as there are things, and it ends when you get tired.

The second road has a corner in it. Everything before the corner exists so the corner can happen.
Everything after it is different because of the corner. And when somebody asks you about that
journey afterwards, the corner is what you describe.

That's the whole difference between an account and a story, and it's not a matter of how
interesting the events were.

Day 14 gave you the machinery for the road itself — *the other day*, *it turned out*, *next thing
I knew* — and it also gave you *that's when*, which is a signpost pointing at a corner. Day 35 gave
you the anecdote with a point on the end of it. Today is narrower than either and it sits between
them: the corner itself, and whether there is one.

Three parts, and their sizes are wrong in almost everybody's telling.

**The setup — the approach.** Its only job is to make the corner possible. That's it. It is not
where the detail goes, and this is where nearly every story dies, because the setup is the part
you remember most clearly and the part that costs you nothing to say.

> This was back when I was still {{doing the thing}}.

And then the one line that does the actual work of a setup — planting the thing the corner is
about to break:

> We'd already {{done the sensible thing}}, obviously.

That *obviously* is deliberate here, and it's the only place in this whole curriculum where you
want it. It plants an expectation in the listener's head so there's something for the corner to
break. Day 14's *which was fine, until …* does the same job from the other end.

**Here's the test, and it's the strict one.** The setup must contain the thing the corner
contradicts. If your corner is *it was shut*, the setup has to have contained *we'd checked*. If
your corner is *and he'd never met her*, the setup has to have contained something that made you
sure he had. Any detail in the setup that the corner doesn't use is a detail that comes out.

Which is a hearable rule with a specific consequence: a setup full of unused detail is why
listeners' eyes go, and why you find yourself speeding up halfway through your own story trying to
get to the good bit. If you're speeding up, the setup was too long, and the fix is not to speed up.

**The turn — the corner.** One sentence. Not a paragraph, not a build.

> And then, out of nowhere —
> Except it wasn't.

*Except it wasn't* is three words and it's the smallest complete turn in English. Whatever the
setup said was true, wasn't. It works because everything it needs is already sitting in the
listener's head, put there by the setup.

Say the corner differently from the sentences around it. Slow into it, then land — Day 26 gave you
the fall, and this is the sentence it was for. A corner said at the same pace and pitch as the
approach isn't a corner; it's just the next thing on the list, and the listener will hear it as
one.

**The test that tells you whether you have a story at all:** say the turn sentence on its own,
out loud, with nothing round it. Does it still turn? If it's just a fact — *the shop was shut* —
you've got a sequence. If it's a fact that contradicts something, you've got a story, and the
thing it contradicts is what your setup needs to contain.

**And the point — where you arrive.** One sentence, and it is not a moral. It's the reason you
told it.

> So now I always {{do the thing differently}}.
> I've thought about it a lot since.

The point is what makes the story yours rather than an event you witnessed. Delivered as a
consequence you still live with, it lands. Delivered as a lesson for the listener, it curdles
immediately, and everybody in the room can feel the difference.

Then stop. Do not go back round for another lap.

**Today's phrases** — the approach, the corner, the arrival. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md`
tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| This was back when I was still … | Opening the setup with the ordinary state, in one line |
| We'd already {{done the sensible thing}}, obviously. | Planting the expectation the corner is about to break |
| And then, out of nowhere — | Marking the corner just before you take it |
| Except it wasn't. | The turn in three words, when the expectation simply fails |
| That's the part I didn't see coming. | Pointing at the corner, so they know where to look |
| So now I always … | Delivering the point as a habit you kept |
| I've thought about it a lot since. | Closing on the point without spelling out a moral |
| Which is the only reason I'm telling you. | Naming why the story exists, when it needs saying |

## Say it now

One real thing that happened to you. Build the whole shape out of these, in order.

1. *This was back when I was still {{doing the thing}}.*
2. *We'd already {{done the sensible thing}}, obviously.*
3. *And then, out of nowhere — {{the corner}}.*
4. *Except it wasn't.*
5. *That's the part I didn't see coming.*
6. *So now I always {{do the thing differently}}.*
7. *I've thought about it a lot since.*
8. *Which is the only reason I'm telling you.*

Then take line 3 or line 4 — whichever is your actual corner — and say it on its own, three times,
with nothing before or after it. Each time, ask out loud: does that still turn, or is it just a
fact? If it's just a fact, the story doesn't need a better corner. It needs a setup that plants
what the corner breaks, and that's the line to go and write.

## The deliberate failure

Tell a real story with the corner taken out. Every event, in order, nothing left out, nothing
promoted above anything else. Just what happened, then what happened next.

**What breaking sounds like:** it never ends. You'll hear yourself running out of road and looking
for somewhere to stop, and you'll stop by trailing off, or by saying *anyway*, or by summarising
what you just said. The whole thing sags in the middle and the ending arrives because you stopped
talking rather than because you got anywhere.

The uncomfortable part is that this is the version most people tell most of the time. It isn't
bad English. It's fluent, the grammar is fine, and it's completely unfixable by speaking better —
which is why nobody has ever been able to tell you what was wrong with it.

Now tell the same events again, cutting the setup to two sentences and keeping the corner. It'll
be far shorter and it'll be a story. Play both back. The short one is better, and it's not close.

## When it breaks in the wild

You start a story at a table. The setup is going well, and because it's going well you add another
bit of background, and then somebody asks a perfectly friendly clarifying question about the
background — who that person was, when this was — and you answer it, and then answer the follow-up.
Now you're explaining a family tree. The corner never arrives. Somebody starts a different
conversation at the other end of the table and your story quietly stops existing.

**The repair:**

> Right — skip all that. The thing was, {{the corner}}.

Cut to the corner and let the background go. You will feel that they can't possibly follow it
without the background, and you'll be wrong — a corner carries most of its own setup with it, and
what it doesn't carry, nobody was going to miss.
