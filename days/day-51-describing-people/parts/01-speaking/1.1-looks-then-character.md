## One-line answer

*What does she look like?* asks about the body — height, hair, face, clothes — and *What is she like?*
asks about the person — how she behaves, what she is like to be around — so hear which question
came, answer that one, and for character always add one thing the person actually does.

## The story

A friend from your old job calls. You have a new manager.

> So what's she like?

You answer:

> She's tall, short hair, she wears glasses, around forty.

There is a pause.

> No, I mean — is she nice? Is she strict?

Your friend asked *what's she like*, and you answered *what does she look like*. The two questions
sound almost the same. They ask for completely different things.

You try again:

> Oh — she's fair. Very direct. On the first day she told us exactly what she didn't like about the
> old system, and then she asked us what we didn't like about it.

Now your friend knows something about working for her.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **appearance** — how somebody looks: tall, grey hair, glasses.
- **character** — what somebody is like as a person: kind, strict, funny, shy.
- **build** — the shape and size of a body: slim, heavy, broad.
- **example** — one real thing a person did that shows their character.
- **one person, one word, one fact** — the shape from
  [day 14](../../../day-14-talking-about-family/parts/01-speaking/1.2-talk-about-your-own-family.md).

On day 14 you described a family member with one word and one fact. Today there are two separate
kinds of description, and two questions that ask for them:

| The question | What it asks for | The answer starts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *What does she look like?* | appearance | *She's tall …* · *She's got short hair …* |
| *What is she like?* · *What's she like?* | character | *She's fair …* · *She's quite shy …* |

The only audible difference is *look*. Listen for it. If you hear *look*, describe the body. If you do
not, describe the person.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a story from your life, and stories are full of people. A listener needs to see
the important ones and to know what they are like, in a line or two each, without the story stopping.

And both questions come up all the time: somebody meeting your relative at the station needs looks; a
friend asking about your new neighbour wants character. Answering the wrong one wastes both of your
time.

## The rule

**Two dictionary entries were opened on 2026-09-23.**
[look like](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/look_like): "To resemble or have the appearance of being".
[be like](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/be_like): "To be typical or characteristic of (someone or
something)", with the example "… what people are like …".

**Appearance, in the order a stranger needs it** — from the big things you see across a room to the
small things you see close up:

| Order | What | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | height and build | *tall*, *short*, *medium height*, *slim*, *heavy* |
| 2 | age | *about forty*, *in her twenties*, *elderly* |
| 3 | hair | *short grey hair*, *long black hair*, *bald* |
| 4 | one face detail | *glasses*, *a beard*, *a big smile* |
| 5 | clothes, if you are describing today | *a blue sari*, *a white shirt* |

Two verbs carry it. The page
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got),
opened on 2026-09-23, lists appearance among the uses of *have got*, with "My sister **has got** long
hair. = My sister**'s got** long hair."

> She's tall. He's about sixty. — *is* for height, build and age
> She's got short hair. He's got a beard. — *has got* for things on the body

**Character: one word, then one example.** The word alone could be anybody. The example makes it
theirs:

> He's very patient. When my son broke his window, he just laughed and fixed it himself.

**Soften the hard words.** Words like *strict*, *quiet*, *lazy* sound unkind said flat. English
softens them:

> She's quite strict. · He's a bit quiet at first. · She can be a little impatient.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **answering the other question**, as in the story. It is easy, because
the questions differ by one word.

The second is **character with no example**:

> He's very nice, very good person, very helpful.

Three kind words. The listener still does not know him.

The third is **looks in a jumble**:

> She has glasses, she's tall, blue sari, long hair, she's maybe fifty.

All the details are there, and a person searching a crowd needs them in the order they will see them:
tall first, glasses last.

The fourth is ***has* for height or age**:

> He has tall. She has forty years.

*He's tall. She's forty.*

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers answer *what's she like?* with a word and a small story almost every time: *she's
lovely — she brought us food the day we moved in*. The story is what the listener remembers.

Under pressure — describing somebody to a guard, a doctor, the police — people give the details they
remember first, in any order, and the listener cannot build a picture. The order in the table is the
one people use when they are trying to help somebody find a person.

A native listener hears a flat *she's strict* as a complaint. *She's quite strict, but fair* sounds
like a description. That small softening decides whether you sound kind.
