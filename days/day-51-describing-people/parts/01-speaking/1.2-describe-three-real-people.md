## One-line answer

Describe three real people for three real reasons — so somebody can find one in a crowd, know what to
expect from another, and meet the third properly — and test each description on the listener it was
for, because a description is only good if it does its job.

## The story

Your cousin is coming to the city and will stay with your neighbour for two nights while you are away.
She rings you with three questions in one call.

> What does your neighbour look like? I have to find her at the bus stand.
> And what's she like? Should I be careful?
> And your husband's brother is coming for dinner — what's he like?

Three people, three descriptions, and each one has a different job. The first must get your cousin
to the right woman in a crowd. The second must tell her how to behave in somebody else's house. The
third must get a dinner conversation started.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **appearance** and **character** — how somebody looks and what they are like, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-looks-then-character.md).
- **purpose** — what a description is for.
- **tip** — a small piece of useful advice: *she likes people to take their shoes off*.
- **conversation starter** — something the listener can ask the person about.

The same person needs a different description for a different purpose.

| Purpose | What goes in | What stays out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **find them** | height and build, age, hair, one face detail, what they will wear | character |
| **know what to expect** | one or two character words, each with an example, and one tip | most appearance |
| **meet them well** | one character word, what they care about, one conversation starter | almost all appearance |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a story from your life told to a stranger. The people in that story need to be
described for the story's purpose — usually *know what to expect* — in a line or two, not a full list.
This part trains that choice.

And these three calls happen in real life every week: someone collecting a relative, someone staying
with a friend, someone meeting your family.

> She's short and quite heavy, about sixty-five, with white hair in a bun. She'll have a green bag.

For *know what to expect*, a word, an example and a tip:

> She's very kind, but she's particular about her kitchen — she once rewashed every plate I washed. So
> ask before you cook.

For *meet them well*, a word, what they care about, and a starter:

> He's quiet at first, but he's mad about cricket. Ask him about the last match and he won't stop.

**Step three — say each one three times** without writing it down, recorder on.

**Step four — give each to its real listener** and ask one question that tests its job:

| Purpose | The test question |
| --- | --- |
| find them | *If she walked in now, would you know her?* |
| know what to expect | *What would you be careful about?* |
| meet them well | *What would you talk to him about?* |

Write down their answers.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the same description for every purpose**: looks and character and
life history, every time. The listener has to dig out what they need.

The second is **the find-them description full of character**:

> She's very kind, very religious, very good cook.

Nobody can find a kind woman at a bus stand.

The third is **the tip that is really a complaint**: *she's very difficult, she shouts*. Turn it into
something the listener can do: *she likes things done her way, so ask first*.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers change their description of the same person without thinking, depending on who is
asking and why. That flexibility is what makes them sound helpful rather than chatty.

Under pressure, on the phone, the find-them description gets jumbled. Say height and build first,
every time. Everything else can follow.

A native listener values the tip most of all — *ask before you cook*, *don't mention politics*. It is
the part of a description that saves them from a mistake.
