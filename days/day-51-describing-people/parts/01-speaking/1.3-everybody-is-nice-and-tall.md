## One-line answer

Describe one person in a real group photo on purpose with only the words that fit everybody — *nice,
medium height, black hair* — then describe them properly, and ask a listener to point at the right
face each time, because a description that fits five people describes nobody.

## The story

A cousin is showing a friend the photos from a family function on her phone. The friend asks which
one is your uncle.

> He's the one who's medium height, black hair, very nice man.

The friend looks at a photo with eleven men in it. Eight of them are medium height with black hair.
All of them look nice. The friend guesses, and points at the wrong man.

Then your cousin says:

> Second row, the one who's much taller than everyone, with the grey moustache. He's laughing — he's
> always laughing.

The friend points straight at him.

Every word in the first description was true. None of it separated your uncle from anybody else.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not
one we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **general** — true of many people at once.
- **distinctive** — true of this person and not the ones around them.
- **appearance** — how somebody looks, from [part 1.1](1.1-looks-then-character.md).
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A description works when it picks one person out from the others near them.

| Kind of detail | Example | Picks one person out? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| general | *medium height*, *black hair*, *nice* | no — it fits most people in the photo |
| distinctive | *much taller than everyone*, *grey moustache*, *laughing* | yes |
| position | *second row, on the left* | yes, but only in this photo |

The RED description uses only general details. The GREEN one uses at least two distinctive ones.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a story told to a stranger who has never seen the people in it. General
descriptions leave them with a room full of identical people. One distinctive detail per person is
what makes a story's characters different from each other.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[look like](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/look_like): "To resemble or have the appearance of being" —
which is exactly what a description has to capture: what this person resembles that the others do not.

**Step one — choose a real group photo** on your phone with at least six people in it, and one person
the listener does not know.

**Step two — build the RED description.** Only general details: height as *medium*, hair colour, age as
*middle-aged*, character as *nice* or *good*. Nothing that separates them.

**Step three — build the GREEN description.** At least two distinctive details: something that is
different from the people near them — taller, older, a beard when nobody else has one, what they are
doing in the photo.

**Step four — the pointing test.** Show the photo to one listener and give the RED description. Ask
them to point. Then show it to a second listener and give the GREEN one.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED description that slips in a distinctive
detail**, because you know the person. Test it: does each detail fit at least three people in the
photo? If not, take it out.

The second is **a listener who knows the family**. They will point from memory.

The third, in real life, is **the distinctive detail that is unkind**: *the fat one*, *the one with the
big nose*. It picks the person out, and it will reach them. Choose a detail they would not mind hearing:
*the tallest*, *the one in the red shirt*, *the one laughing*.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers describing someone in a crowd almost always use a comparison — *the tall one*, *the
older man*, *the one with the beard* — because comparison is what picks one person out. They say it
fast, and the listener finds the face.

Under pressure — on the phone, trying to help somebody find a relative — people give the general
details first because those come to mind first. Start with the one thing that is different.

A native listener also notices kindness. A description that picks somebody out with a friendly detail
sounds warm; one that uses a body part sounds rude, even in a joke.
