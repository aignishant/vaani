## One-line answer

A paragraph about a person you know well opens with one line that says what they are like, and then
proves it — every character word is followed by something the person actually did or does — because
a reader believes what they are shown, not what they are told.

## The story

Your uncle turns sixty. The family is making a small book, and every relative writes one paragraph
about him.

Two paragraphs arrive on the same evening.

> Mama is a very kind, generous and hardworking person. He always helps everybody. He is very funny
> and everyone loves him. We are lucky to have him.

> Mama is the person everyone calls when something breaks. He has fixed my mother's sewing machine,
> the neighbours' gate and, once, a goat's leg. He never takes money. He says he is paid in tea, and he
> keeps a list of whose tea is best.

Everyone reads the second one aloud at the party. The first one is correct and warm, and it could be
about any uncle in any family. The second could only be about him.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **character** — what somebody is like as a person.
- **character word** — an adjective for character: *kind*, *patient*, *stubborn*.
- **evidence** — something that proves what you said: a thing the person did or does.
- **topic sentence** — the first line, which says the topic and the point, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **describe a person** — who, looks, character, one thing they do, from
  [day 13](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/02-writing/2.2-describe-a-person.md).

On day 13 you wrote a person in four pieces: who, how they look, what they are like, one thing they
do. That paragraph helps a stranger find somebody. Today's paragraph is for a person you **know well**,
and its job is different: it lets the reader *know* them.

The difference is the ratio. Day 13 had one line of character. Today most of the paragraph is
character, and every piece of it comes with proof.

| Piece | Job | How many |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the first line | what they are like, in one idea | one |
| evidence | things they did or do that show it | two or three |
| a detail only you would know | a habit, a phrase they say, an object | one |
| the last line | one plain sentence to end on | one |

Appearance can be one short phrase, or left out. Somebody who knows him does not need his height.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story from your life, and its people have to feel real. A reader
believes a person through what they do, not through a list of good words about them.

It is also a piece of writing people actually ask for: a birthday message, a farewell card, a few lines
about a teacher or a grandparent.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got),
for the one line of appearance if you use it: "My sister **has got** long hair."

**The first line: what they are like, as a promise.** Not a list of adjectives, one idea:

> Mama is the person everyone calls when something breaks.
> My grandmother has never wasted anything in her life.

Each of these is a topic sentence with a point. The rest of the paragraph proves it.

**Evidence: what they did, or what they always do.** Two tenses do this job:

| Tense | For | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| past simple | one time that shows it | *He fixed my mother's sewing machine.* |
| present simple with a how-often word | what they always do | *He never takes money.* · *She always saves the string.* |

**The rule of the pair.** If you write a character word, the next sentence proves it:

> She is very patient. When I failed my exam the first time, she sat with me every evening for a month
> and never once said *I told you so*.

**The detail only you would know.** A phrase they always say, an object they keep, a small habit:

> He says he is paid in tea.

This is the line readers remember.

**The last line: plain.** One sentence, no big lesson:

> He is sixty now, and the goat is still walking.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the adjective pile**:

> He is kind, generous, hardworking, honest and funny.

Five words, no proof. It reads like a certificate. Part [2.3](2.3-kind-helpful-and-hardworking.md)
builds this on purpose.

The second is **proof with no first line**: three good stories in a row, and the reader does not know
what they are meant to show.

The third is **the ending that becomes a speech**:

> People like him are rare in this world and we should all learn from him.

It is sincere, and it pulls the reader out of the person and into a lesson. A plain last line keeps
them with him.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

The pieces of writing that people keep — cards, speeches at weddings and funerals, a few lines in a
book — are full of small true things the person did. That is not a writing trick. It is how people
recognise somebody they love in words.

Under pressure, when the feeling is strong, writers reach for the biggest words: *best*, *kindest*,
*greatest*. They feel right and they say less than one small action.

A reader who knows the person will forgive clumsy grammar in a paragraph that sounds like them. They
will not quite believe a perfect paragraph that could be about anyone.
