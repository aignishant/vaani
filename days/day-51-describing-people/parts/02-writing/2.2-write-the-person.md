## One-line answer

Write a full paragraph about one real person you know well — a first line, two or three pieces of
evidence, a detail only you would know and a plain last line — and give it to somebody else who knows
that person, then ask them *is this them?*, because they are the only reader who can tell you if it is
true.

## The story

The birthday book for your uncle is almost full. Your cousin asks you to read her paragraph before she
sends it.

It is good, but one sentence stops you: *Mama is always very calm.* You have seen him shout at a
cricket match. You tell her. She laughs, and changes it: *Mama is calm about everything except
cricket.* Now it is true, and it is funnier.

Only someone who knows him could have caught that. A stranger would have read *always very calm* and
believed it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **evidence** — something the person did or does that proves what you said, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-say-it-then-prove-it.md).
- **the rule of the pair** — a character word is followed by the sentence that proves it, from the
  same part.
- **true** — what somebody who knows the person would recognise.
- **revise** — change a piece of writing to make it better.

This part asks two different readers two different questions:

| Reader | Knows the person? | The question | What it tests |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | yes | *Is this them? Is anything not true?* | whether it is true |
| B | no | *What would you expect this person to do if …?* | whether it shows them |

A paragraph can pass one and fail the other. It needs to pass both.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story from your life, read by a stranger. Your people have to be true
enough for somebody who knows them and clear enough for somebody who does not. This part is that test,
done early, on one person.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got),
for any appearance line: "I **have got** brown hair."

**Step one — choose one real person** you know well and one reason to write: a birthday, a farewell, a
thank-you, or a page in a family book.

**Step two — plan in four lines**, not sentences:

| --- | --- |
| first line — one idea | `TODO(me)` |
| evidence 1 — one time (past) | `TODO(me)` |
| evidence 2 — always does (present) | `TODO(me)` |
| a detail only I would know | `TODO(me)` |

**Step three — write the paragraph**, six to nine sentences. Keep the first line first, put the detail
near the end, and finish with one plain sentence.

**Step four — the pair check.** Underline every character word. Check the sentence after it proves it.
If it does not, add the proof or take the word out.

**Step five — reader A**, who knows the person. Ask:

> Is this them? Is anything not true?

Write down exactly what they say, and change anything they say is not true. Often the fix is not to
remove it but to make it more exact, as in the story: *calm about everything except cricket*.

**Step six — reader B**, who does not know the person. Ask one question about a situation the paragraph
does not mention:

> If this person found a wallet on the road, what would they do?

If reader B can answer with confidence, the paragraph showed the person. If they cannot, the evidence
is too thin.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **skipping reader A**, because the paragraph feels true to you. Everyone
has a picture of the people they love that is a little too kind or a little out of date. Reader A
corrects it.

The second is **the paragraph that describes what the person means to you**, not the person:

> He taught me so much. Without him I would not be who I am.

It is about you. A reader learns nothing about him.

The third is **evidence that is too big**: *he saved the whole village*. Small true things are more
believable and more *him*.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Before a speech or a card goes out, fluent writers often show it to someone else who knows the person.
It is the same as reader A. It catches the line that is not quite true before the person hears it.

Under pressure — a card to write tonight — people use the adjective pile, because it is fast. Even one
pair, one word and its proof, turns a card into something the person keeps.

The person you describe is also a reader, in the end. They will notice the detail only you knew, and
it is usually the sentence they remember.
