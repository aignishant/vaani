## One-line answer

Write a paragraph on purpose about your real person using only character words and no evidence, then
write the same kind of paragraph about a second real person, remove both names, and ask somebody who
knows both to say which is which — because if they cannot tell, the paragraphs described nobody.

## The story

The birthday book comes back from the printer. Your aunt reads through it and stops at two paragraphs
on facing pages.

> He is a very kind and helpful person. He always helps everyone. He is hardworking and honest. We are
> proud of him.

> She is a very kind and loving person. She always helps everyone. She is hardworking and never
> complains. We are proud of her.

One is about your uncle. The other is about your aunt herself, from a different book the printer put in
by mistake. Without the names, she says, she could not have told which was which. The only difference
is *he* and *she*.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not
one we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **character word** — an adjective for what somebody is like, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-say-it-then-prove-it.md).
- **evidence** — something the person did or does that proves it, from the same part.
- **interchangeable** — could be about anybody, so it is about nobody.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A paragraph of character words is warm, correct and interchangeable. The words most often used —
*kind*, *helpful*, *hardworking*, *honest*, *loving* — fit almost everyone who is being written about
kindly. So two such paragraphs about two very different people come out nearly the same.

| Paragraph | What it is made of | Can a reader tell who it is? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RED | character words, *always*, *very* | no |
| GREEN | one idea, evidence, a detail only you would know | yes |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story with people in it. If two people in your story are both described
as *kind and helpful*, the reader cannot keep them apart. This part shows you, with your own people,
how fast that happens.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Present simple: 'have got'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple-have-got) —
a reminder that even appearance, the easiest evidence, is specific: "My sister **has got** long hair",
not *my sister is nice-looking*.

**Step one — write two RED paragraphs**, one about the person from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-the-person.md) and one about a second real person that the same reader knows. Each
four or five sentences, **only** character words with *very* and *always*. No evidence. No names — use
*he* or *she*, or *this person*.

**Step two — write two GREEN paragraphs** about the same two people, with no names: your revised
paragraph from part 2.2 and a new one about the second person.

**Step three — the which-is-which test.** Give the two RED paragraphs to a reader who knows both people.
Ask:

> Which of these is about [person 1], and which is about [person 2]?

Then do the same with the two GREEN paragraphs.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **choosing two people who are very different in
appearance**, and letting a *he* and a *she*, or an age, give it away. Choose two people of the same
kind — two aunts, two colleagues — so only the evidence can decide.

The second is **a reader who knows only one of them**. They cannot do the test.

The third, in real writing, is **a GREEN paragraph that becomes RED at the end**, with *he is the best
person in the world*. One such line at the end is fine. Check it has not replaced the evidence.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Reference letters, farewell cards and speeches are full of RED paragraphs, and readers skim them because
they have read the same words many times. The one sentence of real evidence is what they stop at.

Under pressure, when you are writing about somebody you are fond of, the character words come first
because they feel generous. The evidence is more generous: it shows you noticed.

A formal reader — an employer reading a reference — treats *hardworking and honest* as the minimum and
reads nothing into it. *She stayed until the last box was packed, three nights running* tells them
something.
