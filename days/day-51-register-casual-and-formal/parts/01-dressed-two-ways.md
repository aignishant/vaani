# The same sentence, dressed two ways

`IN-11` · Day 51

## The one-line answer

Register is not a second language — it is the same sentence in different clothes, and the skill is
being able to put the other outfit on the moment the room asks for it.

## The model

You already do this in your first language, constantly, without noticing. You do not speak to a
close friend the way you speak to somebody's grandmother, and you did not learn two languages to
manage it. You learned one wardrobe. The words underneath are the same words. What changes is what
they are wearing.

In English, most people learning it end up with one outfit and wear it everywhere. Which outfit
depends on where the English came from. If yours came mostly from documents and forms and study,
you own a suit, and you wear the suit to a tea break. If yours came mostly from films and songs
and friends, you own a t-shirt, and you wear the t-shirt into a room where somebody is deciding
whether to trust you with something. Neither is a vocabulary problem. Both are people who own one
outfit.

**Three dials, and that is genuinely all.** You do not need a formal vocabulary list. You need to
know which three things move.

**The first dial is length.** Formal English takes the long way round. *Can you send it?* becomes
*Would it be possible for you to send it across?* Same request, more steps. Casual English cuts
steps out until only the meaning is left standing. Listen for it:

> Can you send it?
> Would it be possible to send it across?

Say both out loud, one after the other. The second one is not more polite because of any single
word. It is more polite because it takes longer, and taking longer is what respect sounds like in
English.

**The second dial is hedging.** Formal English pads the edges. *I think* becomes *I'd have thought*.
*No* becomes *I'm not sure that would work*. A hedge is a small cushion around the thing you are
saying so it does not land hard.

> That won't work.
> I'm not sure that would quite work, to be honest.

**The third dial is the words themselves** — and this is the smallest one, though it is the one
everybody thinks the whole thing is about. A handful of everyday pairs do most of the lifting.
*Get* against *receive*. *Sort out* against *resolve*. *Cheers* against *thank you*. *Reckon*
against *think*. You need about a dozen of these, not a dictionary.

**Now the thing that catches people out.** Formal spoken English is not written English read
aloud. It still contracts. It still says *I'd*, *we'll*, *that's*, *isn't*. A person who speaks
formally in a meeting still says *I don't think we're ready* — they do not say *I do not think we
are ready*, and if they did, everybody would look up. Uncontracted speech is not more formal. It
is either an emphasis or a foreigner in a suit that does not fit.

So the suit is not made of long words and full forms. It is made of length and cushioning, said in
an ordinary mouth.

**Which outfit, and how you tell.** You do not read the room from the room's decoration. You read
it from the other person's first sentence, and you match it. If they greet you with *alright?*,
you are not in a suit conversation, whatever the building looks like. If they open with *thanks so
much for making the time*, put the jacket on. Matching is the whole move. English speakers do it
to each other constantly, within a sentence or two of meeting, and almost nobody notices it
happening.

**And you can move mid-conversation**, in either direction — that is normal, and it is how a
conversation warms up. The formal opening relaxes after a few exchanges. Somebody rolls their
sleeves up first and everybody follows. Going the other way — cooling down — happens when the
subject turns serious, and it is a signal in itself.

**Today's phrases** — four pairs, the same job done twice, once dressed up and once dressed down.
All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Would it be possible to …? | The jacket on. Asking a favour of someone senior, or a stranger |
| Could you do me a favour? | The same request, sleeves rolled up, to somebody you know |
| What are your thoughts on …? | Asking for a view in a room where you are being assessed |
| Do you reckon …? | The same question, over a table, to a friend |
| That's really helpful, thank you. | Thanking upward, or in front of other people |
| Cheers for that. | The same thanks, to a colleague, in passing |
| I'll get that over to you. | Promising to send something, in a formal exchange |
| I'll send it across. | The same promise, to somebody at your own level |

## Say it now

Each line twice. First as written. Then swapped into its twin's register, using your own words —
so line 1 becomes a favour you are asking of a friend, line 2 becomes a request to somebody
senior, and so on down the list.

1. *Would it be possible to {{the thing you want}}?*
2. *Could you do me a favour — {{the thing you want}}?*
3. *What are your thoughts on {{the decision}}?*
4. *Do you reckon {{the same decision}}?*
5. *That's really helpful, thank you — I'll {{what you'll do with it}}.*
6. *Cheers for that, I'll {{the same thing}}.*
7. *I'll get that over to you once {{the condition}}.*
8. *I'll send it across once {{the same condition}}.*

Then run the odd-numbered lines as one stretch and the even-numbered lines as one stretch, back to
back, and listen to the two of you. Same person, same information, two different rooms.

## The deliberate failure

Take three ordinary things you would say at home today — passing something at a table, asking
somebody to move, saying you are going out — and say each one fully dressed up. Long form,
hedged, jacket on. *Would it be possible for you to pass me the salt, if it's not too much
trouble?* Say them at normal speed, as though they were completely ordinary.

**What breaking sounds like:** it is not funny, which is the surprise. It is cold. Over-dressing
does not sound extra polite; it sounds like distance, and it puts distance in the room. Say those
lines to somebody and watch what comes back — they will go formal too, because matching is
automatic, and now the two of you are strangers over the salt.

Now the half of this you cannot hear on your own tape, and the reason today exists. **Nobody
corrects your register.** Not once, ever. Get a word wrong and somebody helps you. Turn up
over-dressed to a friendly conversation and nothing happens at all — the other person just cools
by a degree, the chat stays short, and you walk away thinking that person was a bit unfriendly. It
is the most invisible mistake in this whole curriculum, and it costs more than pronunciation does.

## When it breaks in the wild

A tea break, a corridor, somebody you have worked near for months. They say *alright? Good
weekend?* — and out comes your suit. *It was quite pleasant, thank you. And how was yours?* It is
correct English. Every word is right. And the conversation is over in two exchanges, because you
handed them a form to fill in and they only wanted a chat. Repeat that for a few months and you
are the person nobody stops to talk to, which reads back to you as a language problem when it is
a wardrobe problem.

**The repair:**

> Sorry, that came out really formal — yeah, it was alright, we didn't do much.

Naming it costs you nothing and buys you the other outfit immediately. English speakers do this to
each other all the time.
