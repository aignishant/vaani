## One-line answer

The same words can be a statement or a question — *You're leaving tonight.* or *You're leaving
tonight?* — and the whole difference is where your voice goes on the **last loud word**: down for a
statement, up for a question, with any small words after it following the same direction.

## The story

At the chemist. The woman behind the counter hands you a box and says:

> Two tablets after dinner.

You want to check. You say the same words back:

> Two tablets after dinner.

Your voice goes down at the end. She nods and turns to the next customer. She heard you *agree*, not
*ask*. You meant *is that right? Not one?*

The next day, a different chemist, the same box. This time:

> Two tablets after ↗DINner?

Your voice rises on *din-*. She stops and says:

> Yes, two, and always after food — never on an empty stomach.

Same four words. The second time, the tune turned them into a question, and she answered it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **tune** — how your voice goes up and down, from
  [day 24](../../../day-24-voice-up-and-down/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md).
- **falling** and **rising** — the voice going down, *I've finished*, and up, *your turn*, from the
  same part.
- **loud word** — a word that carries meaning and is said loud and long, from
  [day 13](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/01-speaking/1.1-loud-words-and-quiet-words.md).
- **statement** — a sentence that tells something.
- **the turning word** — the last loud word in a sentence, where the tune goes up or down.
- **the tail** — the small quiet words after the turning word.

On day 24 you learned that falling means *finished* and rising means *your turn*. Today is about
**where** the fall or rise happens.

It does not happen on the last word. It happens on the **last loud word**, and it carries on through
any quiet words after it.

| Sentence | The turning word | The tail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Two tablets after dinner?* | **din**ner | — |
| *You're leaving tonight?* | to**night** | — |
| *You're seeing him tonight?* | to**night** | — |
| *You bought it for her?* | **bought** — if *for her* is quiet | *it for her* |

In the last example the rise starts on *bought* and keeps climbing through *it for her*. If you wait for
*her* to rise, the rise is too late and too small.

## Why Vaani needs it

Checking what you heard, without changing the words, is one of the most useful things you can do in
English: at a counter, on the phone, with a doctor. It only works if the tune makes it a question.

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story, and stories are full of lines like *and she says, "You're leaving
tonight?"*. The tune on that line is how the listener knows it was a question.

## The rule

**The page checked for the tune, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Intonation (linguistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_(linguistics)). It says that "a
falling pitch movement is associated with statements, but a rising pitch turns a statement into a
yes–no question, as in *He's going ↗home?*", and that the main movement comes on "the *nucleus*, which
corresponds to the main accented syllable of the intonation unit, usually in the last lexical word".
That *nucleus* is what this part calls the turning word; a *lexical word* is a meaning word — what day
13 called a loud word.

**Two words to hear first.** Both pages were opened on 2026-09-23. Listen first, then say it with the
recorder on, then play both back.

| Word | Listen here | What the page gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tonight | [tonight](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tonight) | /təˈnaɪt/, with a US audio file; the stress is on *-night* |
| really | [really](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/really) | /ˈɹɪə.li/ for Received Pronunciation; /ˈɹɪl.i/ and /ˈɹi.li/ for US English, with a US audio file; the interjection "Indicating surprise at, or requesting confirmation of, some new information" |

A dictionary gives each word on its own. Your job is to put it at the end of a sentence and turn it.

**The two shapes, on the same words:**

```text
   statement — you are telling
   You're leaving to-NIGHT.
                     ‾‾‾‾\
                          \___         ← falls on the turning word, and stays low

   question — you are checking
   You're leaving to-NIGHT?
                        ___/‾‾         ← rises on the turning word
                    ___/
```

**Find the turning word before you speak.** Ask: *which is the last word that carries meaning?* In
*two tablets after dinner*, it is *dinner*. In *you're coming with us*, it is *coming* — *with us* is
the tail.

**Start the move on the stressed part of the turning word.** *To-NIGHT* turns on *night*. *DIN-ner*
turns on *din*, and *-ner* carries on in the same direction.

**Make the move big.** A small rise at the very end sounds unsure, like a statement that trailed off.
A clear rise on the turning word sounds like a question.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the checking question said falling**:

> Two tablets after dinner.

The listener hears agreement and moves on. You leave with your doubt.

The second is **the rise saved for the last syllable**:

> You bought it for ↗HER?

The listener may hear a question about *her* — *for her, not for somebody else?* — when you meant *you
BOUGHT it? I thought it was a gift*.

The third is **the rise on the wrong loud word**:

> ↗YOU'RE leaving tonight?

Now the question is *you, of all people?* rather than *tonight?*.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers check information with statement-shaped questions all the time — *the shop's closed
today?*, *you're not coming?* — and they rely on the tune completely. A listener answers these without
thinking, as long as the rise is clear.

Under pressure, in a noisy place or on a bad line, the rise is often the first thing to shrink. Make it
bigger than feels natural: a question that sounds like a statement gets no answer.

Many languages mark a question with a word or a particle and do not need the tune. If yours does, your
English checking questions may come out flat without you noticing. That is the reason for the recorder.
