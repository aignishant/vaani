## One-line answer

When somebody tells you something important, say the key part back to them with a clear rise on its
turning word — *after dinner?*, *four hundred?*, *Thursday?* — and count how often they confirm or
correct it, because a rising echo is the fastest way to catch a mistake before you leave the counter.

## The story

The chemist again, a week later, and a new prescription. She gives you three pieces of information in
one breath:

> One in the morning, two at night, and don't take it with milk.

You say back only the parts that matter, each with a rise:

> One in the ↗MORning?
> Two at ↗NIGHT?
> No ↗MILK?

She answers each one: *yes*, *yes*, *not even in tea*. That last answer is new information you would
not have got if you had just said *okay*. Three short rises, and the conversation went further than
the instructions.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the turning word** — the last loud word, where the tune goes up or down, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-the-tune-turns-on-the-last-loud-word.md).
- **echo** — saying back part of what somebody just said.
- **confirm** — say that something is right.
- **key detail** — the part of a message you would be in trouble for getting wrong.

A checking echo has three pieces:

| Piece | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| only the key detail | keeps the check short | *two at night?* — not the whole instruction |
| a rise on its turning word | makes it a question | *two at ↗NIGHT?* |
| one detail at a time | lets them answer each one | three short rises, not one long one |

And there is one word that does a checking job all on its own: *Really?* with a rise asks *is that
true?*; *Really.* with a fall says *I see* or *I doubt it*.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the speaking skill that protects you in every practical conversation in phase 5: offices,
banks, landlords, doctors. Checking a detail with a rising echo takes a second and catches the
mistakes that cost the most.

It also produces the small back-and-forth that makes a recorded conversation sound real — the kind the
phase 3 gate story will have in its middle.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **echoing the whole sentence**:

> One in the morning, two at night, and don't take it with milk?

One rise at the end of a long line. The listener answers *yes* to all of it, and any single mistake
slips through.

The second is **the echo with no rise**, which sounds like you are just repeating to remember. The
listener says nothing, because nothing was asked.

The third is **a *Really* that falls when you meant to show interest**. *Really.* said flat to somebody's
good news sounds like doubt or boredom.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers echo key details constantly on the phone: *the fourteenth?*, *flat six?*, *you said
two?*. It is so normal that nobody hears it as doubt; they hear it as care.

Under pressure — a queue behind you, a busy counter — people skip the echo to avoid slowing things down.
It costs one second, and the mistake it prevents costs a second trip.

A native listener who hears a clear rising echo almost always adds something: a correction, a reason, a
warning. That extra line is often the most useful thing you heard all day.
