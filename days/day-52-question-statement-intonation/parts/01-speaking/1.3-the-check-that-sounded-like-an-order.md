## One-line answer

Record six real checking questions three ways — rising on the turning word, falling, and rising on
the wrong word — and ask a listener after each *was that a question, and what was I asking about?*,
because a check said with the wrong tune turns into a statement, an order, or a question about
something else.

## The story

At the chemist, you want to check whether you can take the new tablets with your old ones. You say:

> I can take them together.

Your voice falls. The chemist hears you *telling* her, as if you had already decided. She frowns:

> Well — I'd ask your doctor first, actually.

She sounds a little annoyed, because it sounded like you were not asking. You were.

Later, to your brother about collecting the medicine:

> ↗YOU'RE going to the chemist tomorrow?

The rise landed on *you*. He answers the question it sounded like:

> Why not me? I'm free.

You meant *tomorrow?* — you thought it was today. Two checks, two wrong tunes, two answers to questions
you never asked.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **the turning word** — the last loud word, where the tune moves, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-the-tune-turns-on-the-last-loud-word.md).
- **order** — telling somebody what to do.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Three takes of the same checking question:

| Take | The tune | What a listener tends to hear |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | rise on the turning word | a question about that detail |
| B | fall | a statement — sometimes a decision or an order |
| C | rise on an earlier loud word | a question about a different thing |

Take B is the dangerous one with requests and permission: *I can take them together.* falling sounds
like you are telling them. Take C is the dangerous one with details: the listener answers a question you
did not mean.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every phase 5 conversation — the bank, the landlord, the office — is full of checks. A check that comes
out as a statement sounds pushy; one that comes out as the wrong question gets the wrong answer. This
part lets you hear both on purpose, once, so you hear them when they happen by accident.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Intonation (linguistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_(linguistics)): "a falling pitch
movement is associated with statements, but a rising pitch turns a statement into a yes–no question",
and the main movement falls on the nucleus, "usually in the last lexical word".

**Step one — choose six real checking questions** from your life, statement-shaped, four or more words
each, and mark the turning word in each.

**Step two — record takes A, B and C** for each of the six, eighteen lines in all. Record them in a
shuffled order and keep the order hidden.

**Step three — the listener test.** Play them to a listener who has not seen your list. After each, ask:

> Was that a question? If yes, what was I asking about?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when fewer than five of your six A takes are
heard as questions about the right detail. It is **GREEN** when at least five of the six A takes are
heard correctly, and the B takes are mostly heard as statements.

If it went RED, the rise in your A takes is too small or starts too late. Say each A take again with
the rise starting on the stressed part of the turning word and going higher than feels natural, and
play them to a second listener.

**Step five — the tone drill.** For two of your sentences, say take B aloud and then say what it would
mean to a stranger — *you've already decided*, *you're telling me*. Hearing yourself name it is what
makes you notice it later.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **takes that sound the same**. If your listener cannot
tell A from B, the problem is almost always a rise that is too small. Exaggerate it.

The second is **a listener who reads your face**. Play the recording, and look away, or send it.

The third, in real speech, is **a permission question said falling** — *I can leave early today.* —
which sounds like you have already decided. It is the one most likely to offend a manager or an older
relative.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A native listener rarely thinks *that was the wrong tune*. They think *that was pushy* or they answer
the question they heard. The tune is invisible to them; its meaning is not.

Under pressure, a falling tune is the default for many learners, because it is how the sentence looks
on a page. If a check really matters, use a question word or *is it?* as well: *I can take them
together, can I?*, *Is it tomorrow?*. The words carry it even if the tune slips.

A clear rise on the right word makes you sound careful, not uncertain. Fluent speakers use it to check
the most important thing in a conversation, and nobody minds.
