## One-line answer

To compare two things, a short adjective takes *-er* — *bigger*, *cheaper* — and a long one takes
*more* in front — *more expensive* — never both, and *than* comes before the second thing: *The first
flat is bigger than the second.*

## The story

You and your sister are looking for a flat to rent. You have seen two. You message her:

> The first one is more big and more cheap then the second. But the second is more near to the station.

She understands, and writes back:

> OK so first one bigger and cheaper, second one closer. Which is quieter?

She has fixed every comparison in her reply without saying so. You notice she wrote *bigger*, not *more
big*, and *than* where you wrote *then*. Your message worked, and it read as a first draft.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **adjective** — a word that tells you more about a noun: *big*, *cheap*, *quiet*, from
  [day 28](../../../day-28-at-the-doctor/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-an-adjective-does.md).
- **syllable** — one beat of a word: *cheap* has one, *qui-et* has two, *ex-pen-sive* has three.
- **compare** — say how two things are different.
- **comparative** — the form of an adjective used to compare two things: *bigger*, *more expensive*.
- ***than*** — the word that comes before the second thing: *bigger than the second*.

There are two ways to make a comparative, and the length of the adjective decides which.

| Adjective | How | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| short — one syllable | add *-er* | *cheap* → *cheaper* |
| long — three or more syllables | *more* in front | *expensive* → *more expensive* |
| two syllables | usually *more*; some take either, and words ending in *-y* take *-ier* | *careful* → *more careful*; *happy* → *happier* |

**One or the other, never both.** *More cheaper* has two comparatives in it.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3 is about telling what happened and **comparing**. Tomorrow's writing part builds the
superlative — *the biggest*, *the most expensive* — straight on top of these forms, and the phase 3 gate
page will almost certainly say *it was worse than I expected* or *the second time was easier*. And every
choice people write about — flats, jobs, schools, doctors — runs on these forms.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Comparative and superlative adjectives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/comparative-superlative-adjectives).
It gives the rules below, and the model sentence:

> She is two years older **than** me.

**Short adjectives, one syllable: add *-er*.** The page gives *old* → *older*, *long* → *longer*, and
three spelling changes:

| The word ends in | What happens | Page examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *-e* | add only *-r* | *nice* → *nicer* |
| one vowel + one consonant | double the consonant | *big* → *bigger*, *fat* → *fatter* |
| consonant + *-y* | change *-y* to *-i*, add *-er* | *happy* → *happier* |

**Long adjectives: *more* in front.** The page gives *careful* → *more careful*, *interesting* → *more
interesting*.

**The two-syllable words that can go either way.** The page lists *common, cruel, gentle, handsome,
likely, narrow, pleasant, polite, simple, stupid* as taking either form: *simpler* or *more simple*.
Both are right.

**The three irregular ones**, which you must learn as words:

| Adjective | Comparative |
| --- | --- |
| good | better |
| bad | worse |
| far | farther or further |

**Than, and where it goes.** The comparative, then *than*, then the second thing:

```text
   The first flat   is   bigger          than   the second.
   subject          be   comparative     than   the other thing
```

**Than and then look alike and do different jobs.** *Than* compares: *bigger than*. *Then* is about
time: *first the kitchen, then the bedroom*. On the page, one letter separates them.

**Leaving out the second thing** is fine when the reader knows it:

> The second one is closer to the station, but the first one is quieter.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the double comparative**:

> more bigger · more cheaper · more better

One of them is enough: *bigger*, *cheaper*, *better*.

The second is ***more* with a short word**:

> more big · more cheap · more near

The page's rule is *-er* for one syllable: *bigger*, *cheaper*, *nearer*.

The third is ***then* for *than***:

> The first one is bigger then the second.

The fourth is **the regular form of an irregular word**:

> gooder · badder · more good

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Comparisons are everywhere in practical writing: messages about choices, reviews, complaints (*the new
room was worse than the first one*). A reader who needs to decide something reads them closely.

Under pressure, *more* goes in front of everything, because it is one rule instead of three. It is
understood, and it looks like a first draft. *Then* for *than* is the error even fluent writers make
when typing fast.

A native reader passes over *more big* in a message. In a formal complaint or a report, *more cheaper*
and *then* make the reader stop, and a reader who stops trusts the facts a little less.
