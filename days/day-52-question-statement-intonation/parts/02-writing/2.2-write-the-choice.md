## One-line answer

Write a real choice between two things as one short message — each point a comparison, the points
balanced between the two, and your choice at the end — and give it to the person who has to decide,
because a comparison message is good when they can choose from it without asking you anything.

## The story

Your sister cannot come to see the flats. She asks you to write it all down so she can decide tonight.

You write:

> Flat A is bigger than flat B and it's cheaper — about two thousand less a month. But B is much closer
> to the station, so my journey would be shorter. A is on a busier road, so it's noisier at night. B is
> newer, but the kitchen is smaller. I think A is better for us, because the rent matters more than the
> journey.

She replies in one line: *Agree. A.* She did not need to call you. Every point was a comparison she
could weigh, and the last line told her what you thought and why.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **comparative** — *bigger*, *more expensive*: the form for comparing two things, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-bigger-cheaper-more-expensive.md).
- ***than*** — the word before the second thing, from the same part.
- **point** — one way two things differ: price, size, distance.
- **balanced** — each side gets its good points, not only one.
- **verdict** — your choice at the end, with one reason.

A comparison message has three pieces:

| Piece | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the points | one difference per sentence, as a comparison | *A is bigger than B.* |
| the balance | at least one point in favour of each | *But B is much closer to the station.* |
| the verdict | which one, and the reason that decided it | *I think A is better, because the rent matters more.* |

**Much** makes a difference bigger: *much closer*, *much more expensive*. **A bit** makes it smaller: *a
bit noisier*. Both go in front of the comparative.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate page will compare, even briefly — before and after, then and now. And phase 4 is built
on this exact shape: points on each side, then a verdict with a reason. This message is a small version
of a phase 4 paragraph.

## The rule

> Could you choose from this? Is there anything you'd need to ask me?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **describing each thing separately** instead of comparing:

> Flat A is big and cheap. It is on a busy road. Flat B is small and new. It is near the station.

Every fact is there, and the reader has to do the comparing. One comparison per sentence does it for
them.

The second is **the one-sided message**: five points all for A. The reader wonders why B was ever
considered.

The third is **the verdict with no reason**: *I like A.* The reader cannot agree or disagree with a
reason they have not been given.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

People who write good comparison messages — about quotes from two builders, two hospitals, two schools —
put every point as a comparison and end with *I'd go with …, because …*. The reader can reply in one
word.

Under pressure writers list facts about each option in turn, because that is the order they saw them.
Turning the list into comparisons takes one pass and saves the phone call.

A formal reader — a manager choosing between two suppliers — expects exactly this shape, and reads the
verdict first. If your reason is clear, they will usually follow it.
