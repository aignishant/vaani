## One-line answer

Plant the four common comparative errors in your real message on purpose — the double comparative,
*more* with a short word, *then* for *than*, and a regular form of an irregular word — and find out
which ones a reader notices and whether any of them changed what the reader understood.

## The story

Your first draft about the two flats, before you fixed it, went to your mother instead of your sister:

> Flat A is more bigger and more cheap then flat B. B is more near to the station. A is more noisy at
> night but the kitchen is gooder.

Your mother calls:

> I understood. A is bigger and cheaper, B is closer. But — *then* flat B? Then what happened with
> flat B?

She understood every comparison except one. *Then* made her think a second event was coming. The other
five errors looked odd to her and changed nothing; one small letter changed the sentence into a story.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **plant** — put a mistake somewhere on purpose to see if it is found.
- **comparative** — the form for comparing two things, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-bigger-cheaper-more-expensive.md).
- **irregular** — a word that does not follow the rule: *good → better*.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Four errors, and what each tends to do:

| Error | Example | Does it change the meaning? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| double comparative | *more bigger* | no — looks unfinished |
| *more* with a short word | *more cheap* | no — looks unusual |
| *then* for *than* | *bigger then B* | **sometimes** — *then* can read as time |
| regular form of an irregular word | *gooder*, *more good*, *badder* | no — looks like a beginner |

Most of these are harmless to meaning and costly to trust. One of them can change the meaning.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate page will be read by somebody who has to follow what happened. *Then* where *than*
belongs is the comparative error that can do that damage, and you will not find it by looking for what
looks wrong, because *then* is a correct word.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Comparative and superlative adjectives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/comparative-superlative-adjectives):
one-syllable adjectives take *-er*; long ones take *more*; *good → better*, *bad → worse*, *far →
farther/further*; and:

> She is two years older **than** me.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a *then* that cannot be misread**: *bigger then B.* in
a sentence that obviously compares. Put it where a reader could expect a next event, as in the story.

The second is **fixing by rewriting**. Change only the comparative; if you rewrite the sentence, you lose
sight of the one error you are learning to see.

The third, in real writing, is ***more* in front of an *-er* word the writer already fixed**: *more
cheaper* after a quick edit from *more cheap*. Read every edited line twice.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers type *then* for *than* when they are fast, and so do many native speakers. It is the
error that most often survives into a sent message, because a spell checker sees two real words.

Under pressure the double comparative appears when writers are unsure, adding *more* to make sure.
Trust the short form: one *-er* is enough.

A native reader forgives *more cheap*. They notice *gooder* immediately and read the rest more carefully,
looking for other slips. In a complaint or an application, that extra care is not on your side.
