# Going round the word instead of waiting for it

`FL-08` · Day 52

## The one-line answer

When the word will not come, you do not stop and hunt for it — you describe the thing out loud,
in the same breath, and let the other person hand you the word.

## The model

Think of a guessing game round a table. Somebody has a word they are not allowed to say, so they
describe it — *it's the thing you use for opening bottles*, *it's like a spoon but flat* — and
across the table somebody shouts the answer. Nobody in that game has ever sat in silence waiting
for a word to arrive. The whole point of the round is that you keep talking and let the table do
the naming.

That game is today, with one enormous advantage: **you are allowed to talk.** In the actual game
you often have to mime it. In a conversation you have every other word in English available to
you, and you only need one thing named. It is the easiest version of the round, and it is the one
people freeze in.

**This is not a vocabulary problem, and today does not add a single noun to your English.** The
word you lost is usually a word you know. It turns up later, on its own, in the shower. What
you are missing is not the word — it is the manoeuvre that carries the sentence past the hole
while your mouth is still moving.

Day 9 gave you the moves that hold the turn while a whole thought assembles. Today is narrower and
sharper than that: the thought is fine, the sentence is already running, and exactly one word has
gone. Reaching for a hesitation move here is a small waste, because you do not need to buy time.
You need to go round.

**Four routes round, and you only ever need one.**

**By what it does.** The strongest one, and the one that works for almost every object.

> It's the thing you use for getting the lid off.

**By what job it is like.** Not what it looks like — what it does that something else also does.

> It does the same job as a hammer, but smaller.

**By what it is not.** Fast, and very good for ideas rather than objects.

> It's the opposite of a discount, basically.

**By where you'd find it.** Cheap, and often enough on its own.

> You'd find one in a kitchen drawer, usually at the back.

Say all four out loud now. Notice that not one of them contains a hard word. The escape hatch is
built out of the most ordinary English you own, which is precisely why it is always available,
including on the days when nothing else is.

**Then hand it over.** This is the half people leave out. You describe, and then you go quiet and
look hopeful, and the other person is not sure whether they are allowed to help. Ask. Out loud.

> What's it called? Help me out.

That question does two things at once. It gets you the word, and it tells the other person that
this is a shared job rather than a performance they are watching you fail.

**And when they give it to you, take it and keep going.** Do not thank them elaborately. Do not
go back and say the whole sentence again with the word in it. That is the move that makes the gap
sound like a gap. You say *that's the one, yeah* and you carry straight on from where you were, as
if the word had simply arrived a moment late — which, from the outside, is exactly what it sounds
like.

**The one rule with a sound attached: the sentence does not stop.** Not slower, not quieter,
not trailing off. You go from *I need a — * straight into *the thing you use for* with no seam.
That seam is the entire difference between a fluent speaker missing a word and a struggling
speaker missing a word, and it is audible from the next room.

**Today's phrases** — the four routes, the handover, and the two words that get you moving again.
All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| It's the thing you use for …ing | Going round an object, by what it does |
| It does the same job as a … | Going round it by comparing what it is for, not what it looks like |
| It's the opposite of … | Going round an idea or a quality rather than an object |
| You'd find one in a … | Going round it by where it lives |
| What's the one where …? | The word is missing from a whole situation, not an object |
| Help me out — what's it called? | Handing the guess to the other person, out loud |
| You know the ones? | Checking mid-description that they are still with you |
| That's the one, yeah. | They gave you the word and you are taking it without ceremony |
| Whatever it's called, … | Nobody could name it, and you are carrying on regardless |

## Say it now

Pick one real object near you whose English name you are not sure of, and one idea you find hard
to name. Run these on both, filling the slots with them.

1. *I need the — it's the thing you use for {{what it does}}.*
2. *It does the same job as {{a thing you can name}}, but {{the difference}}.*
3. *It's the opposite of {{the easier word}}, more or less.*
4. *You'd find one in {{where it lives}}, usually {{whereabouts}}.*
5. *What's the one where {{the situation}}?*
6. *Help me out — what's it called?*
7. *{{Your description}} — you know the ones?*
8. *That's the one, yeah — so anyway, {{carry on}}.*
9. *Whatever it's called, {{the point you were making}}.*

Now run lines 1 to 4 again as a single unbroken stretch about one object, starting with *I need
the —* and never stopping, moving from route to route until you run out of routes. You are
deliberately over-describing. What you are training is not the description; it is the absence of a
gap between the routes.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d52-free.m4a`.**

> Describe **five things whose English names you do not know**, one after another, without ever
> naming any of them and without stopping between them. Anything at all — a kitchen object, a part
> of a vehicle, a feeling, a job somebody in your family does, something on the street outside.
> The recorder does not stop between the five.

- **Solo form:** as written. At the end, and only at the end, look up nothing — just say which of
  the five you would now bet you could get a stranger to name for you.
- **With a partner:** they shout out a guess whenever they have one, and you say *that's the one*
  or you keep describing. Count how many of the five they landed. They will land more than you
  expect, and from thinner descriptions than you expect.

## The deliberate failure

Do a second short recording on the same five things with one rule: **when the word does not come,
stop and go and get it.** Search your memory in silence. Translate from your first language in your
head. Do not say anything until you have it.

**What breaking sounds like:** the silence is long and it is dead, and then the recovery is worse
than the silence — you restart the whole sentence from the beginning, so the listener hears the
same opening twice. That double opening is the tell. It is the sound of somebody who left the
conversation to go and look for something.

Now play the first recording straight after it, and listen for the moments where you went round a
word. Here is the uncomfortable bit. **You cannot hear them.** A description slid into a moving
sentence sounds exactly like ordinary talking, because ordinary talking is full of descriptions.
Nobody listening to that tape could tell you which five words were missing. The hole was never the
problem. Stopping was the problem, and stopping is the only part anybody can hear.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are at a counter buying a part for something, and you cannot name the part. You know exactly
what it is. You could draw it. But the noun is gone, so you go quiet, and the person behind the
counter waits, and then helps in the only way available to them — by asking a question you also
cannot answer. You leave with the wrong thing, or with nothing, and the story you tell yourself
afterwards is that your English is not good enough for shops.

**The repair:**

> It's the thing you use for holding the pipe against the wall — you'd find them in the plumbing
> aisle. Help me out, what's it called?

The person behind that counter names objects all day. They are the best guesser you will meet this
week, and the description you were embarrassed by is more than they need.
