## One-line answer

When somebody asks you to compare two things you know, give them the same three points for both, one
point at a time — *this one's cheaper, but that one's closer* — and finish with a verdict that depends on
them: *if you want X, go to A; if you want Y, go to B*.

## The story

A new neighbour stops you on the stairs. There are two markets near your building, and she asks which
one is better.

The first answer:

> The Monday market is very big. It has everything. Many shops. It's a bit far. The station market is
> small. It's near. It's okay.

She thanks you and looks as unsure as before. You described two markets. She still has to compare them
herself.

The second answer:

> The Monday market's cheaper, especially for vegetables, but it's further — you'd need an auto. The
> station market's closer and the fish is fresher, but it's more expensive. So — for a big weekly shop,
> the Monday market. For something quick after work, the station one.

She says, *Perfect, I'll do the big shop on Monday.* Same markets, same facts. The second time you
compared them, and then told her how to choose.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **compare** — say how two things are different, or the same.
- **point** — one way two things can differ: price, distance, quality.
- **verdict** — your answer at the end: which one, and when.
- **comparative** — *cheaper*, *closer*, *more expensive*: the form for comparing two things.
- **the three moves** — what it's about, what you thought, who it's for, from
  [day 50](../../../day-50-film-show-or-book/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-its-about-and-what-you-thought.md).

On day 50 you ended a recommendation with *who it's for*. A spoken comparison ends the same way, with
a verdict that depends on the listener.

The difference from a description is simple. A description talks about one thing, then the other. A
comparison talks about **one point at a time**, for both things together.

| Description | Comparison |
| --- | --- |
| *A is big. A is far. B is small. B is near.* | *A is bigger, but B is closer.* |
| the listener compares | you compare for them |

Three points is usually right. One is too few to be fair; five is too many to hold in the ear.

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 3's theme includes comparing, and this is the spoken version: two places, two jobs, two schools,
two doctors. People ask you to compare the things you know all the time, because you know them and
they do not.

In phase 4 this becomes arguing for and against, and its gate asks for reasons on both sides. A spoken
comparison with a fair verdict is the first step.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Comparative and superlative adjectives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/comparative-superlative-adjectives):
one-syllable adjectives take *-er*, longer ones take *more*, and *good → better*, *bad → worse*, *far →
farther/further*, with:

> She is two years older **than** me.

**Choose three points** that matter to the listener, not to you. For a market: price, distance, quality.
For a job: pay, hours, the people.

**Say each point as one comparison, with *but* when it turns:**

> The Monday market's cheaper, but it's further.
> The station one's closer, and the fish is fresher.

In speech, the second thing is often left out because the listener already knows it: *cheaper* means
*cheaper than the other one*.

**Keep the points balanced.** At least one point for each side, or the listener will wonder why the
other one exists.

**The verdict, tied to the listener:**

> For a big weekly shop, the Monday market. For something quick, the station one.
> If money matters most, go for A. If time matters most, B.
> Honestly, for you, I'd say B.

**The link words that carry a spoken comparison:**

| Word | Job | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *but* | turns to the other side | *cheaper, but further* |
| *and* | adds a point on the same side | *closer, and fresher* |
| *so* | leads to the verdict | *so for a big shop, A* |
| *both* | says what is the same | *both are open on Sundays* |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **two descriptions in a row**, as in the story. Each thing gets its own
list, and the listener has to line them up in their head.

The second is **a verdict with no condition**:

> The Monday market is better.

Better for what? It sounds like your taste, not advice.

The third is **too many points**:

> It's cheaper, bigger, further, older, busier, dirtier, open later …

By point four, the listener has lost point one.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers compare in pairs, fast: *cheaper but further*, *nicer but pricier*. And they nearly
always end with *depends what you want* and then say what it depends on. That ending is what makes the
listener trust them.

Under pressure, in a second language, speakers describe one thing fully and then the other, because
that is how they remember them. The listener has to do the work.

A native listener notices fairness. A comparison with one honest point on the losing side sounds like
advice; one where everything is on the same side sounds like a sales pitch.
