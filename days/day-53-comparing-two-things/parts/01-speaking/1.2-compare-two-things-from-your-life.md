## One-line answer

Compare two real things for two real people who want different things, and change the points and the
verdict for each — because a comparison is not about the two things, it is about what the listener
needs to decide.

## The story

A week later, two more people ask about the markets. One is a young man who has just started work and
has no time. The other is a retired woman who cooks for a big family.

For him:

> The station market's closer — you can go on your way home. It's more expensive, but you'll save an hour
> on Sunday. For you, the station one.

For her:

> The Monday market's much cheaper, and the vegetables are fresher in the morning. It's further, but they
> deliver if you buy a lot. For you, definitely Monday.

The same two markets, two different sets of points, two different verdicts. Both were honest. Each one
was about the person listening.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **point** — one way two things differ, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-two-things-three-points-one-verdict.md).
- **verdict** — which one, and when, from the same part.
- **priority** — the thing that matters most to somebody.
- **tailor** — change something to fit one person.

Before you compare, ask what the listener cares about. Then choose points that touch it, and make the
verdict answer it.

| Listener's priority | Points to lead with | Verdict shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| money | price, deals, what lasts longer | *If money matters most, …* |
| time | distance, queues, opening hours | *For you, with no time, …* |
| quality | freshness, service, reliability | *If you want the best, …* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a story told to a stranger, and the phase 4 gate is an opinion with reasons and a
counter-argument answered. Both need you to think about the listener while you speak. Tailoring a
comparison is the smallest version of that, and it is something you can do today with real people.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the same comparison for everyone**, led by the point that matters to
you. The listener who cares about something else has to wait for it or ask.

The second is **skipping the priority question** and guessing wrong: telling a busy person about prices,
or a careful shopper about speed.

The third is **a verdict that contradicts the points**: all three points favour A, and the verdict is B,
because that is what you use. Say why: *I use B, but for you, A.*

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers ask *what are you looking for?* before they compare, almost without noticing. It turns a
list of facts into advice.

Under pressure — a quick question in a corridor — they skip the question and give their own preference,
with a condition: *I like A, but if you're in a hurry, B*. That condition is the whole skill in one
line.

A native listener feels looked after when a comparison is tailored. It is one of the easiest ways to be
helpful in a language you are still learning.
