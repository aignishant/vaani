## One-line answer

Answer *which is better?* on purpose by describing the first thing fully and then the second, with no
comparison and no verdict, then ask the listener which one they would choose and why — because two
descriptions give them all the facts and none of the help.

## The story

A cousin visiting from another city asks which of two hospitals near you is better for a check-up.

> The city hospital is very big. It has many departments. It is government, so it is cheap. There is
> always a long line. The doctors are good. The private clinic is small. It is new. It is expensive.
> There is no line. The doctor is young.

Your cousin says:

> So … which one?

Ten facts, all true. None of them was compared, and there was no verdict. Your cousin now has to hold
both hospitals in their head and do the comparing themselves — which is exactly what they asked you to do.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one
we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **description** — talking about one thing at a time.
- **comparison** — talking about one point at a time, for both things, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-two-things-three-points-one-verdict.md).
- **verdict** — which one, and when, from the same part.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will answer the same *which is better?* about the same real pair twice.

- **The RED take — two descriptions.** Everything about A, then everything about B. No *-er*, no *more*,
  no *but*, no verdict.
- **The GREEN take — a comparison.** Three points, one comparison each, and a verdict tied to the listener.

Then you ask each listener: *which would you choose, and why?*

## Why Vaani needs it

Two descriptions in a row is the version that comes out by itself, because it is how things sit in memory.
Hearing a listener say *so … which one?* once is the best way to notice it next time. And phase 4's gate —
an opinion with a counter-argument answered — cannot be built from two descriptions.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Comparative and superlative adjectives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/comparative-superlative-adjectives) —
the forms that the RED take leaves out on purpose: *older than*, *bigger*, *more expensive*.

**Use one real pair for both takes**, one you compared in
[part 1.2](1.2-compare-two-things-from-your-life.md) or a new one.

**Build the RED take properly.** At least four facts about A, then at least four about B. Use only *is*,
*has* and *there is*. No comparatives, no *but*, no verdict.

**Give each take to a different listener** who does not know the two things. After each, ask:

> Which one would you choose, and why?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the RED listener cannot choose, asks you

chooses and gives one of your three points as the reason.

**Then the conversion drill.** Take the RED facts in pairs — one about A, one about B on the same point —
and join each pair into one comparison:

> The city hospital is cheap. The private clinic is expensive.
> → The city hospital's much cheaper.

Record four such pairs and their comparisons.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take that slips in a comparison** — *but the clinic
is quicker*. Once one is in, the listener has a handle. Keep it pure.

The second is **facts about different points** for A and B: A's price, B's age. Then the conversion drill
has nothing to pair. Choose facts on the same points for both.

The third, in real speech, is **the comparison that stops before the verdict**: three good points and
then silence. The listener still says *so which one?*. Finish with *so for you, …*.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A native listener asked to choose from two descriptions will usually choose on the last fact they heard,
because it is the freshest. That means a RED answer decides for them by accident.

Under pressure — a relative on the phone, a hurried question — the RED version is quicker to start and
slower to finish. The GREEN version needs one second to choose three points, and then it is done.

What a listener remembers from a good comparison is the verdict and one reason. Make those two things
the clearest part of what you say.
