## One-line answer

To pick one thing out of a whole group, a short adjective takes *the* and *-est* — *the cheapest* — and a
long one takes *the most* — *the most expensive* — and you usually say which group with *in*: *the
cheapest shop in the market*.

## The story

Your cousin is staying in your flat for a week while you are away. You leave a note on the fridge:

> The shop downstairs is cheap. The chemist on the corner is near. The tea stall by the gate is good.

Your cousin reads it and goes to the shop downstairs for everything, all week. On the last day a
neighbour tells her there are three other shops on the same road, and the one downstairs is the most
expensive. *Cheap* only meant *not bad*.

What you meant was:

> The cheapest shop on this road is the one next to the temple. The nearest chemist is on the corner.
> The best tea in the area is at the stall by the gate.

Now each sentence picks one out of a group, and your cousin knows she does not need to look further.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **comparative** — *cheaper*, *more expensive*: for comparing two things, from
  [day 52](../../../day-52-question-statement-intonation/parts/02-writing/2.1-bigger-cheaper-more-expensive.md).
- **superlative** — *the cheapest*, *the most expensive*: for picking one out of a whole group.
- **syllable** — one beat of a word: *cheap* has one, *ex-pen-sive* has three, from the same part.
- **group** — all the things you are choosing from: the shops on the road, the people in your family.

On day 52 you compared **two** things. A superlative compares **one against all the rest**.

| You compare | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| two things | comparative | *This shop is cheaper than that one.* |
| one thing against a whole group | superlative | *This is the cheapest shop on the road.* |

The same length rule decides the form: short adjectives take an ending, long ones take a word in front.
The superlative just uses *-est* and *most* where the comparative used *-er* and *more*, and it almost
always has *the*.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story from your life, and stories reach for superlatives at their
biggest moments: *the worst day of the year*, *the hardest thing I did*. They are also how people give
useful local advice in writing: *the nearest*, *the cheapest*, *the best*.

## The rule

**Two pages were checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Comparative and superlative adjectives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/comparative-superlative-adjectives)
gives *old → oldest*, *long → longest*, *nice → nicest*, *big → biggest*, *happy → happiest*, *careful →
most careful*, *interesting → most interesting*, *good → best*, *bad → worst*, *far → farthest/furthest*,
and says superlatives are usually preceded by *the*:

> It was **the happiest day** of my life.

[Comparative and superlative adjectives](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/comparative-superlative-adjectives)
on the teens site gives:

> She's the **tallest** in the group.
> It was the **best** pizza I've ever had!

**Short adjectives: *the* + *-est*.** The same three spelling changes as the comparative:

| The word ends in | What happens | Page examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *-e* | add only *-st* | *nice* → *the nicest* |
| one vowel + one consonant | double the consonant | *big* → *the biggest* |
| consonant + *-y* | *-y* becomes *-i*, add *-est* | *happy* → *the happiest* |

**Long adjectives: *the most*.** *The most careful*, *the most interesting*, *the most expensive*.

**The three irregular ones:**

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| good | better | the best |
| bad | worse | the worst |
| far | farther / further | the farthest / the furthest |

**Say which group.** The teens page does it with *in*: *the tallest in the group*. The same works for a
place:

> the cheapest shop in the market · the tallest building in our town

The first page's example does it with *of*: *the happiest day of my life*.

**The shape on the page:**

```text
   The tea stall by the gate   is   the best          tea   in the area.
   the thing                   be   the + superlative  (noun) the group
```

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the double superlative**:

> the most cheapest · the most best

One of them is enough: *the cheapest*, *the best*.

The second is **the missing *the***:

> It is cheapest shop. · She is tallest in family.

The reader understands, and the sentence looks unfinished.

The third is **the comparative where the superlative belongs**:

> This is the cheaper shop on the road.

With a whole road of shops, *the cheapest*. *The cheaper* is for two.

The fourth is **the positive form where you meant the superlative**, as in the story: *the shop downstairs
is cheap*. It is correct English and it says much less.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Superlatives are everywhere in practical notes and messages: *the nearest bus stop*, *the quickest way*,
*the best time to go*. A reader acts on them, because a superlative says *you do not need to look further*.

Under pressure writers use *very* instead: *the shop is very cheap*. It sounds strong and it does not
tell the reader whether there is a cheaper one.

A formal reader expects superlatives to be true. *The cheapest quote we received* can be checked. Use
them when you mean them, and the reader will trust the rest of what you write.
