## One-line answer

Write a real guide note for somebody staying in your area — the best, the nearest, the cheapest and one
honest *the worst* — with every superlative true and its group named, and let the reader tell you which
lines they would actually act on.

## The story

Your cousin comes back a year later, this time with her husband, and asks for the fridge note again, but
better. You write:

> The nearest chemist is on the corner, and it's open until ten. The cheapest vegetables in the area are
> at the Monday market. The best tea is at the stall by the gate. The quickest way to the station is
> through the park, not along the road. The worst time to take an auto is after six, when the traffic is
> terrible.

Her husband reads it and says, *We'll do exactly this.* Every line picked one thing out of a group he
did not know, and told him where to stop looking.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **superlative** — *the cheapest*, *the most expensive*, *the best*: one out of a whole group, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-the-est-and-the-most.md).
- **group** — all the things you are choosing from.
- **guide note** — a short note that helps somebody find their way around a place.
- **true** — a superlative you could defend if somebody checked.

A guide note is almost entirely superlatives, because a visitor wants to know which one out of many.

| Kind of line | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *the nearest / the closest* | saves time | *The nearest chemist is on the corner.* |
| *the cheapest / the best* | saves money or disappointment | *The best tea is at the stall by the gate.* |
| *the quickest / the easiest* | saves effort | *The quickest way to the station is through the park.* |
| *the worst* | a warning | *The worst time for an auto is after six.* |

The warning line is the one people forget, and it is often the most useful.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story, and a story that says *it was the worst day* or *the kindest thing
anyone did* needs the superlative to be exact. A guide note is the simplest place to practise making each
one true and specific.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the superlative with no group**:

> The cheapest is the Monday market.

The cheapest what? Vegetables, clothes, everything? Name it.

The second is **every line *the best***: the best shop, the best chemist, the best tea. The reader cannot
tell what makes each the best. Use the exact word — *nearest*, *cheapest*, *quickest* — for the thing that
matters.

The third is **a superlative that is not true**, used for warmth: *the best chemist in the city*. The
reader who checks will stop trusting the note.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Good local advice — from a neighbour, a host, a colleague — is almost always a set of superlatives: *the
nearest*, *the quickest*, *the one place to avoid*. People act on each line without checking, which is
exactly why each one has to be true.

Under pressure people write *very good* and *very near*. The reader then has to wonder if there is
something better, and often goes looking.

A native reader notices when a superlative is exaggerated, and discounts all the others. One honest
*the worst* makes all the *the bests* more believable.
