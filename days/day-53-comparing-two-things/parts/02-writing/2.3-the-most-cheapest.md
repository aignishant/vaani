## One-line answer

Plant four superlative errors in your real guide note on purpose — the double superlative, the missing
*the*, the comparative for a whole group, and the missing group — and find out which ones a reader
notices and which ones change what they would do.

## The story

The first draft of the guide note, which you nearly left on the fridge:

> The most cheapest vegetables are at Monday market. Nearest chemist is on corner. The tea stall by the
> gate is the better. The quickest is through the park.

Your cousin's husband reads it over your shoulder:

> *The better* — better than what? And the quickest what — the quickest to the station, or to the market?

He understood the first two lines and noticed they looked odd. The last two he could not act on. *The
better* compared the tea stall with nothing, and *the quickest* had lost its group.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **plant** — put a mistake somewhere on purpose to see if it is found.
- **superlative** — one out of a whole group, from [part 2.1](2.1-the-est-and-the-most.md).
- **group** — all the things you are choosing from.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Error | Example | What a reader tends to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| double superlative | *the most cheapest* | notices; understands |
| missing *the* | *Nearest chemist is on corner.* | may not notice in a note; understands |
| comparative for a group | *the tea stall is the better* | stops: *better than what?* |
| missing group | *The quickest is through the park.* | stops: *the quickest what?* |

The first two look wrong and work. The last two can look fine to the writer and stop the reader.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate page will be read by a stranger. A superlative that has lost its group — *it was the
worst* — leaves that stranger asking *the worst what?* at the most important sentence of the story.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **fixing the missing group with a guess**. Write the group
you actually meant; if you are not sure what it was, the line was not ready.

The second is **a reader who knows the area**. They will fill in the missing groups from their own
knowledge.

The third, in real writing, is ***one of the best* with a singular noun**: *one of the best shop*. That is a
separate rule this part does not teach; if you use *one of the*, check it against a grammar reference first.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Native readers pass over *the most cheapest* in a message with a small smile, and act on it. They cannot act
on *the quickest is through the park* without asking, and a note is exactly the thing they cannot ask.

Under pressure writers leave out the group because it is obvious to them. It is not obvious to somebody
new. The group is the part a stranger needs most.

A formal reader — a report, a review, a complaint — reads a superlative as a claim. *The worst service we
have received* is a strong claim and needs its group; *the worst* on its own sounds like a temper.
