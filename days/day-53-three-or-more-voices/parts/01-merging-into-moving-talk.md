# Merging into moving talk

`IN-12` · Day 53

## The one-line answer

With two people the turn is handed to you; with three or more nobody hands you anything, so you
join the way you join moving traffic — you match the speed, you go on a gap that is smaller than
you would like, and you commit.

## The model

Everything you have done in this phase so far has had one other person in it. That conversation is
generous. They ask you something and they stop. They look at you. The turn arrives at your feet
like a parcel, and your only job is to pick it up.

Add one more person and the physics change completely. Nobody is waiting for you now, because
there is always somebody else available to talk. The talk is a road with cars already on it, and
it does not slow down because you are standing at the junction with your indicator on.

**Three things change, and all three are audible.**

**The gaps get shorter.** In a two-way conversation the pause between turns is roomy — the other
person has nowhere else to give the turn, so they will wait. In a group, that same pause is being
watched by two other people who also have something to say. It closes almost as soon as it opens.
If you are waiting for the gap you are used to, you are waiting for one that is not coming.

**So you come in on the tail, not in the silence.** This is the single most important thing today
and the one that feels wrong. You start speaking while the other person is finishing their last
few words — overlapping them slightly, at the point where their sentence is clearly landing and
everybody can hear where it is going. That is not rudeness; it is how every group conversation in
English actually works, and if you listen to one with this in mind you will hear it happening
constantly. Waiting for full silence is like waiting for an empty road. The road is not going to
empty.

**And the first word has to arrive at full volume.** Not gradually. If you start quietly, testing
whether you are allowed, somebody else's first word will be louder than yours and the group will
follow theirs — not because they were ruder, but because a group follows whichever sound arrived
whole. Your own tape will show you this. You can hear a first syllable get driven over, and it
always sounds the same: it goes up in pitch, it goes down in volume, and it stops.

**Getting in is half of it. Staying in is the other half**, and it is where a group turn is
genuinely harder than a two-way one. In a two-way conversation you are holding your turn against
one person. In a group you are holding it against everybody else at once, and somebody will move
before you have finished. Two things help, and they are both said out loud at the very start of
your turn.

**Say how many things you have.**

> Sorry, two things —

That single move buys you a turn with a fence round it. Everybody now knows there is a second
thing coming, so the pause between your first and second point is not read as the end.

**And attach yourself to whoever just spoke.**

> Building on that —

A group grants the floor most easily to somebody who is continuing rather than starting. Starting
a new subject in a group is the most expensive thing you can do; continuing is nearly free.

**Being driven over is normal, and the recovery is not what you think.** When somebody talks over
you, you do not go back to the beginning of your sentence — that is a whole new merge and you have
to earn it a second time. You wait for their sentence to land, and then you resume from where you
stopped, as though nothing happened.

> Just before we move on —

**The strongest move in any group is bringing somebody else in.** Say the quiet person's turn
into being, out loud, and something changes: you stop being one of the people trying to talk and
become the person running the conversation. It is also the single easiest turn to take, because
nobody ever talks over a question aimed at somebody else.

**Today's phrases** — the merge, the fence round your turn, and the way back in. All of these go
into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Can I come in on that? | The plain merge, over the tail of somebody's last few words |
| Building on that — | Joining by continuing the previous speaker rather than starting fresh |
| Sorry, two things — | Putting a fence round a turn that needs more than one sentence |
| Can I just land this bit? | Somebody moved while you were mid-point and you want the rest of it |
| Just before we move on — | The group has changed subject and you had not finished |
| That's what I was going to say. | Somebody said your point first, and you claim your half of it |
| Go back a step — what did you mean by …? | Pulling the whole group back to something that went past too fast |
| We haven't heard from you on this. | Bringing in the person who has not spoken — the easiest turn in the room |

## Say it now

Say each line as though the room is already loud — full volume on the very first word, no run-up,
no throat-clearing before it.

1. *Can I come in on that? Because {{your point}}.*
2. *Building on that — {{the thing you'd add}}.*
3. *Sorry, two things — {{the first}}, and {{the second}}.*
4. *Can I just land this bit? {{the rest of your point}}.*
5. *Just before we move on — {{the thing that got left}}.*
6. *That's what I was going to say, actually — {{your version of it}}.*
7. *Go back a step — what did you mean by {{the phrase that went past}}?*
8. *We haven't heard from you on this — {{what you want them to say}}?*

Now put on any recorded conversation with three or more voices in it — a discussion, an interview
panel, a radio programme — and run the same eight lines live, into the real thing. You do not
pause the recording, and you do not wait for a silence. You wait for a sentence that is clearly
landing, and you go on its last two or three words. Do all eight. You will get driven over, out
loud, by people who cannot hear you, and that is the rep.

## The deliberate failure

Same group recording, second short take, one rule: **you may only speak in a real silence.** Wait
for a gap with nothing in it at all. Perfectly polite. Perfectly patient.

**What breaking sounds like:** almost nothing, for a long stretch. Then, when a gap finally opens,
you come in on a subject that has moved on twice since you had the thought, so you have to explain
why you are saying it — *sorry, going back to what you were saying before* — and the explanation
is longer than the point was.

Now the finding, and it is the uncomfortable one. Play that tape to somebody who was not there and
ask them what you thought about the subject. They cannot tell you, because you did not say. **From
outside a group, having nothing to say and having plenty to say but never getting in sound exactly
the same.** Nobody in that room ever knew there was a difference. That is why the polite version
is not actually the polite version — it is the version where your view was never in the room, and
where the group's decision got made without it.

## When it breaks in the wild

Four people, a table or a call, and the subject is one you know more about than anybody there. You
have a point. Twice you draw breath and somebody else goes. The third time you get one word out
and it is driven over, so you stop and let them have it. The conversation reaches a conclusion. On
the way out, in the corridor, you tell one person the thing you had wanted to say and they say
*that's a really good point, you should have said*. You did say. Nobody heard it.

**The repair:**

> Sorry, can I go back a step — I did start to say this earlier. Two things.

Naming that you started earlier is legitimate and it works, because it is true and everybody half
remembers it. And the *two things* fences the turn so that this time you finish it.
