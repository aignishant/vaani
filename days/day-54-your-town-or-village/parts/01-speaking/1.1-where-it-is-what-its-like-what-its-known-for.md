## One-line answer

To talk about your town or village to somebody who has never been there, give it in three moves —
where it is, placed next to somewhere they know; what it's like, with one thing you can see; and what
it's known for, with one honest thing that isn't good — because a listener needs a map before they need
an opinion.

## The story

A new colleague in the canteen asks where you are from. You say the name of your town. They have never
heard of it.

> Oh. Where's that?

> It's a small town. It's very nice. Peaceful. Good people.

They nod politely. They still do not know where it is, how big it is, or anything that makes it
different from every other small town.

You try again:

> It's about two hours south of the city, on the river. Quite small — one main road, and the market
> runs along the river bank. It's known for its sweets — people stop on the highway just to buy them.
> The only problem is the road floods every monsoon.

Now your colleague says, *Oh, I think I've eaten those sweets!* In four sentences they know where it
is, can picture a street, and have something to ask about.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **town** — a place bigger than a village and smaller than a city.
- **village** — a small place, usually in the countryside.
- **anchor** — a place the listener already knows, from
  [day 18](../../../day-18-where-things-are/parts/01-speaking/1.1-there-is-and-the-anchor.md).
- **known for** — famous for, at least locally.
- **honest bad point** — one true thing that is not good about the place.

On day 18 you gave a listener somewhere to stand in a room with an anchor. Today the anchor is a whole
place: a city, a river, a highway they have heard of.

Three moves:

| Move | What it answers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · where it is | *Where is that?* | *about two hours south of the city, on the river* |
| 2 · what it's like | *What would I see?* | *one main road, and the market runs along the river bank* |
| 3 · what it's known for | *Why would I remember it?* | *its sweets* — and one honest bad point |

## Why Vaani needs it

*Where are you from?* is one of the first questions anybody asks you, and *what's it like?* comes
straight after. The phase 3 gate story will happen somewhere, and a listener needs to be able to place
it in two sentences.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Using 'there is' and 'there are'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/using-there-there-are),
which says "We use *there is* to say that something exists or is in a place", with:

> **There is a bridge** in the park.
> There's a café, a supermarket and a bus stop on my street.

**Move one — where it is, from something they know.** A big city, a river, a highway, a famous place:

> It's about two hours south of the city.
> It's on the main road to the coast.
> It's near the border, in the hills.

The distance is often said as time, *two hours from*, because that is how people travel.

**Move two — what it's like: size, and one thing you would see.** Use *there's* and *there are*:

> It's quite small. There's one main road and a big temple at the end of it.
> It's a busy town. There are markets everywhere, and the traffic never stops.

**Move three — what it's known for:**

> It's known for its sweets.
> It's famous for the fair in winter.
> People come for the waterfall.

**And one honest bad point.** It makes the rest believable, and it is often what people ask about:

> The only problem is the road floods every monsoon.
> It's nice, but there's not much work.

**Keep it in the present.** You are describing the place as it is now. If you left long ago, say so
first: *I haven't been back for years, but …*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the name with no anchor**:

> I'm from Rampur. It's a nice place.

If the listener does not know Rampur, *nice* tells them nothing about where or what.

The second is **the three adjectives every town gets**:

> It's peaceful, green, and the people are very good.

Almost everyone says this about their home. The listener hears nothing about yours.

The third is **only good things**. A town with no problems sounds like an advert, and the listener stops
believing the good things too.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers place a town in one line — *it's near X, on the way to Y* — and then say the one thing it
is known for. Almost always, the listener picks up on that one thing and asks about it.

Under pressure, speakers fall back on *it's a nice place*, because it needs no vocabulary. One concrete
thing — a river, a market, a food — needs a single new word and changes the whole answer.

A native listener enjoys the honest bad point. *The only problem is …* is often where the real
conversation starts.
