## One-line answer

Describe your real town or village for three different listeners — somebody visiting for a day,
somebody who might move there, and somebody who will never go — and change what you choose to say for
each, because the same place needs a different three moves depending on what the listener will do with
them.

## The story

In one week, three people ask about your town.

A friend is driving past it on the highway and wants to stop for an hour:

> Stop at the market by the river — the sweet shop with the green board. Don't go into the old town, you'll
> never find parking.

A cousin is thinking of taking a job there:

> It's much cheaper than the city, and quieter. But there's only one decent hospital, and the buses stop
> at nine.

A colleague is just making conversation:

> It's the town with the sweets. Every monsoon the road floods and the whole town goes to the bridge to
> watch.

Three descriptions of the same place, all true. Each one gave its listener what they needed.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the three moves** — where it is, what it's like, what it's known for, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-where-it-is-what-its-like-what-its-known-for.md).
- **visitor** — somebody who comes to a place for a short time.
- **practical** — useful for doing something.
- **tailor** — change something to fit one person.

The three moves stay the same. What goes into them changes.

| Listener | What they need | What to lead with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a visitor for a day | what to see, what to do, what to avoid | one place to go, one thing to eat, one warning |
| somebody who might live there | what daily life is like | costs, transport, hospitals, schools, one honest problem |
| somebody who will never go | a picture and a story | the thing it is known for, and a small scene |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a story told to somebody who has never been where it happened. The place has to be
described for *that* listener, in two or three sentences, in the way that helps the story. This is that
choice, practised on the place you know best.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the same description for everyone**: the one you always give. The visitor
hears about schools; the future resident hears about sweets.

The second is **the visitor's version with no warning**. Every place has one thing visitors get wrong:
parking, a closing time, a festival day. Leaving it out is the only unhelpful thing you can do.

The third is **the resident's version that hides the problems**, to be kind. The person who moves there
will find them in a week, and remember that you did not mention them.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers adjust a description of home without thinking: a tip for a visitor, the truth for
somebody moving, a story for somebody curious. The listener feels it was said for them.

Under pressure — a stranger on a train, a quick question at work — people give the version they always
give. Asking one short question first, *are you thinking of going?*, lets you choose.

A native listener values the practical line most: the parking warning, the bus that stops at nine. It is
the line they will thank you for later.
