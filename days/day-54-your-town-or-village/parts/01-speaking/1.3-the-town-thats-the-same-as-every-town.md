## One-line answer

Describe your real town on purpose using only the words people use for every town — *peaceful, green,
nice people, good food, lots of temples* — and then describe a friend's town the same way, and ask a
listener to tell the two apart, because a description that fits every town tells a stranger nothing
about yours.

## The story

On a long train journey, two strangers in the same compartment each describe their home town to a third
passenger.

The first:

> It's a very peaceful place. Very green. The people are very friendly. The food is very good. There are
> many temples.

The second:

> It's a very nice place. Very green, very calm. The people are good. The food is excellent. There are
> many old temples.

The third passenger smiles and says:

> They sound like the same town!

They are four hundred kilometres apart, in different states, with different languages. Every word both
of them said was true. None of it was about *their* town.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one
we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **general** — true of almost every place.
- **specific** — true of this place and not others.
- **the three moves** — where it is, what it's like, what it's known for, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-where-it-is-what-its-like-what-its-known-for.md).
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Some words are said about almost every town and village: *peaceful*, *green*, *friendly people*, *good
food*, *many temples*, *nice climate*. They are warm and true, and they fit everywhere.

| Kind of detail | Example | Tells a stranger which town? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| general | *very peaceful, good people* | no |
| specific | *the market runs along the river; known for its sweets* | yes |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate story will be set in a real place. If that place is described in general words, the
listener pictures a generic town and the story loses its ground. This part lets you hear, once, how
similar two very different places can be made to sound.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Using 'there is' and 'there are'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/using-there-there-are) —
whose own example, "There's a café, a supermarket and a bus stop on my street", is specific: a listener
could walk down that street.

**Step one — the RED take for your own town.** Five or six sentences, using only general words: *peaceful*,
*green*, *friendly*, *nice*, *good food*, *many temples* or *many shops*. No anchor, no name of anything,
nothing it is known for.

**Step two — a RED take for a second real place** you know — a friend's town, where a relative lives — in
the same general words.

**Step three — the GREEN take for your own town**, with the three moves from
[part 1.1](1.1-where-it-is-what-its-like-what-its-known-for.md): an anchor, one thing you would see, what
it is known for, one honest bad point.

**Step four — the tell-apart test.** Play the two RED takes to a listener, with the names hidden, and ask:

> Can you tell me one difference between these two places?

Then play the GREEN take and ask:

> What's one thing you'd recognise if you went there?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take that sneaks in a real detail** — a river, a
famous temple. Keep it general enough that it could be about the other town.

The second is **a listener who knows one of the places**. They will tell them apart from memory.

The third, in real speech, is **the specific detail that is too local to mean anything**: *it's near
Sharma uncle's shop*. Specific to you, and useless to a stranger. Choose details a stranger could see or
find.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Almost everyone describes home in general words first, in any language, because home is too familiar to
see. Fluent speakers learn to reach for the one thing a stranger would notice.

Under pressure — a quick question, a stranger's polite interest — the general words come out by
themselves. One specific sentence after them is enough to change the conversation.

A native listener forgives *it's very peaceful* and remembers *the whole town goes to the bridge to watch
the flood*. The second is the one they will repeat to somebody else.
