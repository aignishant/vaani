## One-line answer

A paragraph about your town opens with a topic sentence that says what kind of place it is, then places
it for a stranger, then gives two or three things a visitor would actually see — each with *there is* or
*there are* and one true detail — and ends on one plain line, so a reader who has never been there can
picture it.

## The story

Your cousin lives abroad. Her daughter has a school project: *write about the town your family comes
from*. She has never been. Your cousin asks you for one paragraph she can use.

You write:

> Our town is very beautiful and famous. It has a rich history and culture. The people are very warm and
> welcoming. There are many things to see. Everyone should visit once.

Your cousin writes back:

> Lovely! But what does she actually say is there? Her teacher will ask.

Five sentences, and not one thing a child could draw or a teacher could check. The paragraph says the
town is worth visiting and never says what is in it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **topic sentence** — the first line, which says the topic and the point, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- ***there is*, *there are*** — saying what exists in a place, from
  [day 30](../../../day-30-the-schwa/parts/02-writing/2.1-there-is-and-there-are.md).
- **location** — where a place is.
- **detail** — one small, true, specific thing.
- **present simple** — the verb form for things that are generally true now: *the river runs*, *the
  market opens*.

A paragraph about a town has one job: to put a stranger there. It has five pieces.

| Piece | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| topic sentence | what kind of place, as one idea | *My home town is a small market town that lives on its river.* |
| location | where, from something the reader knows | *It is about two hours south of the capital.* |
| two or three things you would see | the picture | *There is one long market along the river bank.* |
| one true detail each | makes each thing specific | *… and every stall has a blue plastic roof.* |
| last line | one plain sentence | *In the monsoon, the river comes up to the market steps.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story, and every page of story happens somewhere. The paragraph that sets
the place is often the first one, and a reader decides from it whether they can see the story at all.

It is also a piece of writing people are asked for: a school project, a letter to a pen-friend, a profile,
an application that asks where you are from.

## The rule

**Two pages were checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Using 'there is' and 'there are'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/using-there-there-are):
"We use *there is* to say that something exists or is in a place. We use *there is* for singular nouns and
*there are* for plural nouns", with:

> **There are two cafés** in the shopping centre.
> There's a café, a supermarket and a bus stop on my street.

[My last holiday](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/my-last-holiday), a
model of a short piece about a place, with the tips "Use adjectives to add detail to your descriptions."
and "Write clear and simple sentences and organise your ideas in short paragraphs. Give each paragraph a
different topic."

**The topic sentence: what kind of place, as one idea.** Not *my town is beautiful*. One idea a reader can
hold:

> My home town is a small market town that lives on its river.
> My village is at the end of the bus route, and it feels like it.

**The location, from something the reader knows.** A capital, a coast, a famous city, a highway:

> It is about two hours south of the capital, on the main road to the coast.

**Two or three things a visitor would see**, each with *there is* or *there are*:

> There is one long market along the river bank.
> There are three temples on the hill above the town.

**One true detail for each.** An adjective, a number, a colour, a sound:

> There is one long market along the river bank, and every stall has a blue plastic roof.

**The present simple, all the way through.** You are describing what the town is like now, in general.

**A plain last line.** Something true and small, not a slogan:

> In the monsoon, the river comes up to the market steps.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the brochure paragraph**, as in the story: *beautiful*, *famous*, *rich
culture*, *warm people*, *many things to see*. Part [2.3](2.3-the-brochure-paragraph.md) builds it on
purpose.

The second is **the history lesson**: the paragraph starts with the year the town was founded and never
reaches today.

The third is **the list with no sentences**:

> River. Market. Three temples. Bus stand.

Every item is specific, and it is not a paragraph. Each item needs its sentence and its detail.

The fourth is ***there is* with a plural**: *there is three temples*. The word after decides.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

The writing people remember about places — in letters, travel pieces, school essays — is full of small
true details: the blue roofs, the one bus a day, the smell of the sugar factory. The big words are
everywhere and nobody remembers them.

Under pressure writers reach for *beautiful* and *famous*, because they feel generous and need no looking
up. One detail per sentence is the whole fix.

A teacher or an examiner reading a paragraph about a place looks for exactly this: a clear first line,
specific things, correct *there is* and *there are*. The brochure paragraph reads as empty however
correct it is.
