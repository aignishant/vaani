## One-line answer

Write a paragraph about your real town on purpose in brochure words — *beautiful, famous, rich culture,
warm people, a must-visit* — and give it and your real paragraph to two readers with a pencil, because a
paragraph that praises a place and names nothing in it leaves the pencil still.

## The story

Your cousin's daughter reads both versions to her class: the first one you sent, and the one you rewrote.

> Our town is very beautiful and famous. It has a rich history and culture. The people are very warm and
> welcoming. There are many things to see. Everyone should visit once.

The teacher asks the class to draw it. Most of the children draw a sun, some hills and a house — the same
picture they would draw for any town. One child asks, *What are the things to see?*

Then she reads the rewritten one, and they draw a river, blue roofs and three temples on a hill. Same town,
same class. One paragraph gave them a place; the other gave them an advert.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one we
have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **brochure** — a small printed advert for a place, full of praise.
- **brochure words** — words that praise without showing: *beautiful*, *famous*, *rich culture*.
- **the map test** — asking a reader to draw what a paragraph describes, from
  [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-town.md).
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Paragraph | Made of | What a reader can draw |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RED — brochure | *beautiful*, *famous*, *rich culture*, *warm people*, *many things to see* | nothing specific |
| GREEN — your real paragraph | a location and things with details | the actual place |

Brochure words are not wrong. They are the words a reader has already seen about a thousand other places,
so they stop meaning anything.

The phase 3 gate page has to put a stranger in a real place. A brochure setting paragraph is the most

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[My last holiday](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/a2/my-last-holiday), whose
tip is "Use adjectives to add detail to your descriptions." Brochure words are adjectives that add no detail.

**Step one — write the RED paragraph** about your real town or village, five or six sentences, using only
brochure words. No location, no name of any place in the town, no number, no colour, nothing you could
photograph.

**Step two — the map test with two readers.** Give the RED paragraph to one reader and your GREEN paragraph
from [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-town.md) to another. Neither knows your town. Ask each:

> Draw what you just read.

Then:

> What's one thing you could find if you went there?

things that are in your paragraph and names one they could find.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED paragraph with one real thing in it** — the name of
the river, a famous temple. That one thing gets drawn and the test is spoiled. Keep it all praise.

The second is **readers who know the town**. They draw from memory.

The third, in real writing, is **the brochure line at the end of a good paragraph**: *everyone should visit
once*. One such line is harmless. It is only a problem when it replaces the details.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Real brochures use these words because they cost nothing. Readers have learned to skip them, which is why
brochures also include photographs. A paragraph has no photograph, so the details have to do its job.

Under pressure, writing about a place you love, the brochure words arrive first, because they are the size
of the feeling. The details are the size of the place, and those are what a reader can use.

A teacher or an examiner who reads *rich culture and warm people* reads it as filler. A reader who meets
*blue plastic roofs* believes the writer has been there.
