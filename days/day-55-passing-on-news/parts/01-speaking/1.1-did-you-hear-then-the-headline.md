## One-line answer

To pass on news, open with *Did you hear …?*, give the headline — who and what — in the very next
sentence, add one or two details and how you know, and then stop and let the other person react, because
news is a turn you hand over, not a speech.

## The story

Morning, at the gate of your building. A neighbour is waiting for the milk van.

The first way:

> You know the tailor, the one near the temple? He's been there for so long, since before we came. His
> son was studying in the city, and last year the shop was not doing so well, and his wife was not well
> also, and anyway yesterday evening, they closed the shop. For good.

Your neighbour has been waiting a long time to find out why you were telling her about a tailor.

The second way:

> Did you hear? The tailor near the temple closed down. For good — there's a *to let* sign on the
> shutter. My husband saw it last night.

She says:

> No! Oh, that's a shame. What happened?

Now she asks for the background, and you give it to somebody who wants it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **news** — something that has just happened that other people do not know yet.
- **headline** — the news in one short sentence: who and what.
- **source** — how you know: you saw it, somebody told you, you read it.
- **react** — say something back that shows how you feel about it.
- **reporting verb** — *said*, *told*, *asked*, from
  [day 49](../../../day-49-what-someone-said/parts/01-speaking/1.1-he-said-and-the-voice-change.md).

Passing on news has four beats, and the order matters more than the words.

| Beat | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 · the opener | warns the listener news is coming | *Did you hear?* · *Guess what.* |
| 2 · the headline | who and what, in one sentence | *The tailor near the temple closed down.* |
| 3 · one or two details, and the source | makes it real, and says how you know | *There's a sign on the shutter. My husband saw it last night.* |
| 4 · stop | hands the turn over | … |

The headline comes **before** the background. The background is what the listener asks for, if they want it.

## Why Vaani needs it

Passing on news is one of the commonest kinds of talk between neighbours, colleagues and family, and it is
the part of daily English where a story has to be short. The phase 3 gate is a longer story; this is the
short version, with the point first.

## The rule

**Two pages were checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Responding to news](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b1/responding-news)
lists the reactions you are handing the turn over to, for bad news:

> Oh dear. What's happened?
> Oh no, that's awful.

and for good news:

> Oh wow! Congratulations!

[Showing interest](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/a2/showing-interest)
gives the short replies a listener uses to keep you going, including "Really?", "You're joking!" and "No way!"

**The opener, with a rising tune.** A question that is not really a question — it gets attention:

> Did you hear about the tailor?
> Have you heard? · Guess what.

**The headline: who, then what, in one sentence.** The past simple does the job:

> The tailor near the temple closed down.
> Meena got the job.

**One or two details, and the source.** Say how you know, so the listener knows how much to trust it:

> My husband saw it last night.
> Her mother told me this morning.
> I read it on the building group.

If you are not sure, say so:

> I'm not sure if it's true, but apparently …

**Then stop.** Wait for the reaction. When they ask *what happened?*, that is your invitation to give the
background.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the background first**, as in the story: every piece of history before the
headline. The listener waits and waits, and by the time the news arrives, it lands flat.

The second is **no source**:

> The tailor closed down.

The listener does not know if you saw it or heard a rumour. They may pass it on as fact.

The third is **not stopping** — the headline, then details, then your opinion, then more details, with no gap
for the listener to react.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers give the headline almost at once — *did you hear, so-and-so's moving* — and then pause. The
listener's reaction tells them how much more to say.

Under pressure, in a second language, speakers build up to the news, because the background comes first in
their own memory. Saying the headline first can feel abrupt. It is not; it is what English listeners expect.

A native listener cares a lot about the source. *Apparently* and *I heard* mark news you did not see
yourself, and using them makes you sound careful, not uncertain.
