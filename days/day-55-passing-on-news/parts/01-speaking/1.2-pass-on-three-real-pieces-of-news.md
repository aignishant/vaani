## One-line answer

Pass on three real pieces of news this week — one good, one bad, one ordinary — to real people, and
handle what comes back: their reaction, their question, and sometimes the news they already had, because
passing on news is only half the turn.

## The story

Three conversations in one day.

To a colleague, good news:

> Did you hear? Ravi's wife had the baby last night — a girl. He messaged the group at two in the morning.

> Oh wow! That's wonderful. Are they both okay?

To a neighbour, bad news:

> Have you heard about the old man on the third floor? He fell on the stairs yesterday. They took him to
> the hospital.

> Oh no. Is he all right?
> I don't know yet. His daughter's with him.

To your sister, ordinary news:

> Guess what — the bus timings have changed. The first one's at six now, not half past.

> I know, I saw it yesterday.

Three kinds of news, three kinds of reaction. And in the last one, the news was not news — which happens,
and needs an answer too.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the four beats** — opener, headline, detail and source, stop, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-did-you-hear-then-the-headline.md).
- **reaction** — what the listener says to show how they feel about the news.
- **follow-up question** — a question about the news: *is he okay?*, *when?*
- **old news** — news the listener already knows.

The three kinds of news need slightly different handling:

| Kind | Opener | What the listener will usually ask | Your next line |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| good | *Did you hear?* — bright | *When?* *Who told you?* | the detail they asked for |
| bad | *Have you heard about …?* — lower, slower | *Is he okay?* *What happened?* | only what you know; say if you don't |
| ordinary | *Guess what* — light | often nothing, or *I know* | *Oh, you knew!* and move on |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is real, daily, unprepared English with real people — the practice that makes speaking easier
everywhere else. And it trains the part of any story that the phase 3 gate needs: saying the important thing
first, then answering questions about it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **bad news said too brightly**, with the same *did you hear?* as good news.
The listener hears the tune before the words, and the mismatch sounds careless.

The second is **guessing when you don't know**: *he's fine, don't worry*. If you do not know, say so. A guess
passed on becomes a fact.

The third is **repeating old news** after they say *I know*, because you prepared it. Stop, and let them add
what they know.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers change their voice for bad news before they say it: slower, lower, *have you heard about …*.
The listener is ready before the words arrive.

Under pressure — news you are upset about — speakers pile on background and feelings. Headline first still
works best, even for hard news; it lets the listener react to the news rather than to your worry.

A native listener values *I don't know yet* much more than a confident guess. It is the phrase that keeps
news honest as it passes from person to person.
