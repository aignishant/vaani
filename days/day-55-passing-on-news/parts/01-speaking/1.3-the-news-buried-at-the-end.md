## One-line answer

Pass on one real piece of news on purpose with all the background first and the headline in the last
sentence, then stop the listener partway through and ask *what's the news?* — because a listener who has to
wait for the point stops listening for it, and cannot tell you what it was.

## The story

On the stairs, you tell a neighbour about the water:

> So you know the tank on the roof, it's very old, the society was saying for years it has to be changed, and
> the secretary got three quotes last month, and there was a meeting, you weren't there, and they chose the
> cheapest one, and the man came to see it on Monday …

Your neighbour's phone rings. She says *sorry, one second*, answers it, and then goes down the stairs waving.

She never heard the last sentence: *so there'll be no water on Saturday*. That was the only part that was
news to her, and the only part she needed.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one we
have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **headline** — the news in one sentence: who and what, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-did-you-hear-then-the-headline.md).
- **background** — the story before the news.
- **buried** — hidden at the end, under everything else.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

You will pass on the same real news twice.

- **The RED take — headline last.** Every piece of background, in the order it happened, and the headline as
  the very last sentence.
- **The GREEN take — headline first**, with the four beats from
  [part 1.1](1.1-did-you-hear-then-the-headline.md).

And you will stop each listener halfway, as a phone call would, and ask them what the news was.

## Why Vaani needs it

Real conversations get interrupted: a phone, a bus, a child. News that arrives at the end is news that is
often never heard. The phase 3 gate story is longer and can build, but this habit — getting the point out
early — is what keeps a listener with you when it matters.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Responding to news](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b1/responding-news) —
every reaction on it, from "Oh no, that's awful." to "Congratulations!", is a reaction to a headline. A
listener cannot react to background.

**Step one — choose one real piece of news** with some background: a change at work, a problem in the
building, a family plan.

**Step two — build the RED take properly.** At least six sentences of background in time order, and the
headline as the last sentence.

**Step three — the interruption test.** Give the RED take to one listener and the GREEN take to another.
With each, stop at about the halfway point — say *sorry, one second* — and then ask:

> Before I go on — what's the news, do you think?

Then finish.

**Step five — the headline drill.** Take three real things that happened this week and, for each, say only the

make sense, it is not a headline yet.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED take where the background gives the news away** — *the
man came to fix the tank, so the water will be off*. Keep the consequence for the last sentence.

The second is **stopping too early** in the GREEN take, before the headline. Stop after the second sentence at
the earliest.

The third, in real speech, is **the headline softened into background**: *there's some problem with the tank,
maybe something on Saturday*. That is a headline in disguise. Say it plainly: *there's no water on Saturday*.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A native listener who hears a long lead-up starts to guess, and often asks *so what happened?* That question
is the sign the headline was buried.

Under pressure — news you are anxious about — the background comes out first because it is how you are
thinking it through. Say the headline first anyway, and then think it through with the listener.

Background is still welcome. It just belongs after the news, when the listener asks for it.
