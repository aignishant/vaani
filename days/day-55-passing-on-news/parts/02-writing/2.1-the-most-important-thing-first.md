## One-line answer

A short news report puts the most important facts in its first sentence — who, what, when and where —
then adds the details in order of importance, all in the past simple and with no opinion, so a reader who
stops after one sentence still has the news.

## The story

Your street prints a small newsletter once a month. The committee asks you to write a few lines about the
fire at the corner shop.

You write:

> It was a normal Tuesday evening and people were coming home from work. Mr Das was closing his shop at
> about eight. He noticed some smoke from the back. He called his son. His son called the fire brigade. They
> came quickly. Luckily nobody was hurt. The shop will be closed for two weeks.

The committee secretary reads it and says:

> Good — but most people will read the first line and stop. They'll think it's about a normal Tuesday.

She moves one sentence to the top:

> A small fire damaged the corner shop on Tuesday evening, but nobody was hurt. The shop will be closed for
> two weeks.

Now everybody who reads only the top knows what happened.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **report** — writing that says what happened, as facts.
- **opening sentence** — the first sentence, which carries the news.
- **the five Ws** — *who*, *what*, *when*, *where* and *why*: the questions a report answers.
- **opinion** — what you think or feel, as opposed to what happened.
- **topic sentence** — the first line that makes a promise, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **past simple** — *damaged*, *called*, *came*: the ordinary past, from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/02-writing/2.1-was-cooking-when.md).

A story told to a friend usually goes in the order it happened. A news report goes in the order of
**importance**.

```text
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  what happened, to whom, when, where        │   ← sentence 1: the news
   ├───────────────────────────────────────┐─────┘
   │  the most useful details              │           ← sentences 2–3
   ├───────────────────────────────┐───────┘
   │  background, how it started   │                   ← sentences 4–5
   ├───────────────────┐───────────┘
   │  what next        │                               ← last
   └───────────────────┘
```

The shape is wide at the top because the top carries the most. A reader can stop at any line and still
have the most important part.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past. A report is its opposite in shape — importance first, not
time first — and knowing both is what lets you choose. A report is also a thing you will write for real:
a note to a landlord, an incident at work, a message to a family group about something that happened.

## The rule

**Two pages were checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Inverted pyramid (journalism)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverted_pyramid_(journalism)) says the
widest part at the top "represents the most substantial, interesting, and important information that the
writer means to convey, illustrating that this kind of material should head the article", and that "Who,
when, where, why, what, and how are addressed in the first paragraph."
[Past continuous and past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/past-continuous-past-simple)
says the past simple is for completed past actions — the tense of every fact in a report.

**Sentence one: the news.** Who or what, what happened, when, where. One sentence:

> A small fire damaged the corner shop on Tuesday evening.

If the most important fact is that nobody was hurt, it belongs here too:

> A small fire damaged the corner shop on Tuesday evening, but nobody was hurt.

**Sentences two and three: the most useful details.** What a reader needs to know next — what changes for
them:

> The shop will be closed for two weeks while the wiring is replaced.

**Then the background**, briefly, in the past simple and the past continuous:

> The owner was closing the shop at about eight when he noticed smoke at the back.

**No opinion.** *Luckily*, *terrible*, *very sad* are your feelings. A report says what happened; the reader
feels the rest.

| Opinion | Fact that does the same job |
| --- | --- |
| *Luckily nobody was hurt.* | *Nobody was hurt.* |
| *The fire brigade came very quickly.* | *The fire brigade arrived about ten minutes after the call.* |

**Names and sources.** Say where a fact comes from if you did not see it: *the owner said*, *according to his
son*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the report that starts at the beginning of the day**, as in the story: *it
was a normal Tuesday evening*. The news is in sentence five, and most readers never get there.

The second is **the opinion report**:

> A terrible fire happened. It was very frightening and sad.

The reader learns how you felt and not what happened.

The third is **the missing *when* or *where*** in the first sentence: *a fire damaged a shop*. Which shop?
When?

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Newspapers, work incident reports and notices all use this shape, because their readers are busy. The
first sentence is often the only one most people read.

Under pressure writers tell it in time order, because that is how they remember it. The fix is to write it
that way, find the sentence that is the news, and move it to the top.

A formal reader — a manager, an office, an insurer — reads the first sentence of a report as its summary
and judges everything else by it. If the first sentence is *it was a normal day*, the report looks unsure of
what matters.
