## One-line answer

Write a short report of a real event for real readers — six to eight sentences, most important first,
facts only, past simple — and then give a reader only the first sentence and ask them what happened,
because if one sentence is not enough for them, it is not the news yet.

## The story

The next month, the newsletter needs a report about the street's first cleaning day. You write it
importance first:

> Thirty-two residents cleared the drain along Station Road on Sunday morning, the first cleaning day the
> street has held. They filled forty-one bags with rubbish and removed a broken cart that had blocked the
> drain since the summer. The committee plans to hold a cleaning day every three months. The next one is on
> the first Sunday of January.

The secretary shows only the first sentence to an old man at the gate and asks him what happened. He says,
*People cleaned the drain on Sunday.* That is the news, from one sentence.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **report** — writing that says what happened, as facts, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-the-most-important-thing-first.md).
- **the five Ws** — who, what, when, where, why.
- **the one-sentence test** — showing a reader only the first sentence and asking what happened.
- **number** — a count or amount: *thirty-two*, *forty-one bags*. Numbers make a report exact.

The report has four layers, from the widest to the smallest:

| Layer | Sentences | What goes in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the news | 1 | who or what, what happened, when, where |
| what matters next | 1–2 | what changes for the reader, what they need to do |
| details | 2–3 | numbers, names, what was seen, what somebody said |
| background or next steps | 1–2 | how it started, what will happen next |

## Why Vaani needs it

Reports are the most practical writing in phase 3: something happened, and somebody who was not there needs
to know. The phase 5 writing — incident notes, updates, complaints — is built on the same order.

[Past continuous and past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/past-continuous-past-simple),
for the past simple of every fact and the past continuous for any background:

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the first sentence that sets the scene**: *On Sunday morning the weather
was fine and people came out of their houses.* The reader has a morning and no news.

The second is **the vague report**: *many people came and cleaned a lot*. Numbers make it a report. *Thirty-two
residents*, *forty-one bags*.

The third is **the thank-you speech** at the end: *we thank everyone for their wonderful support*. It may
belong in a separate note. It is not news.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

People who write reports for work, a school or a community group learn to write the first sentence last:
they write the whole thing, then find the news and put it on top. Either way works as long as the top is
the news.

Under pressure — a report due today — the opinion words creep in, because they are quick. Every one of them
is a place a reader might disagree with you. Facts are harder to argue with.

A reader who only reads the first sentence is not lazy. They are most readers. Write for them first.
