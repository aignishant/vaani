## One-line answer

Rewrite your real report on purpose in time order — starting from the beginning of the day, with the news
in the last sentence and a few opinion words — then give two readers one version each and only the first
two sentences, and ask what happened, because a report in time order hides the news from every reader who
stops early.

## The story

The first draft of the cleaning-day report, before you reordered it:

> On Sunday we woke up early. The weather was very nice. At eight o'clock people started coming out of their
> houses with brooms and bags. Everybody was very enthusiastic. We started from the temple end of Station Road.
> It was hard work. By eleven we reached the bus stand. In the end, thirty-two residents cleared the whole drain
> and filled forty-one bags.

The newsletter is folded so that only the top of each report shows on the front. Your neighbour reads the top
and says:

> Nice, you all woke up early on Sunday.

That is everything she got. The news — the drain cleared, forty-one bags — was in the last line, inside the
fold.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong version below is one beginners commonly produce. It is not one we have
seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **time order** — telling things in the order they happened.
- **importance order** — telling the most important thing first, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-the-most-important-thing-first.md).
- **opinion word** — a word that says how you feel: *nice*, *enthusiastic*, *hard*.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Version | Order | Where the news is | What a two-sentence reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RED | time order, with opinion words | the last sentence | the weather and the time you woke up |
| GREEN | importance order, facts only | the first sentence | the news |

## Why Vaani needs it

Time order is the natural shape of a story, and a story is exactly what the phase 3 gate asks for. That is
why it creeps into reports: you have just practised it. This part makes you see the difference, so you choose
the right shape for the right reader.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Inverted pyramid (journalism)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverted_pyramid_(journalism)): the most
important information "should head the article", and "Who, when, where, why, what, and how are addressed in
the first paragraph."

**Step one — write the RED version** of your real report from
[part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-report.md): the same facts, in the order they happened, starting before the event,
with the news in the last sentence and at least three opinion words.

**Step two — the fold test.** Give the RED version to one reader and your GREEN report to another. Cover
everything below the second sentence. Ask each:

> What happened?

Then uncover the rest and ask:

> Now what happened? Which sentence had the news in it?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the RED reader cannot say what happened from the
first two sentences. It is **GREEN** when the GREEN reader can say it from the first sentence alone.

**Step four — the reorder drill.** Take the RED version and, without rewriting any sentence, number the
sentences in order of importance. Then copy them out in that new order. Mark the ones that now need a small
change — usually a time word such as *in the end* that no longer fits.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a RED version with the news in the middle**. Put it right at
the end, where time order puts it naturally.

The second is **readers who were at the event**. They know the news already.

The third, in real writing, is **time order for a report that somebody needs to act on**: a leak, a closed road,
a cancelled meeting. The action — *don't use the back stairs today* — must be in the first line, or it will be
missed.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A fluent writer uses time order for a story and importance order for a report, and knows which one they are
writing before they start. The same event can be both: a story for a friend, a report for a notice.

Under pressure, time order wins, because it needs no decisions. Reordering takes one pass and changes who
actually gets the news.

A reader of a notice, a newsletter or a work message reads the top and decides whether to read more. If the top
is breakfast, they decide not to.
