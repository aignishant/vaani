# Heat in the idea, not in the voice

`IN-13` · Day 56

## The one-line answer

In a room with other people in it, what gets remembered is not whether you disagreed but how much
of the room you scorched doing it — and almost all of that is carried in your voice, not in your
words.

## The model

Day 28 gave you the dial. *No, you're right.* *Yeah, up to a point.* *That's true, although…*
*I see what you mean, but…* *That's where I'd disagree.* You have all of it, and none of it is
today's problem.

Today's problem is the room.

Two people disagreeing is a warm thing. Useful heat — it cooks something. Put four more people in
the room and change nothing else, and the same heat starts catching on other surfaces. The person
you disagreed with is now being watched while they lose. Somebody senior is deciding whether you
are difficult. Somebody junior is learning from you what disagreeing looks like here. And on
Monday you and the other person still have to work together, which means whatever you burn today
you sit in for a long time afterwards.

So the skill is not disagreeing. You can already do that. The skill is putting the heat somewhere
it does some work and nowhere else.

**Aim it at the thing, and say the thing out loud.** A disagreement that names no object spreads.
*I don't think that's right* has nothing in it, so the room fills the gap, and what the room fills
it with is a person. Name the exact object and the fire has a container.

> Where I'd land differently is …

**Make it small on purpose.** You almost never disagree with all of it. You disagree with one
part, and you have been swallowing the rest of it whole because it is quicker. Split it out loud.
Agree with the part you actually agree with, first, and mean it — this is not a softener, it is a
boundary line, and it tells everyone watching exactly how big this is.

> I'm with you on the plan — it's the order I'd question.

That sentence has scorched nothing. Everyone in the room now knows the disagreement is one item
wide, including the person you are disagreeing with, who can now defend one item instead of
defending themselves.

**Leave the door open.** In private, you can lose an argument and nobody sees. In a room, backing
down is expensive, so people defend positions long past the point they believe them. You can make
that cheap for both of you, and it costs you a sentence.

> Happy to be talked out of it.
> You might well be right — I just can't see it yet.

Both of these mean *I have a position and I am not welded to it.* Say them level and move on. Do
not say them and then keep arguing for another four sentences, because then they were decoration
and everybody could hear that.

**Disagreeing upward is a question.** When there is a hierarchy in the room, a flat statement
against the most senior person is read as a challenge whether you meant it that way or not. A
question is not, and it is honest — usually they do know something you don't.

> What am I missing?
> Is it worth checking whether …?

*What am I missing?* is the most useful sentence in this whole day. It is a genuine question. It
gives the senior person a way to answer without anyone climbing down, and if they have a good
answer you have learned something instead of losing something. Ask it and then be quiet. If you
ask it and answer it yourself, it was never a question.

**And know where the exit is.** Some disagreements do not close in the room, and pushing a third
time in front of an audience is what does the damage.

> Neither of us has to be right today.

**Now the part you can actually hear.** Everything above is words, and words are the half that
does not travel. Here is the audible rule, and it is the whole day:

**The sentence where you disagree must sound like the sentence before it.** Same volume. Same
pitch. Same speed. The heat goes into the words and nothing else moves.

What happens instead, when you are nervous and being watched, is that your pitch climbs and your
volume goes up a notch on the disagreeing sentence, because your body has decided this is a
confrontation. The listener hears the pitch before they hear the words, and they answer the
pitch. You said *I'm with you on the plan, it's the order I'd question* — which is gentle — in a
voice a whole step higher than everything around it, which is not. The room registers an attack
and you have no idea, because from inside your head you were being polite.

That mismatch is on your recording tonight and nowhere else. Nobody will ever tell you.

**Today's phrases** — the room versions, for when the disagreement has an audience. All of these
go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I'm with you on {{X}} — it's {{Y}} I'd question. | Splitting it so the disagreement is one item wide |
| Where I'd land differently is … | Naming the exact object, so the room can't fill in a person |
| Can I say the other side of that? | Taking a turn for a disagreement, in front of people |
| What am I missing? | Disagreeing upward, as a question you actually want answered |
| Is it worth checking whether …? | Putting a doubt in the room without owning a fight |
| Happy to be talked out of it. | Leaving the door open so nobody has to climb down |
| You might well be right — I just can't see it yet. | Flat disagreement that leaves the other person standing |
| Neither of us has to be right today. | Stopping it before it follows you both out of the room |

## Say it now

Pick one real thing you currently disagree with somebody about. The same one for all eight lines.
Say each line twice — and both times, say an ordinary sentence *first* (*so that's the situation*,
or anything) and then the line, with no change in pitch or volume between them.

1. *So that's the situation. Can I say the other side of that?*
2. *So that's the situation. I'm with you on {{the part you agree with}} — it's {{the one part}} I'd question.*
3. *So that's the situation. Where I'd land differently is {{the exact object}}.*
4. *So that's the situation. What am I missing?*
5. *So that's the situation. Is it worth checking whether {{the doubt}}?*
6. *So that's the situation. Happy to be talked out of it.*
7. *So that's the situation. You might well be right — I just can't see it yet.*
8. *So that's the situation. Neither of us has to be right today.*

Then run lines 2, 3, 4 and 6 as one continuous turn, twice, with no pause anywhere in it. Joined
up is the condition that matters — a disagreement you had to stop and set yourself up for is one
everybody watched you decide to make.

## The deliberate failure

Take the gentlest line you have — *I'm with you on {{X}}, it's {{Y}} I'd question* — and say it
twice into the recorder.

First time: flat, at the pitch and volume of ordinary talk.

Second time: exactly the same words, but let the pitch climb through the sentence and put a little
more air behind it. Not shouting. Just the small lift your voice does on its own when you are
being watched and you know a disagreement is coming.

**What breaking sounds like:** the second one is an attack. Identical words, every softener in
place, and it lands as a challenge — you will hear it in one pass and you will not be able to
un-hear it. That is the uncomfortable finding, and it is probably historical: some of the times
you have been read as difficult, you were saying the kind sentence in the tense voice, and
everybody in the room answered the voice.

Then say it a third time, flat again, and notice how little you have to do. It is not a
performance of calm. It is just refusing to let one sentence rise above the ones around it.

## When it breaks in the wild

A full room, and the most senior person there says the thing you think is wrong. You disagree — one
careful sentence, well built — and the room goes completely silent. Nobody backs you, nobody
argues, and the silence starts to feel like a verdict on you rather than on the point. So you fill
it. You explain again, longer, with more reasons, and each new sentence sounds more like a person
defending themselves. By the third one it has stopped being about the idea.

**The repair:**

> I'm not trying to block it — I just want to be sure about {{the one thing}}. What am I missing?

Say it and then stop talking, all the way, and let the silence sit there. Silence after a
disagreement is the normal sound of people thinking. Filling it is what turns one disagreement
into an argument, and it is always the disagreer who fills it.
