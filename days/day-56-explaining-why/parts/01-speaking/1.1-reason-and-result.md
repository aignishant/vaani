## One-line answer

*Because* comes in front of the **reason**, *so* comes in front of the **result**, and *that's why* points
back at a reason you have just given — so say the one you mean, because swapping them tells the listener
the cause the wrong way round.

## The story

You arrive late for work. Your manager looks up.

> Sorry, I'm late. The bus broke down, so I had to walk from the bridge.

She nods. The reason came first, then *so*, then the result. She understood in one breath.

The next morning, a colleague is late too, and says:

> Sorry. I was late, so the bus broke down.

The manager laughs:

> You were late, *so* the bus broke down? You broke the bus?

He had the right words in the wrong places. *So* sat in front of the reason, and the sentence said his
lateness caused the bus to break down.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **reason** — why something happened: *the bus broke down*.
- **result** — what happened because of it: *I was late*.
- **cause** — the thing that makes something else happen. Here, the same as the reason.
- **joining word** — a small word that joins two parts of a sentence and shows how they connect.
- **beat three** — what you did about a problem, from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-went-wrong-and-what-you-did.md).

Every explanation has two halves: a reason and a result. Three joining words connect them, and each points a
different way.

| Word | What comes after it | Order | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *because* | the reason | result → *because* → reason | *I was late because the bus broke down.* |
| *so* | the result | reason → *so* → result | *The bus broke down, so I was late.* |
| *that's why* | the result, pointing back to a reason already said | reason. *That's why* result. | *The bus broke down. That's why I was late.* |

Before you speak, ask: *which half am I saying first?* If it is the reason, you need *so* or *that's why*.
If it is the result, you need *because*.

## Why Vaani needs it

Explaining why is the heart of phase 4 — opinions with reasons — and it runs through every story in phase 3.
A story in the phase 3 gate recording without *because* and *so* is a list of events; with them, it is a chain
the listener can follow.

In daily life, you explain why all the time: why you are late, why you changed a plan, why you said no. A
swapped joining word turns a good excuse into nonsense.

## The rule

**Two pages were checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Conjunctions: and, or, but, so, because and although](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/conjunctions-or-so-because-although)
says that after *because* "the second clause explains the reason", and after *so* "the second clause shows the
result of the first clause", with:

> I'd like to see that Irish band, **because** Celtic music is pretty cool.
> It's one of those cars for seven people, **so** there's plenty of room.

[that's why](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/that%27s_why) is defined as "a statement to emphasise the reason or
explanation just given".

**In speech, the order you think in decides the word.** If the reason comes to mind first, say it, then *so*:

> It was raining, so we stayed in.

If the result comes first — often because somebody asked *why?* — say it, then *because*:

> We stayed in because it was raining.

**Answering *why?*** The most natural spoken answer is *because* and the reason alone:

> Why were you late? — Because the bus broke down.

***That's why* for emphasis.** Say the reason as its own sentence, pause, and then point back:

> The bus broke down on the bridge. That's why I was late.

Push *that's* a little, and let the rest fall. It sounds like the explanation is complete.

**One *because* per reason.** If there are two reasons, say both after one *because*, joined by *and*:

> I was late because the bus broke down and my phone died.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the swap**:

> I was late, so the bus broke down.
> The bus broke down because I was late.

Both say the wrong thing caused the other. The listener either laughs or is confused.

The second is **both words at once**:

> Because the bus broke down, so I was late.

The listener understands; the sentence has two joining words doing one job. Use one: *Because the bus broke
down, I was late* or *The bus broke down, so I was late*.

The third is ***that's why* with no reason before it**:

> That's why I was late.

Pointing back at nothing. The listener asks *why?*

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use *so* constantly in stories — *so I called him, so he came, so we left* — and *because* when
they are asked why. *That's why* usually closes an explanation, often with a small laugh or a sigh.

Under pressure — explaining yourself to a manager, a teacher, a police officer — people pile up reasons and
lose track of which word points which way. Say the reason, then *so*, then stop.

Some languages put the reason word in a different place, or use one word for both jobs. If yours does,
*because-so* together or a swap may come out naturally. The recorder is how you find out.
