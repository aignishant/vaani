## One-line answer

Explain three real decisions you made to three real people who ask *why?* — a small one, a practical one and
one that mattered — with a reason, *so* or *because*, and one detail that makes the reason believable, and
notice whether they accept it or ask again.

## The story

Your manager asks why you moved your leave from May to June.

> Because my sister's wedding moved. The hall wasn't free in May, so they changed the date. That's why I need
> June.

She says, *Fine, put it in writing.* One question, one clear chain: the reason, the result, and *that's why*
closing it.

Later, your mother asks why you stopped taking the bus.

> Because it's always full after eight. I stood the whole way three days in a row, so now I take the train.

She says, *Good, the train's cheaper anyway.* A reason with one detail — *three days in a row* — and she did
not need to ask again.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- ***because*, *so*, *that's why*** — the three joining words for reason and result, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-reason-and-result.md).
- **decision** — a choice you made.
- **detail** — one small true fact that makes a reason believable.
- **accept** — take an explanation as enough.

An explanation somebody accepts has three things:

| Piece | Example |
| --- | --- |
| the reason, with the right joining word | *because it's always full after eight* |
| one detail that makes it real | *I stood the whole way three days in a row* |
| the result, if they did not already know it | *so now I take the train* |

A reason without a detail — *because it's not good* — often gets another *why?*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the reason with no detail**:

> Because it's not convenient.

It is true, and it invites *why not?*. One fact — *the shop closes at six and I finish at six* — ends it.

The second is **too many reasons**, one after another, with *and because … and because …*. The listener starts to
wonder which is the real one. Give the main one; add a second only if asked.

The third is **the defensive *because***, said fast and sharp, as if the question were an accusation. The listener
hears the tone before the reason.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers give one reason and one detail, and usually stop there. They add a second reason only when the
listener pushes back.

Under pressure — a manager, an official, an unhappy relative — the reason tends to come out as a pile of reasons,
because each one feels weak on its own. One strong reason with a detail is more convincing than three weak ones.

A native listener accepts a simple, specific reason easily. What makes them ask again is vagueness, not grammar.
