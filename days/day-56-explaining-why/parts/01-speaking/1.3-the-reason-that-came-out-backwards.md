## One-line answer

Record six real explanations on purpose with *because* and *so* swapped, mixed in with six correct ones, and ask
a listener after each *what caused what?* — because a swapped joining word does not sound like a grammar
mistake; it sounds like a different, stranger fact.

## The story

A colleague is explaining why the office tea machine was switched off:

> The fuse blew, because the machine was switched off.

Your manager frowns:

> Switching it off blew the fuse?

He meant: *the machine was switched off because the fuse blew*. Same two facts, same *because*, the halves in the
wrong order. It sounded like a real claim about electricity, and the manager took it seriously for a moment.

Later he says:

> It was raining, so I brought an umbrella. — correct.
> I brought an umbrella, so it was raining. — and everybody laughs.

That second one is so strange that nobody believes it. The first one was only a little strange, and that is the
dangerous kind.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not ones we
have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **reason** and **result** — why something happened and what happened because of it, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-reason-and-result.md).
- **swap** — put the reason where the result should be, or the other way round.
- **believable** — sounds like it could be true.
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A swap goes one of two ways:

| Kind of swap | Example | What the listener does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| clearly impossible | *I brought an umbrella, so it was raining.* | laughs, and works out what you meant |
| believable | *The fuse blew because the machine was switched off.* | believes it, or has to stop and ask |

The believable swaps are the ones that do harm, because nobody corrects them.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 4 gate is an opinion with reasons. A reason that comes out backwards is a reason that argues for the
other side. Hearing on purpose, once, which of your swaps a listener believes is how you learn to catch them in
yourself.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Conjunctions: and, or, but, so, because and although](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/a1-a2-grammar/conjunctions-or-so-because-although):
after *because*, "the second clause explains the reason"; after *so*, "the second clause shows the result".
[that's why](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/that%27s_why): "a statement to emphasise the reason or explanation just
given".

**Step one — choose six real explanations** from your life, each a reason and a result.

**Step two — record twelve lines**: each explanation once correctly and once swapped — with *so* or *because* in
front of the wrong half. Shuffle the order and keep a hidden list.

**Step three — the cause test.** Play them to a listener one at a time. After each, ask:

> What caused what?

Write down their answer, and whether they laughed, believed it, or asked.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **choosing only impossible swaps**. They teach nothing, because
nobody would believe them. Choose explanations where both directions sound possible: tired and late, sick and
absent, busy and forgot.

The second is **a listener who knows the real stories**. They will correct from memory.

The third, in real speech, is **the swap that happens under *because* at the start**: *Because I was late, the bus
broke down.* Starting with *because* makes the swap easier to make and harder to hear.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A native listener who hears a believable swap usually just believes it. That is how small misunderstandings
start at work: *he said the report was late because the system crashed* becomes *the system crashed because the
report was late*.

Under pressure — explaining a mistake — the swap is most likely, because you are thinking about the result you
are embarrassed by and the reason you want them to accept. Say the reason first, then *so*; it is the order that
is hardest to swap.

What protects you is the pattern. If you always say reason, *so*, result, the swap almost cannot happen.
