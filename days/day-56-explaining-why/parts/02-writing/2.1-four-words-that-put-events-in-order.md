## One-line answer

*When*, *while*, *after* and *before* join two past events and tell the reader their order — *after* puts
its event first, *before* puts its event second, *while* holds a longer action in progress, and *when* marks
the moment — and if the joined part comes first in the sentence, a comma follows it.

## The story

You write a letter to your grandmother about the day your son started school.

> We reached the school. I took a photo. He cried. He saw his friend. He stopped crying. I left.

She writes back:

> Lovely — but did he cry before or after you took the photo? And did you leave while he was crying?

Six short sentences, all true, all in the right order — and she still could not tell how the events
connected. Which came first? Which happened during which?

You write it again:

> When we reached the school, I took a photo. He cried while I was taking it. After he saw his friend, he
> stopped crying, and I left before he could change his mind.

Now she can see the whole morning, in order, and she knows you did not leave while he was crying.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **clause** — a part of a sentence with its own subject and verb, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-four-joining-words.md).
- **joining word** — a word that joins two clauses and shows how they connect, from the same part.
- **time word** — a joining word that shows *when* something happened compared with something else.
- **past continuous** — *was* or *were* plus *-ing*, for something in progress, from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/02-writing/2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **main clause** — the clause that can stand alone as a sentence.

On day 39 you joined sentences with *and*, *but*, *because* and *so*. Today's four words join them by *time*.

| Word | What it tells the reader | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *after* | the *after* event happened first | *After he saw his friend, he stopped crying.* |
| *before* | the *before* event happened second | *I left before he could change his mind.* |
| *while* | an action was in progress during the other one | *He cried while I was taking the photo.* |
| *when* | the moment one thing happened, or the moment of an interruption | *When we reached the school, I took a photo.* |

The trap is *after* and *before*: the word goes in front of the event it describes, not in front of the event
that comes first in time.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past, and every story joins events in time. A page of short
separate sentences reads like a list; a page with *when*, *while*, *after* and *before* reads like a story. These
four words are how a reader always knows what came first.

## The rule

**Three pages were checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Past continuous and past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/past-continuous-past-simple),
for *while* and *when* with an action in progress:

> While I was studying, I suddenly felt sleepy.
> We were watching television when the power went off.

[before](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/before), whose conjunction sense is "In advance of the time when":

> Brush your teeth **before** you go to bed.

[Comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma), section *Separation of clauses*: "In English, a comma is often used
to separate a dependent clause from the independent clause if the dependent clause comes first: *After I fed the
cat, I brushed my clothes.* (Compare this with *I brushed my clothes after I fed the cat*.)"

**The four words, in pictures:**

```text
   after     [ he saw his friend ]  ──►  [ he stopped crying ]
             after + this             happened before this

   before    [ I left ]  ──►  [ he could change his mind ]
                              before + this happened second (or never)

   while     [ I was taking the photo ═══════════════ ]
                     [ he cried ]      ← during it

   when      ─────── ● we reached the school ───────
                       ● I took a photo      ← at that moment
```

**While goes with the past continuous** for the longer action:

> He cried while I was taking the photo.

**When goes with the moment**, often with the past continuous on the other side for an interruption:

> I was paying the fees when the bell rang.

**Two orders, one comma rule.** The time clause can come first or second:

| Order | Punctuation | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| time clause first | comma after it | *After I fed the cat, I brushed my clothes.* |
| main clause first | no comma needed | *I brushed my clothes after I fed the cat.* |

**A time clause alone is not a sentence.** *When we reached the school.* with a full stop leaves the reader
waiting for what happened then.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***before* and *after* swapped**:

> I left after he could change his mind.

Now you left later, not sooner. The meaning reverses.

The second is ***while* with two short actions**:

> While he saw his friend, he stopped crying.

Seeing is a moment, not a stretch of time. *When he saw his friend, he stopped crying.*

The third is **the missing comma after a time clause that comes first**:

> After he saw his friend he stopped crying.

Often readable, and a reader sometimes runs the two clauses together.

The fourth is **the time clause standing alone**:

> When we reached the school. I took a photo.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Written stories, letters and reports use these four words in almost every paragraph, and readers use them to
build the timeline without thinking. Get one wrong and the reader's timeline is wrong, often without them
noticing.

Under pressure writers use *then* for everything: *we reached, then I took a photo, then he cried*. It works,
and it only shows order. *While* and *when* also show what overlapped, which *then* cannot.

A formal reader — an incident report, a complaint — reads *before* and *after* as exact claims. *The water came
in after we turned off the tap* and *before we turned off the tap* are two different complaints.
