## One-line answer

Take a real day written as short separate sentences and join it with *when*, *while*, *after* and *before*
into a paragraph for a real reader, then ask them to draw the timeline — because if their timeline matches
yours, every joining word pointed the right way.

## The story

Your grandmother asks for another letter, about the day of your cousin's engagement. Your first notes look
like this:

> We left the house. It started raining. We reached the hall. The priest arrived. The lights went off. The
> ceremony started. The lights came back.

You join them:

> It started raining after we left the house, so we reached the hall soaked. We were drying our clothes when
> the priest arrived. The ceremony started while the lights were still off, and they only came back after the
> rings were exchanged.

Your grandmother reads it aloud to her neighbour and says, *They did the whole ring ceremony in the dark!*
That is exactly what happened. The joining words carried her there.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **time word** — *when*, *while*, *after*, *before*: a joining word that shows time, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-four-words-that-put-events-in-order.md).
- **timeline** — a line showing events in the order they happened.
- **overlap** — two things happening at the same time.
- **notes** — short separate sentences written before the real piece.

This part turns notes into a paragraph. Every pair of events you join must answer one question for the
reader: **which came first, or did they overlap?**

| If … | Use |
| --- | --- |
| A was in progress when B happened | *A* (past continuous) *when B* · *B while A* (past continuous) |
| A happened, then B | *after A, B* · *A before B* |
| A and B are at the same moment | *when A, B* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past, and it will be read by a stranger who has only your joining
words to build the timeline. The timeline test in this part is a small version of that reader.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Past continuous and past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/past-continuous-past-simple):

> While I was studying, I suddenly felt sleepy.

[before](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/before): "In advance of the time when".
[Comma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma): a comma follows the time clause when it comes first — *After I fed
the cat, I brushed my clothes.*

**Step one — write your notes.** A real day from the last month with seven to ten events, each as one short
sentence, in order. At least one event must have been in progress when another happened.

**Step two — draw your own timeline** on paper: a line with each event marked, and any overlap shown as a bar.

**Step three — write the paragraph** for a real reader, joining the notes with at least one of each: *when*,
*while*, *after*, *before*. At least two time clauses come first, with a comma. Keep one or two plain
sentences: not every sentence needs joining.

**Step four — the comma and verb check.** Underline every time clause. If it comes first, is there a comma after
it? After *while*, is the verb *was/were + -ing*?

**Step five — the timeline test.** Give the paragraph to a reader and a blank page. Ask:

> Could you draw a line and put these events on it, in order?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **every sentence joined**: long chains of *when*, *after*, *while* in every line.
The reader gets tired. Mix joined sentences with short plain ones.

The second is **the joining word that repeats the order the reader already knows**: *we left the house, and after
that, after we left, it rained*. Once is enough.

The third is **the overlap shown as a sequence**: *we dried our clothes, then the priest arrived*. The reader
thinks you finished drying first. *We were drying our clothes when the priest arrived* shows the overlap.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Letters, messages about family events and stories told in writing are all built this way: a few joined
sentences where the timing matters, and short plain ones between them.

Under pressure writers join nothing and write *then* between every event. The reader gets the order but not the
overlaps — and the overlaps are usually where the story is: the lights went out *while* the rings were exchanged.

A reader who can draw your timeline trusts the rest of your writing. A reader who draws it wrong may never know
they did.
