## One-line answer

Plant four time-word errors in your real paragraph on purpose — *before* and *after* swapped, *while* with a
moment, a missing comma, and a time clause left alone as a sentence — and ask a reader to draw the timeline,
because the swap that reverses the order looks perfectly correct and moves an event to the wrong place.

## The story

The first draft of the engagement letter, the one you did not send:

> Before we left the house it started raining. We reached the hall. While the priest arrived we were drying our
> clothes. When the lights went off. The ceremony started after the rings were exchanged.

Your cousin reads it and says:

> It was raining before we even left? And the ceremony started *after* the rings? That's backwards.

She stopped at two sentences, and they were the two that looked most correct. *Before we left the house it
started raining* put the rain at the wrong time, and *after the rings were exchanged* put the whole ceremony
after its most important moment.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not ones we
have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate** — done on purpose.
- **plant** — put a mistake somewhere on purpose to see if it is found.
- **time word** — *when*, *while*, *after*, *before*, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-four-words-that-put-events-in-order.md).
- **timeline** — a line showing events in order, from
  [part 2.2](2.2-join-your-own-day.md).
- **RED / GREEN** — a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Error | Example | Looks wrong? | Moves an event on the reader's timeline? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *before* and *after* swapped | *The ceremony started after the rings were exchanged.* | no | **yes** |
| *while* with a moment | *While the priest arrived, we were drying our clothes.* | a little | sometimes |
| missing comma after a first time clause | *Before we left the house it started raining.* | barely | rarely |
| time clause alone | *When the lights went off.* | yes | no — the reader waits |

The swap is the one to fear: it looks right and says the opposite.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story read by a stranger. A swapped *before* or *after* in that story reorders
it silently. You will not find it by looking for what looks wrong, only by checking what each word claims.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a swap that is obviously impossible**: *I woke up after I went to
bed*. A reader corrects it without thinking. Choose a pair where both orders could be true.

The second is **a reader who was at the event**. They draw from memory.

The third, in real writing, is **fixing the comma and missing the swap**, because the comma is easy to see. Check
the meaning of every *before* and *after* first; the commas come second.

These are the errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger
had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Native readers almost never notice a swapped *before* or *after* when both orders are possible. They build the
wrong timeline and move on. In a family letter that is a small confusion; in an incident report or a complaint it
can change who is responsible.

Under pressure the swap happens most in sentences that start with the time clause, because you write the
first event you think of after the time word. Reading each *before* and *after* on its own, asking *really?*,
catches it.

A formal reader expects the comma after a first time clause. Its absence rarely changes the meaning, and it
tells them the writing was not checked.
