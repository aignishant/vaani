## One-line answer

A spoken story needs three lengths of silence: a blink between chunks, a breath at the end of a
sentence, and a longer stop when the story moves to a new part. After the long stop, you start
the next part a little higher, and that tells the listener a new part has begun.

## The story

Your friend moved to another city last year. You send her voice notes instead of messages,
because she likes hearing your voice.

Last week you sent her the story of your cousin's wedding. You were excited, so it came out in one
long run:

> So we reached the hall late and the food was already out and then my uncle stood up to give a
> speech and the lights went off and everybody was laughing and in the end they did the speech in
> the dark and it was actually really nice.

She wrote back:

> Wait, the food was in the dark? Or the speech?

Every word was right. You even put small gaps between the chunks. But the whole story came out at
one level, like one enormous sentence. She couldn't hear where the arrival ended, where the
problem began or where the ending came.

The next day you record it again, and this time you stop after *the food was already out*. You
take a real breath, wait a moment, and start the next part a little higher:

> My uncle stood up to give a speech …

She replies with one line: *The lights went off during the SPEECH? That's amazing.* She heard the
parts.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **pause**: a short silence between chunks of speech, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/01-speaking/1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md).
- **chunk**: a small group of words said as one piece, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/01-speaking/1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md).
- **pitch**: how high or low your voice is, from
  [day 24](../../../day-24-voice-up-and-down/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md).
- **setting**: the where-and-when part at the start of a story, from
  [day 43](../../../day-43-story-with-three-parts/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-three-parts-a-listener-waits-for.md).
- **reset**: going back to a starting level. Here, your voice going back up after a long stop.
- **filler**: a sound like *um*, *er* or *uh* that fills a silence and means nothing.

On day 39 you learned the small gap, the one inside a sentence, about as long as a blink. That gap
tells a listener where one chunk ends.

A story is longer than a sentence, so it needs two more lengths of silence.

| Silence | How long | Where it goes | What it tells the listener |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| the blink | very short | between chunks inside a sentence | *one piece has ended* |
| the breath | a real breath in | at the end of a sentence | *one thought has ended* |
| the stop | a breath, then a moment more | when the story moves to a new part | *a new part starts now* |

The long stop has a partner. **After it, start the next sentence a little higher than you ended
the last one.** Your voice goes down at the end of a part, you stop, and then you come back in
higher. The listener hears that jump, and it tells them a new part has started.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a recorded story from your life of at least two hundred spoken words, with a
beginning, a middle and an end. A stranger has to hear those three parts, and on a recording
there is nothing to see. The long stop and the higher start are how the listener knows the middle
has begun and the end has arrived.

Everything longer than a turn needs this too: a voicemail, a story at a family dinner, an answer
at an interview.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Prosodic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosodic_unit). It says two things this part rests
on:

> Breathing, both inhalation and exhalation, only occurs at the boundaries (pausa) between higher
> units.

> At the boundaries (pauses) between declination units, the pitch and tempo reset.

In plain words: people breathe where one piece of speech ends, and after the pause the voice
starts again at a fresh height and speed.

**How the three silences look on paper.** One mark for each:

```text
   │    the blink — between chunks
   ‖    the breath — end of a sentence
   ‖‖   the stop — end of a part, then start higher
```

Here is the wedding story, marked:

```text
   We reached the hall late │ and the food was already out. ‖‖
   ↑ My uncle stood up │ to give a speech. ‖ Then │ the lights went off. ‖
   Everybody was laughing. ‖‖
   ↑ In the end │ he gave the whole speech │ in the dark. ‖ And it was lovely.
```

The ↑ means *start higher here*.

**Where the long stop goes.** At the three places a listener is waiting for:

1. after the setting, before the problem;
2. after the problem, before what happened next;
3. before the ending.

**Silence, not filler.** When you need a moment to think, stay silent. Don't say *um*. A silence
sounds like you are choosing your words. A string of *um*s sounds like you are lost.

**Breathe in the stop, not in the chunk.** Take your breath at the ‖ and the ‖‖, so you never run
out of air in the middle of a chunk.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the one-level story**. The blinks are there, but no sentence ever
really ends, and the voice never resets:

> We reached the hall late and the food was out and my uncle stood up and the lights went off and …

The listener hears one long sentence. They can't find the problem or the ending.

The second is **the filler stop**. The pauses are in the right places, but each one is filled:

> We reached the hall late, um, and the food was, uh, already out, um, and then, um …

The listener hears the *um*s louder than the story.

The third is **the stop with no reset**. You stop in the right place, but you come back at the
same low, tired pitch. The listener isn't sure whether you've finished or started again, and may
start talking.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Good storytellers are often fast, but their stops are long. Listen to anybody telling a story at a
family dinner and you'll hear it: the story races, then stops, and the next part begins a little
higher. The table goes quiet in that stop, waiting.

Under pressure, the long stop is the first thing to go. Nervous speakers are scared of silence,
so they fill it with *um* or rush past it. The fix is not to speak slower. It's to let one real
silence happen at each part of the story.

A native listener doesn't notice good pauses. They notice when pauses are missing: they feel
tired, lose the thread, or interrupt, because a missing stop sounds like you have finished.
