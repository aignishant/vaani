## One-line answer

Take a real story of about ten sentences, mark every blink, breath and stop on paper, record it as
a voice note for a real person, and ask them how many parts they heard. If they can name the
parts, your pauses did their job.

## The story

Your friend in the other city asks for a proper update: *what happened with the new flat?*

You know the story well. You found the flat. The owner said no to your cooking gas cylinder. You
argued, then found a way round it, and you moved in last Sunday. It has a clear beginning, middle
and end.

But when you record it without a plan, the three parts melt together. You listen back and even
*you* can't hear where the argument with the owner started.

So you write it down in ten short lines and mark it: a blink here, a breath there, a long stop
before the owner says no, and another before *last Sunday*. Then you record it with the page in
front of you.

Your friend's answer: *Ha. So he said no, you found a way, and now you're in. Send photos.* Three
parts, handed back in the right order.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the blink, the breath, the stop**: the three lengths of silence from
  [part 1.1](1.1-three-lengths-of-silence.md), marked │, ‖ and ‖‖.
- **reset**: starting higher after a long stop, marked ↑, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-three-lengths-of-silence.md).
- **voice note**: a short recording you send to somebody instead of a written message.
- **marked script**: your story written out with the pause marks on it.

This part is practice. You use a real story, a real listener and a real voice note. The marks go
on paper first, so your mouth doesn't have to decide anything while the recorder is on.

The marked script is a support, not something to read out. Look at it before each sentence, then
look up and say the sentence.

## The rule

**The page this rests on, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Prosodic unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosodic_unit): breathing "only occurs at the
boundaries (pausa) between higher units", and at those pauses "the pitch and tempo reset".

**Step one: choose the story.** It must be real, from the last month or two, and have three clear
parts: setting, problem, ending. Choose one your listener doesn't already know.

**Step two: write it in eight to twelve short sentences.** One line per sentence. Don't write long
sentences. You'll say them, and long sentences are where breath runs out.

**Step three: mark it.**

- ‖‖ at the end of the setting, the end of the problem, and before the ending;
- ↑ at the start of every line that comes after a ‖‖;
- ‖ at the end of every other sentence;
- │ between chunks inside a sentence, using the four places from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/01-speaking/1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md): before
  *and, but, because, so*; after a time or place at the front; between list items; before the
  important word.

**Step four: breathe at the marks.** Say the story once quietly with no recorder, taking a real
breath at every ‖ and ‖‖ and never anywhere else.

**Step five: record the voice note.** One take, straight through. Look down, look up, speak.

**Step six: listen back once, and fill the table** before you send it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **reading the script aloud**. Eyes on the page, the voice goes flat
and even. Every mark turns into the same small gap, and the listener hears one level again. Look
up before you speak.

The second is **too many long stops**. A ‖‖ after every sentence sounds dramatic and slow, like a
news reader announcing something terrible. Three ‖‖ in a ten-sentence story is about right.

The third is **the reset that goes too high**. You jump up so far that it sounds like a question
or like surprise. A little higher is enough: about where your voice starts a normal sentence when
you're fresh.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Nobody marks up their voice notes in real life. But people who tell stories well have usually
told the story before, and the stops have settled into the right places. Marking a script is how
you get that practice on purpose.

Under pressure, a phone call with a lot of background noise for example, the long stops matter
more, not less. A listener in noise catches the stops and the higher start even when they miss a
word.

What a real listener tells you back is the best check you have. If they give you three parts in the
right order, the pauses worked. If they give you one blur, or ask *wait, when did that happen?*,
look at your recording where they got lost. The stop will be missing there.
