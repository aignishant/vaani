## One-line answer

Each paragraph holds one idea you can name in three words, and you start a new paragraph when the
idea changes: a new time, a new place, a new person or a new point. On the page, you show the
break with a blank line or an indented first line.

## The story

Your sister lives abroad. Once a month you write her a long email with all the news from home.

This month there's a lot: your mother's knee operation, the new neighbours, and your nephew's
exam results. You write it all in one block, because that's how it came out of your head:

> Amma's operation went well and she is walking with a stick now. The doctor says six weeks. New
> people moved into the flat upstairs, a couple with a baby who cries at night. Ravi passed all his
> exams and got first class in maths. Amma is very proud of him. The baby's name is Mira.

Your sister writes back: *Wait, who is Mira? Is Amma looking after a baby?*

Three pieces of news, one block. Your sister couldn't see where one piece of news ended and the
next began, so *Mira* got attached to the wrong story.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph**: a group of sentences about one thing, written as a block, from
  [day 13](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-paragraph-is.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, which says what the paragraph is about, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **idea**: here, the one thing a paragraph is about. You can name it in about three words:
  *Amma's operation*, *the new neighbours*.
- **break**: the place where one paragraph ends and the next begins.
- **indent**: starting the first line of a paragraph a little way in from the edge.

On day 13 you learned what a paragraph is. Today is about **where one paragraph stops**.

The rule is one question you ask before every sentence: *is this still the same idea?* If yes, it
stays in this paragraph. If no, it starts a new one.

The idea usually changes at one of four signals:

| Signal | Example of the new first line |
| --- | --- |
| a new time | *Last Sunday, …* |
| a new place | *At the hospital, …* |
| a new person the paragraph is about | *Ravi has news too.* |
| a new point or a new part of the story | *The problem started the next day.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past, **in paragraphs**. A one-page story in one block
fails the gate however good the sentences are. Every longer piece after it depends on this too:
the two-paragraph argument at the phase 4 gate, the formal letters of phase 5, and the essay at the
end.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Paragraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paragraph). It opens:

> A paragraph (from Ancient Greek παράγραφος (parágraphos) 'to write beside') is a self-contained
> unit of discourse in writing dealing with a particular point or idea.

On how a new paragraph is shown, it says that "Modern English typography usually indicates a new
paragraph by indenting the first line", and that "A second common modern English style is to use
no indenting, but add vertical white space to create 'block paragraphs.'"

**Rule one: one idea per paragraph.** Before you write, list your ideas as three-word labels. Each
label is one paragraph.

**Rule two: break when the idea changes**, at one of the four signals above. Here is the email to
your sister, broken:

```text
   Amma's operation went well. She is walking with a stick now,
   and the doctor says six weeks.

   New people moved into the flat upstairs: a couple with a baby
   called Mira, who cries at night.

   Ravi passed all his exams and got first class in maths. Amma is
   very proud of him.
```

Now *Mira* is inside the neighbours' paragraph, and nobody thinks Amma has a baby.

**Rule three: pick one way to show the break and keep it.** In a notebook, indent the first line.
In an email or a typed page, leave one empty line. Don't mix them, and don't do both.

```text
   Notebook:                     Email or typed page:
   ┌──────────────────────┐      ┌──────────────────────┐
   │    Amma's operation  │      │ Amma's operation     │
   │ went well. She is    │      │ went well. She is    │
   │ walking …            │      │ walking …            │
   │    New people moved  │      │                      │
   │ into …               │      │ New people moved     │
   └──────────────────────┘      │ into …               │
                                 └──────────────────────┘
```

**Rule four: the first line of the new paragraph names the new idea.** That's your topic sentence.
The reader should know what the paragraph is about before they reach its second line.

**Rule five: a paragraph is usually two to six sentences.** One sentence alone is sometimes fine
for a short, strong point. Eight or more usually means two ideas have been joined.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the wall**: everything in one block, as in the story. The reader
can't see where one piece of news ends, and details stick to the wrong idea.

The second is **the break in the middle of an idea**:

> Amma's operation went well.
>
> She is walking with a stick now, and the doctor says six weeks.

The reader thinks the second paragraph is new news, and waits to find out whose stick it is.

The third is **the break with no visible gap**: a new line, but no indent and no empty line. On a
screen it looks exactly like the next line of the same paragraph.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

In emails, people use short paragraphs, often two or three sentences, with an empty line between
them, because a screen is hard to read in blocks. In a letter or a notebook, indented paragraphs
are usual.

Under pressure, writing fast on a phone, people often write the wall. Then the most important
thing, like *the landlord wants the rent by Friday*, sits in the middle of a block and is missed.

A careful reader uses your paragraphs as a map. They'll often read only the first line of each one
to decide what to read properly. If your breaks are in the right places, that map is true.
