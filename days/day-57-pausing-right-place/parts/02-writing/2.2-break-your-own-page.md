## One-line answer

Write a real page of about a hundred and fifty words as one block, label every sentence with its
idea, then break it into paragraphs where the labels change. Ask a reader to name each paragraph
in three words. If their names match your labels, your breaks are in the right places.

## The story

Your sister asks for the full story of your new job. You write it in one go, one block, late at
night:

> I started at the pharmacy on Monday. The owner is strict but fair. On the first day I only
> watched. On Tuesday I used the till and made two mistakes with change. The owner showed me again.
> The other assistant, Salma, is very kind. She has worked there ten years. She brings me tea at
> four. The best part is the customers. An old man comes every morning for his tablets and tells me
> about his grandson. The worst part is the standing. My feet hurt.

Next morning you read it again. Your eyes slide through it and you can't find anything. So you
write a small label in the margin beside each sentence: *first days*, *first days*, *Salma*,
*Salma*, *customers*, *good and bad* …

Where the labels change, you break. Four paragraphs. Your sister replies: *Salma sounds lovely.
And poor feet!* She found both, because each now had its own paragraph.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph** and **break**: the block of one idea, and the place where it ends, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-a-new-idea-a-new-paragraph.md).
- **label**: a short name of about three words for what a sentence is about.
- **margin**: the empty space at the side of the page.
- **draft**: a first version you plan to change, from
  [day 38](../../../day-38-hobbies-and-free-time/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-your-paragraph.md).

Deciding where to break while you're still writing is hard. It's easier to write everything first
and break it afterwards. Labelling each sentence turns *where does the paragraph end?* into a
question you can see the answer to.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate page is a one-page story in paragraphs. Written in one go, it will come out as a
block. This part gives you the method to find the breaks in a block you've already written.

## The rule

**The page this rests on, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Paragraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paragraph): a paragraph is "a self-contained unit of
discourse in writing dealing with a particular point or idea", shown by "indenting the first line"
or by "vertical white space".

**Step one: choose a real subject** with three or four different sides. Good choices: your job or
course, a move, a family event, a trip, a change in your life this year. Choose a real reader too.

**Step two: write the draft as one block.** About a hundred and fifty words. Don't think about
paragraphs at all. Just write.

**Step three: label every sentence.** In the margin, write a three-word label beside each one.
Use the same label when the idea is the same.

**Step four: break where the labels change.** Rewrite the page with a break wherever the label
changes. Show every break the same way.

**Step five: fix the first line of each paragraph.** Make sure it names the idea. If it doesn't,
add a short first sentence that does, or move the sentence that does to the front.

**Step six: check the sizes.**

| 1 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

| 3 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| 4 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

A paragraph of one sentence may belong with its neighbour. A paragraph of eight probably has two
labels hiding in it.

**Step seven: the naming test.** Give the broken page to your reader. Ask:

> Could you give each paragraph a name, three words at most?

Write their names next to yours.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **labels that are too wide**: *my job*, *my job*, *my job*. Every
sentence gets the same label, so there's never a break. Make the labels narrower: *first days*,
*a colleague*, *the customers*.

The second is **labels that are too narrow**: a different label for every sentence, so every
sentence becomes a paragraph. If two labels would sit well under one heading, they are one
paragraph.

The third is **the stray sentence**: one sentence about Salma sitting in the customers' paragraph.
Move it to the paragraph whose label it shares, even if that means changing the order.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Experienced writers do exactly this, and it has a name: revising for structure. They write fast,
then read their own draft as a stranger, and cut it into paragraphs afterwards.

Under pressure, a quick email to a landlord or a manager, people skip the break step. It only
takes one look, though: *where does the subject change?* One empty line there makes the email
twice as easy to answer.

A reader who can name your paragraphs can find things in your writing again later. That's the
real test of a page someone keeps.
