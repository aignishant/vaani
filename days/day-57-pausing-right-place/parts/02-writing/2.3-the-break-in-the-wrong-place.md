## One-line answer

Take your real, well-broken page and deliberately damage it in two ways: join two paragraphs into
one, and split one paragraph in the middle of its idea. Then ask a reader to name each paragraph.
A break in the wrong place doesn't look like an error. It makes the reader give the wrong name.

## The story

You're writing to your sister about your father's retirement party. You have three paragraphs:
the day of the party, the speech he gave, and the gift from his office.

In a hurry, you press *enter* in the wrong place. One break lands in the middle of the speech
paragraph, right after *He stood up to speak.* The next paragraph now begins:

> He thanked Amma first, and then he cried. Then his office gave him a watch.

The speech has been cut in two, and the tears have been joined to the gift. Your sister writes
back: *Appa cried over a WATCH?*

He didn't. He cried at the speech. The break moved his tears.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **paragraph**, **break**, **label**: the block of one idea, the place it ends, and its
  three-word name, from [part 2.1](2.1-a-new-idea-a-new-paragraph.md) and
  [part 2.2](2.2-break-your-own-page.md).
- **plant**: to put an error into a text on purpose, from
  [day 46](../../../day-46-describing-a-trip/parts/02-writing/2.3-there-was-two-beds.md).
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A reader believes your paragraphs. Whatever sits inside one paragraph, they read as one idea, and
whatever sits after a break, they read as a new one. So a wrong break doesn't look wrong. It makes
two ideas look like one, or one idea look like two.

| Damage | What the reader does |
| --- | --- |
| two paragraphs joined | gives them one name, and loses the second idea |
| one paragraph split mid-idea | gives the second half a new name, and attaches it to the wrong thing |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate page will be read by a stranger. The breaks are the only map they have, and
nobody tells a writer *your break was in the wrong place*. They just take away the wrong story.
Seeing that happen once, on purpose, is how you learn to check.

## The rule

**The page this rests on, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Paragraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paragraph): a paragraph is "a self-contained unit of
discourse in writing dealing with a particular point or idea". If the unit isn't self-contained,
the reader's picture breaks.

**Step one: take your good page** from [part 2.2](2.2-break-your-own-page.md), with its three or
four paragraphs and their labels. Keep a copy.

**Step two: plant two damages** in a new copy, and write them on a hidden list:

1. **a join**: remove one break so that two paragraphs become one block;
2. **a split**: add a break in the middle of another paragraph, where the idea hasn't changed.
   Choose a place where the second half could seem to belong with the paragraph after it.

**Step three: the naming test.** Give the damaged copy to a reader who hasn't seen the good one.
Ask:

> Could you give each paragraph a name, three words at most?

Then ask one fact question whose answer the split makes easy to get wrong. In the story, it would
be *what made him cry?*

**Step four: fill the table.**

| Paragraph in the damaged copy | Planted damage here? | Reader's name | Does it match my label? |

| 4 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader gives the joined block one
name only, or gets the fact question wrong. That means the damage worked, and your breaks carry
meaning. It is **GREEN** when a second reader, given the repaired copy, names every paragraph to
match your labels and gets the fact question right.

**Step five: repair and re-test.** Put the breaks back where the ideas change. Give the repaired
copy to a second reader and ask the same two questions.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a split at a real change of idea**. Then it isn't
damage. It's just a new paragraph, and the reader names it correctly. Split in the middle of one
idea.

The second is **a reader who knows the story**. They'll name the paragraphs from memory. Use
somebody who wasn't at the party.

The third, in real writing, is **the break made by the screen**. On a phone, a long line wraps, and
it can look as if a new paragraph has started when it hasn't. Always read your email once in the
form the reader will see it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Readers rarely say *your paragraph break was wrong*. They say things like *wait, who cried?* or
*I didn't see the part about the rent*. Those questions are paragraph problems in disguise, and now
you know where to look.

Under pressure, a long message typed fast, people either never press *enter*, or press it after
every sentence. Both hide the ideas. One slow read before sending, asking only *where does the idea
change?*, catches most of it.

In formal writing, like reports, letters and essays, a reader who meets a wrong break stops
trusting the structure. They start reading every line slowly, and they get tired of you.
