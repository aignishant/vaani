# The listener can't scroll back

`FN-17` · Day 57

## The one-line answer

A reader can go back up the page and find out where they are; a listener cannot, so everything
your structure would have shown on paper you have to say out loud, in words, as you go.

## The model

Put somebody in the passenger seat and paint over the windows. That is your listener. You have the
map — you know there are three parts, you know which one you are in, you know how far there is to
go. They have none of it. All they get is your voice, arriving one word at a time, already gone.

They cannot scroll back. They cannot skim ahead. They cannot see a heading, a bullet, a paragraph
break or a page number. If they lose the thread, there is no mechanism anywhere in the world by
which they get it back — except you saying something.

That is the whole day. **Structure that isn't spoken doesn't exist.** You can have the cleanest
three-part talk ever built and if you never say the number three out loud, your listener heard one
long undifferentiated thing and quietly stopped following somewhere in the middle.

So you say the map. Out loud, in sentences, three jobs.

**Job one: the map, before any content.** First thing out of your mouth, before the first fact.

> There are three things I want to cover.

Then name them. Not explain them — name them, in a handful of words each, so that when each one
arrives your listener recognises it instead of meeting it. This costs you one sentence and it buys
the rest of the talk. Somebody who knows there are three parts can survive being lost in part two,
because they know part three is coming and they can rejoin. Somebody who does not know how many
parts there are cannot rejoin anything, because they have no idea what they would be rejoining.

Three. Not four, not six. Three is the number a listener can hold without writing it down, and you
are not allowed to give them a pen.

**Job two: the junctions.** Every place the talk changes section is a junction, and a junction with
no sign on it is where people get lost. The sign has two halves — what just ended, and what starts
now — and you say both.

> First one — {{…}}
> That's the first. On to the second.
> Which brings me to {{…}}

*That's the first. On to the second.* is two short sentences that between them do more work than
any amount of clever transition. The first half closes a box. The second half opens the next one.
A listener hears a box close and thinks *right, I got that one or I didn't, either way it's done*
— and that permission to let go is what keeps them with you. Without it, everything you have said
is still open in their head, and they are carrying all of it, and carrying is tiring.

**Job three: the position marker.** Somewhere in the middle you will be able to feel that they have
drifted. Or you will not, which is more common. Either way, put one in.

> Just to be clear where we are — this is still the second thing.

That is not a filler sentence. It is the spoken version of glancing at a page number, and it is
the only version your listener is going to get.

**Two more, and they earn their place.**

The detour. You will want to add something that is not on the map, and the danger is not the
detour itself, it is that an unannounced detour is indistinguishable from you losing your way.

> Before I go on, one thing about {{…}}.

Announce it and it reads as generosity. Don't, and it reads as drift.

And the end. Endings that arrive without warning get missed entirely — your listener is still
listening for the next part when you have already stopped, and the last thing you said, which was
your best thing, lands in a room that had not started paying attention to it yet.

> Last one, and then I'll stop.
> If you remember one thing, it's this.

*If you remember one thing, it's this* is the loudest sign you own. Use it once. Use it twice and
you have told your listener that two things are the one thing, which is not a structure, it is a
shrug.

**The audible rule.** A signpost has silence on both sides of it. That is not a stylistic
preference, it is what makes the difference between a signpost and a sentence. Said at speed,
inside a run of other sentences, *that's the first, on to the second* is just more talking — your
listener's ear has no way to separate a sign from the scenery. The gap either side is the marking.
On tonight's tape you will be able to point at your signposts only if you left the gaps, and that
is exactly how your listener finds them too.

## Say it now

Pick one thing you actually know about and could talk about with no preparation. Break it into
three parts in your head — just three names, no content. Then run the lines in order, as a talk
with almost nothing in it: a signpost, one sentence of content, the next signpost.

1. *There are three things I want to cover: {{one}}, {{two}}, and {{three}}.*
2. *First one — {{one sentence about the first thing}}.*
3. *That's the first. On to the second.*
4. *Just to be clear where we are — this is still the second thing. {{one sentence}}.*
5. *Before I go on, one thing about {{the detour}}.*
6. *Which brings me to {{the third thing}}.*
7. *If you remember one thing, it's this: {{the point}}.*
8. *Last one, and then I'll stop. {{the last sentence}}.*

Then run all eight again, and this time leave a real gap before and after every signpost — silence
you can hear, wide enough to feel slightly wrong. Skeleton first, then the same skeleton with the
gaps in. Do both. The difference between the two passes is what your listener is actually working
with, and you cannot hear it by doing only one of them.

> A talk on something you know, with three parts. Say the map first. Sign every junction. Put one
> position marker in the middle and announce the ending before you get there. The content can be

- **Solo form:** as written, standing, to an empty chair. Do not write the three parts down. If you
  can't hold three names in your head, that is the finding — it means it is not three parts yet.
- **With a partner:** they listen with their eyes shut and hold up fingers — one, two, three — for
  whichever part they think you are in, changing when they think you've moved. Watch their hand,
  keep going, say nothing. Compare at the end.

## The deliberate failure

Give the same talk again, same three parts, same order, same content — and take every signpost
out. No map at the front, no junctions, no marker, no warning before the end. Just the content,
run together.

**What breaking sounds like:** the first thing is that it sounds fine. It is coherent, it flows,
and it is arguably better prose than the signposted version. That is the trap, and it is why this
failure is worth doing rather than reading about.

Now play it back and try to do one thing: say out loud, on the tape, where part two begins. You
will not be able to. You wrote it, you delivered it, you know there are three parts — and you
cannot find the seams in your own talk on one listen. Your listener had less than you have and one
chance at it.

Here is the uncomfortable part. Nobody has ever told you they lost your structure. They don't. A
listener who has lost the thread does not put their hand up and say *sorry, which part are we in* —
they nod, they wait, and they quietly stop spending effort. It is completely silent from where you
are standing, which is why an unsignposted talk can feel like it went well every single time.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are explaining something to somebody who asked, and it is going well, and then they ask a
question that you already answered. Not a hard question — the exact thing you covered a few
junctions back. And you feel the small drop of *were you not listening?*

They were. They just had no idea that bit was an answer, because you never told them it was a
section, so it went past as more talking. This is not their failure of attention. It is a missing
sign.

**The repair:**

> Let me do that again properly — there are three things here, and that was the first one.

Restart the map, mid-conversation. It sounds like organisation, never like backtracking, and it is
almost always the fastest route out.
