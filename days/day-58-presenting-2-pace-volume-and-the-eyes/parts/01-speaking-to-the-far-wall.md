# Speaking to the far wall

`PR-15` · Day 58

## The one-line answer

Pace, volume and where your eyes are pointing are all one decision — how far away you are speaking
to — and the third one is the one nobody tells you is audible, because a speaker reading their
notes sounds different from a speaker looking up, on the tape, every time.

## The model

There is a wall at the back of the room. Not the person in front of you, not the table, not the
card in your hand a forearm's length from your face. The far wall.

Everything today comes from that one choice, and the reason it works is that it is physical rather
than a matter of confidence. You cannot decide to sound authoritative. You can decide which
surface you are aiming at, and your body does the rest without being asked.

**Volume is a distance, not a level.** Do not think *louder*. Louder is a thing people do for one
sentence and then abandon, because there is nothing holding it up. Think *that wall*, and your
breath, your chest and your mouth all reorganise on their own — this is the same machinery you use
outdoors calling to somebody across a road, and it has always worked.

Then the part that actually goes wrong: **volume dies at the ends of sentences.** You set it well
and by the last three words of the sentence you are back down at table volume, because you have run
out of air and because the interesting part of the sentence, to you, was at the beginning. To your
listener the last three words are usually the point. On tonight's tape this is the single easiest
fault to hear — pick any sentence and ask whether its last three words are as loud as its first
three. Mostly they are not.

The fix is where you breathe. Take the breath at the signpost, not in the middle of a clause. A
breath taken before the sentence starts is enough air to reach the wall with the end of it still
attached.

**Pace is not slowness.** Being told to slow down produces a strange, even, dragged delivery that
is harder to listen to than fast talking, not easier. What actually changes is not the words per
stretch. It is the air between the stretches.

You have this already — Day 45 gave you the pause on purpose, and the finding there was that a
silence feels far longer from inside your own head than it turns out to be on the recording. That
mismatch does not go away with practice. It is why every speaker under pressure speeds up: the
gaps feel enormous, so they get filled.

One placement rule, and it is audible: **the pause goes before the thing that matters, not after
it.** A pause before makes the room lean in. A pause after is you waiting to see whether it landed,
and everyone can hear the difference.

> This next bit matters.

That is a sentence whose whole job is to be followed by silence.

**And the eyes — which are audible.** This is the one that surprises people, so here is the
mechanism rather than the claim.

When you read, your chin drops toward your chest. That folds your throat, and the sound leaves your
mouth aimed at the table, which is a hand's width away, not at the wall. Volume falls and the ends
of sentences fall further.

When you read, your eyes are running ahead of your mouth. So there are no gaps. You do not need
them — the next words are already there, waiting. A read stretch of tape is continuous, even, and
strangely smooth, and there is nowhere in it that a listener can put anything down.

And when you read, sentence stress goes flat. Day 13 taught you which words get hit; hitting them
requires knowing what is coming and deciding, and a reading mouth is not deciding anything, it is
tracking. Every word comes out roughly the same size.

Down chin, no gaps, flat stress. Three things, all on the tape, all from one thing your listener
cannot see.

So the rule for notes, and it is the whole rule: **look, then speak. Never read and speak at the
same time.** Your eyes go down, you take one line, your eyes come up, *then* your mouth opens. The
gap that creates is not dead time — it is the pause you were supposed to be taking anyway. Done
properly, your notes generate your pacing for you.

And say it out loud when you need to go down.

> I'm going to look down for this one.

That sentence turns a suspicious silence into an announced one. Nobody minds a speaker checking a
note. They mind a speaker who has gone quiet and might be lost.

**Where the eyes go when they're up.** Three places only: the far wall, one face, and back to the
wall. One face for one whole sentence, not a sweep — sweeping the room is a nervous movement and it
comes out in the voice as a wobble. Land on a face, say a complete sentence to that person, leave.
Empty room? Pick a chair and do exactly the same.

**Today's phrases** — short lines that buy you the look-up, said with your head already lifted. All
of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Can you hear me at the back? | Setting volume from the far wall before you need it |
| Everyone still with me? | Checking the room, which forces your eyes and your chin up |
| This next bit matters. | The line you say so the silence after it lands as weight |
| I'm going to look down for this one. | Naming the glance instead of going quiet mid-sentence |
| I'll say that again, slower. | Repeating on purpose, at a speed you chose |
| Two words on why. | Announcing a short stretch so you can take it fast without losing them |
| And I'll leave that there. | Landing the end of a section instead of letting it fade out |

## Say it now

Stand up. Face the furthest wall you have. Write nothing down — these lines are short enough to
hold. Say each one three times: once at ordinary table volume, once aimed at the wall, once aimed
at the wall with a real silence after it.

1. *Can you hear me at the back?*
2. *Everyone still with me? {{the thing you just covered}}.*
3. *This next bit matters. {{the thing that matters}}.*
4. *I'm going to look down for this one. {{the detail}}.*
5. *I'll say that again, slower. {{the same sentence again}}.*
6. *Two words on why — {{the reason}}.*
7. *And I'll leave that there.*

Then run all seven as one continuous stretch, standing, with your hands empty and your chin level,
and make yourself hear the last word of every single line. Not the first word. The last one. The
ends are where this day is won or lost, and running them joined up is the only condition that
makes the ends hard.

- **Solo form:** as written, standing, facing an empty chair with the wall behind it. Put the
  recorder on the far side of the room, not next to you — you want it hearing what the room hears,
  not what your chin hears.
- **With a partner:** they sit as far away as the room allows and their only instruction is to
  raise a hand the moment they miss a word. They do not say which word. You keep going. Count the
  hands at the end and ask then.

## The deliberate failure

Write out one stretch of your talk in full sentences, on paper. Then record it twice.

First: read it. Eyes down the whole way, never lifting, at whatever speed the page gives you.

Second: turn the paper face down and say the same stretch from memory, to the wall, with your chin
level.

**What breaking sounds like:** on the tape, the read one has no gaps in it anywhere. None. And
every sentence gets quieter towards its end — you will hear the last two or three words drop away,
sentence after sentence, like something falling off a shelf. The stress is flat, so nothing in it
is more important than anything else, so nothing at all stands out.

Here is the uncomfortable finding, and it is why this failure is the one worth doing. **The read
version felt better while you were doing it.** It was smoother. You didn't stumble, you didn't
grope for a word, you didn't have a single one of those small humiliating pauses. It was, from the
inside, the better performance — and on the tape it is the worse one by a long way.

Fluency and audibility are two different things, and only one of them is what your listener needs.
Every time you have reached for your notes because you felt yourself getting rough, you were
trading the second for the first.

## When it breaks in the wild

A long table, a fan somewhere overhead, and somebody at the far end says *sorry, could you speak
up?* So you do — for one sentence. Then, without deciding to, you drift back down, because you set
the volume by an act of will and will does not last. Two more sentences and you are back at table
volume, and now nobody at the far end is going to ask twice.

**The repair:**

> Can you hear me at the back? — right.

Then pick the furthest person and say the next three sentences to that person's face, all the way
through, before you look anywhere else. You are not turning the volume up. You are changing what
you are aiming at, and the volume follows on its own and stays there, which the willed version
never does.
