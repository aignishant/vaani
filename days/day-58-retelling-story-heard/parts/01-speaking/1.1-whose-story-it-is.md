## One-line answer

To retell a story somebody told you, first say whose story it is (*my neighbour told me*). Then
turn their *I* into *he* or *she* and their *my* into *his* or *her*, keep it in the past, and
finish with the ending they gave it. That way the listener always knows it didn't happen to you.

## The story

At the tea break at work, your colleague Anil tells a story:

> I got into an auto last night and I said *the station*. The driver took me to the police station.
> I didn't notice until I saw the policemen. I just paid and walked out.

Everybody laughs. That evening, at home, you want to tell your family. You start the way Anil
did:

> I got into an auto last night and said *the station* …

Your mother looks worried straight away:

> Police station? Why were you at the police station?

You try again:

> Anil at work told me a funny story today. He got into an auto last night and said *the station*,
> and the driver took him to the police station …

Now everybody laughs, because they know from the first line whose story it is.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **retell**: to tell a story again in your own words, from
  [day 50](../../../day-50-film-show-or-book/parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-whole-plot-start-to-finish.md).
  Today it's a story you heard from a person.
- **source**: where a piece of news or a story came from, from
  [day 55](../../../day-55-passing-on-news/parts/01-speaking/1.1-did-you-hear-then-the-headline.md).
  Here, the person who told you the story first.
- **he, she**: the words for a man and a woman, from
  [day 14](../../../day-14-talking-about-family/parts/01-speaking/1.2-talk-about-your-own-family.md).
- **his, her**: the words for something that belongs to him or to her: *his auto*, *her phone*.
- **frame**: the first line of a retold story, which says who told it to you.
- **quote**: somebody's exact words, from
  [day 49](../../../day-49-what-someone-said/parts/01-speaking/1.1-he-said-and-the-voice-change.md).

On day 49 you passed on a few sentences somebody said, as a quote. A retold story is longer. You
aren't repeating their exact words any more. You're telling their whole story in your words, so
the words have to change to show it's theirs.

Three things change, and one thing stays:

| What | In their story | In your retelling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| who | *I*, *me*, *my* | *he / she*, *him / her*, *his / her* |
| the start | straight into it | a frame: *Anil told me …* |
| the words | theirs | yours, shorter |
| **stays the same** | the past, the facts, the ending | the past, the facts, the ending |

## Why Vaani needs it

A lot of real conversation is retold stories: what a neighbour said, what happened to a cousin,
what a colleague did. The phase 3 gate is a story from *your* life, and it will be clearer because
you have practised the difference between *I* stories and *he* stories. In phase 4 you will be
reporting what people said at length, and that starts here.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:** [tell](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tell). Sense 3
of the verb is "To narrate, to recount", and the past form is "told". *Tell* takes a person:
*he told me*, *she told us*.

**The frame comes first.** One short sentence: who, and often when.

> My neighbour told me a story this morning.
> You know Anil at work? He told us something funny today.

**Then change every *I* in their story.**

> *I got into an auto* → He got into an auto.
> *my phone died* → his phone died.
> *the driver took me* → the driver took him.

**Keep it in the past.** Their story was in the past, and so is yours.

**Keep the ending.** The ending is usually why the story was told. Anil's point was *I just paid
and walked out*. Don't drop it.

**Say where you are not sure.** You weren't there, so you can say:

> Apparently the driver didn't even say sorry.

**Close the frame if it helps.** A short line brings the listener back to you:

> That's what he said, anyway.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the story told in their *I***, with no frame:

> I got into an auto and the driver took me to the police station.

The listener thinks it happened to you. This isn't a grammar mistake a listener can hear and
forgive. It gives them the wrong facts.

The second is **the half switch**: you start with *he*, and the *I* comes back in the middle
because you remember Anil saying it:

> He got into an auto, and then I saw the policemen …

The listener has to stop and ask who saw them.

The third is **the lost ending**. You tell the setting and the problem and then stop, because you
don't remember the end well. The listener waits for a point that never comes.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers frame a retold story almost without thinking: *so my cousin was telling me …*,
*you'll love this, my neighbour …*. The frame is quick, often just a few words, but it's always
there.

Under pressure, when the story is exciting, people jump in with the other person's *I*. Listen
back for the first *I* in any story you retell. If it isn't really you, it's a slip.

What a native listener notices is who. If they can't tell whose story it is, they'll ask, or
they'll believe it happened to you.
