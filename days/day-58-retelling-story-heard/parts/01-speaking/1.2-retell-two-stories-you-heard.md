## One-line answer

Choose two real stories that people told you and retell each one to somebody who doesn't know the
teller. Use a frame, *he* or *she* all the way through, and the teller's ending. Then ask the
listener *whose story was that, and what was the point?* If they answer both, the retelling
worked.

## The story

Last week your aunt told you about her first train journey alone, at sixteen, when she got off at
the wrong station and a stranger's family fed her dinner and put her on the next train. You loved
it.

Today your friend asks, *any good stories this week?* You want to retell it, but you notice
three problems while you're thinking:

- you don't remember the name of the station;
- you remember the dinner well but not how she got back;
- your aunt told it for a long time, and your friend has one cup of tea.

So you make it smaller. You keep what you're sure of, you say *apparently* where you're not, and
you save the ending for last:

> My aunt told me this on Sunday. When she was sixteen, she took a train alone for the first time,
> and she got off at the wrong station …

Your friend listens to the end and says, *Your aunt was lucky. Strangers were kind in those days.*
She got whose story it was, and she got the point.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **frame**: the first line of a retold story, which says who told it to you, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-whose-story-it-is.md).
- **teller**: the person who told you the story first.
- **the point**: why the story was worth telling. Often it's in the ending.
- **apparently**: a word that means *I heard this, but I didn't see it myself*.
- **gap**: here, a part of the story you don't remember.

A retold story is always shorter than the first telling. You keep the parts you are sure of, the
parts that lead to the ending, and the ending itself. You say *apparently* where you aren't sure.
You don't fill gaps with things you made up.

## Why Vaani needs it

Retelling is how you practise telling a story with a clear beginning, middle and end when the
story isn't about you, so you're not also worrying about how you look in it. The phase 3 gate
recording asks for exactly that shape, from your own life.

**Step four: listen back for every *I*.** Each *I* must really be you, for example in the frame.
Circle any *I* that belonged to the teller, and record that sentence again.

**Step five: tell each story to a real listener**, face to face or as a voice note. Afterwards, ask
two questions:

> Whose story was that?
> Why do you think they told it?

Write their answers down word for word, and compare their second answer with the ending in your
notes.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **filling the gap**. You don't remember how your aunt got home, so
you add *and her father came to fetch her*. It sounds fine, and it isn't true. Say *I don't know
how she got home, actually*. That's honest, and it's natural.

The second is **the retelling that is longer than the story**. You add your own opinions all the
way through: *which is crazy, because I would never …*. Keep your reaction for after the ending.

The third is **forgetting the ending until the middle**: *oh, and the point is she got home fine,
but anyway, the train …*. Hold the ending back until last. That's where it does its job.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

People retell stories all the time, and the good retellers do three things: they frame it in a few
words, they cut hard, and they deliver the ending cleanly. Stories that get retold again and again
get shorter and sharper each time.

Under pressure, a story you half-remember at a table full of people, beginners fill gaps to keep
going. Fluent speakers say *I don't remember exactly, but …* and carry on. Nobody minds.

A native listener's reaction tells you whether you delivered the point. If they laugh, sigh or say
*wow* at the end, the ending landed. If they wait, then say *and?*, it didn't.
