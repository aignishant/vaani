## One-line answer

Record a real retold story on purpose with no frame and with the teller's *I* left in. Play it to a
listener, then ask *who did that happen to?* A story that keeps the teller's *I* doesn't sound like
a grammar slip. It becomes your story, and the listener believes it.

## The story

Your neighbour told you her purse was stolen on the bus, and that she chased the thief two stops on
foot and got it back.

At work, you retell it. You're excited, and you start in her words:

> I was on the 45 bus and a boy took my purse …

Two sentences later your colleague is holding your arm:

> Are you OK? Did you go to the police?

You laugh and say *no, no, not me, my neighbour*. But for the rest of the day people keep asking
you about your purse.

Two small words were missing, *she* and *my neighbour told me*, and the story moved from her life
into yours.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **frame** and **teller**: the first line that says who told you, and the person who did, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-whose-story-it-is.md) and [part 1.2](1.2-retell-two-stories-you-heard.md).
- **the teller's *I***: the *I* in the story as it was told to you, which means the teller, not you.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A listener works out whose story it is from two things only: the frame at the start, and the
pronoun (*I* or *she*) all the way through. Take both away and the listener has only one
possible answer: *it happened to the person talking to me*.

| What you say | Who the listener thinks it happened to |
| --- | --- |
| frame and *she* throughout | the teller |
| frame, then the teller's *I* slips back | unclear; they ask |
| no frame, the teller's *I* throughout | you |

## Why Vaani needs it

In the phase 3 gate recording, a stranger hears one voice, yours, telling a story from your life.
If a retold piece gets into it, say *my father told me that when he was young …*, the pronouns are
the only thing keeping his life and yours apart. Hearing that go wrong once, on purpose, is how you
learn to listen for it.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:** [tell](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/tell): "To
narrate, to recount", past form "told". *Tell* names the teller: *she told me*.

**Step one: choose one real retold story** from [part 1.2](1.2-retell-two-stories-you-heard.md), or
a new one.

**Step two: record three versions:**

1. **A**: frame and *he* or *she* all the way through, the right version;
2. **B**: no frame, and the teller's *I* all the way through, deliberately wrong;
3. **C**: the frame, then the teller's *I* slipping back in the middle, deliberately wrong.

**Step three: the whose-story test.** Play *one* version each to three different listeners who
don't know the story. After it, ask:

> Who did that happen to?

Write down their answer, and anything they asked you.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when version A's listener doesn't name the
teller. That means your right version isn't clear yet. It is also RED if B's listener names the
teller correctly, because then the deliberate version didn't really keep the *I*. It is **GREEN**
when A's listener names the teller at once, and B's listener says it happened to you.

**Step five: the repair drill.** Take version C. Find the sentence where the *I* came back. Say that
sentence three times with *he* or *she*, then record the whole story once more.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a story nobody would believe was yours**, like
your grandfather's war years when you are twenty-five. The listener sorts it out from common sense.
Choose a story that could have happened to you.

The second is **one listener for all three versions**. After version A, they know the truth. Use a
new listener for each.

The third, in real speech, is **the slip at the exciting moment**. People keep *she* through the
calm part, and the teller's *I* comes back at the climax: *and then I ran after him!* Listen for
it there.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A listener who believes a retold story is yours won't correct you. They'll react as if it were your
life: with worry, sympathy, questions or advice. If you ever get a reaction that seems too big for
the story, check your pronouns.

Under pressure the frame is the first thing people drop, because they want to get to the good
part. Two words fix it: *my neighbour*. Say them before anything else.

Some languages don't need a pronoun in every sentence, or mark whose story it is in a different
way. If yours does, the teller's *I* may slip in easily in English. The recorder is how you catch
it.
