## One-line answer

To retell a story in your own words, read it twice and then close it. Write a few short notes from
memory, write the retelling from your notes only, and then open the original again to check every
name, number and event. Your sentences are new. The facts stay exactly the same.

## The story

A long message is going round your family group: a story about a man in your grandfather's village
who found a bag of money on a bus and walked eleven kilometres to return it to the owner.

Your uncle doesn't read long messages. He asks you: *what's this story everybody is sending?*

Your first try is to copy the message and cut bits out. It comes out strange: half the sentences are
the message's, half are yours, and one important sentence has gone missing, the one where the owner
offered a reward and he said no.

So you read it twice, put the phone face down, and write five short notes from memory: *man, bus,
bag of money*, *no name in the bag*, *found an address*, *walked eleven km*, *said no to the reward*.
Then you write to your uncle from the notes alone:

> A man from Amma's village found a bag of money on a bus. There was no name in it, only an
> address, so he walked eleven kilometres to return it. The owner offered him a reward, and he said
> no.

Then you turn the phone over and check. Eleven, yes. Bus, yes. Your uncle replies: *Good man.* He
got the whole story in three sentences.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **retell in your own words**: to write a story again with your own sentences, keeping all the
  facts.
- **original**: the story as you first read or heard it.
- **notes**: short separate phrases written before the real piece, from
  [day 56](../../../day-56-explaining-why/parts/02-writing/2.2-join-your-own-day.md).
- **fact**: something true that could be proved to a stranger, from
  [day 7](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-true-fact-per-sentence.md). Here: a
  name, a number, a place, what happened.
- **copy**: to write the same words as the original.

Retelling in your own words has two halves, and each has its own rule:

| Half | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| the **words** | yours: new sentences, usually fewer of them |
| the **facts** | theirs: exactly the same names, numbers, places and events, in the same order |

The easiest way to make the words yours is to *not be looking* at the original while you write.
The easiest way to keep the facts theirs is to *check* against the original afterwards.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past. A retelling is the same skill as writing your own
story, with the facts supplied for you, so you can practise shape and sentences without also having
to remember your life. Later, in phase 6, you'll write a summary of an article, and that is the
same skill made formal.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:** [Paraphrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraphrase).
It opens:

> A paraphrase or rephrase is a rewording of a text that retains the original meaning.

It also warns that changing single words is not enough: "it is important to teach students how to
restructure sentences to avoid plagiarism". *Plagiarism* means passing off somebody else's writing
as your own.

**Step one: read the original twice.** The first time for the story, the second time for the facts.

**Step two: close it.** Put the page away, or turn the phone over.

**Step three: write notes from memory.** One line for each event, three to five words each. No full
sentences.

**Step four: write the retelling from your notes.** Start with a frame that says where the story
comes from:

> There's a story going round the family group about …
> I read in the local paper that …

Then the events, in the past, in order. Keep it shorter than the original.

**Step five: open the original and check the facts.** Every name, every number, every place and
the order of events. Fix any fact that's wrong. Don't fix the sentences to look like the original.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the copy with small changes**: the original's sentences with a
few words swapped.

> Original: *He walked eleven kilometres in the heat to return the bag.*
> Retelling: *He walked eleven kilometres in the hot weather to give back the bag.*

The reader who knows the original sees the same sentence wearing a different shirt.

The second is **the changed fact**. You wrote from memory and didn't check, so eleven kilometres
became twelve, or the bus became a train. The sentences are yours, and the facts are wrong.

The third is **the retelling with no frame**. It starts *A man found a bag of money*, and your
uncle thinks you're telling him something you saw.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

People retell in writing all the time: a message summarising what the school sent, a note to a
colleague about what the manager said, a post about a story in the news. Good retellings are
short, framed and checked.

Under pressure, busy and on a phone, people copy and paste instead, and then the reader gets a
long message nobody reads. Three sentences in your own words usually do more.

In formal writing, at school, at work or in any application, copying somebody's sentences and
calling them yours is taken seriously. The close-the-page method protects you from doing it
without meaning to.
