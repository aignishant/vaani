## One-line answer

Take a real story of two hundred words or more and retell it for a real reader in about a hundred
words, from notes, with the page closed. Then check the facts in a table and ask your reader one
question about the ending. If the facts match and the reader gets the ending, you've retold it.

## The story

Your nephew's school has sent a story home, a page and a half about a girl who taught her
grandmother to read. His homework is to tell it to somebody at home. He tells you, badly, and asks
you to write it for his grandfather, who missed it.

You read the page twice. You notice the facts that matter: the girl is eleven, her grandmother is
seventy, they practise with the labels on spice tins, and at the end the grandmother reads a letter
from her son in another city without help.

You close the book, write six notes, and write a hundred words for his grandfather. Then you open
the book and check: *seventy*, yes; *spice tins*, yes; *a letter from her son*, not her daughter.
You had it wrong. You fix it.

His grandfather reads it and says, *Like my mother. She learned from my sister.* The story reached
him, in your words, with the right facts.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **retell in your own words**, **original**, **fact**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-close-the-page-then-write.md).
- **fact check**: going through the original to confirm every fact you wrote.
- **word count**: how many words a piece of writing has, counted.
- **cut**: to leave out a part that isn't needed.

A retelling is shorter than the original, usually about half or less. What you cut is description,
repetition and the small side events. What you keep is the frame, who, where, the problem, what
happened, and the ending.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate page is a one-page story. The hardest part of a one-page story is choosing what to
leave out, and a retelling makes you do exactly that with somebody else's facts, where you can
check yourself.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:** [Paraphrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraphrase):
"a rewording of a text that retains the original meaning".

**Step one: choose a real original** of two hundred words or more that a real person hasn't read. It
could be a news item from the local paper, a story in a schoolbook, a long forwarded message, or a
notice from your building. Name your reader.

**Step two: read twice, close it, write notes.** One line per event, three to five words each.

**Step three: write the retelling** from the notes: about a hundred words, a frame first, in the past,
in order, ending on the ending. Count the words and write the number at the bottom.

**Step four: fact check.** Open the original and fill the table.

| Fact | In the original | In my retelling | Same? |

| where | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| each number | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| the problem | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| the ending | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

Fix every *no*. Change the fact only, not the sentence around it.

**Step five: read it once for sentences.** Capital letters, full stops, past forms, and no sentence
that runs on.

**Step six: give it to your reader** and ask:

> How did it end?

If their answer matches the original's ending, the retelling worked.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **cutting the ending** to save words, and keeping the long description
at the start. The reader gets the setting and not the point. Cut from the middle, never from the end.

The second is **the retelling as long as the original**. Nothing was cut, so it's really the same
story rewritten sentence by sentence, which usually means you were looking at it.

The third is **the fact check that fixes sentences**. You open the original to check a number and,
while you're there, copy the nicer sentence too. Check facts only.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Newspapers, school reports and work emails are full of retold stories. The good ones are much
shorter than the original and just as accurate, and they are always clear about where the story
came from.

Under pressure people check nothing, and one wrong number in a retold story (*the rent goes up by
five thousand* instead of *five hundred*) causes real trouble. The table in step four is small, and it
prevents it.

A reader of a retelling trusts the facts in it, because they assume you checked. Make that true.
