## One-line answer

Write a second retelling of your real story on purpose with the original open in front of you,
changing a word here and there. Then give it and your real retelling to a reader with the original
and ask them to underline every run of five words that match. A copy with small changes doesn't
look like your writing. It looks like the original with a few words crossed out.

## The story

Your nephew has a school project: retell a story from the newspaper. He writes his version with the
newspaper open beside him, changing a word in each sentence:

> Original: *The fire started in the kitchen of a small restaurant late on Saturday night.*
> His: *The fire began in the kitchen of a little restaurant late on Saturday night.*

His teacher puts the two side by side and underlines *in the kitchen of a*, and *restaurant late on
Saturday night*. Half the sentence is underlined. She writes: *Your own words, please.*

He tries again the next day, with the paper closed:

> On Saturday night, a small restaurant caught fire. It started in the kitchen.

Nothing to underline. Same facts, his sentences.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **original**, **retell in your own words**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-close-the-page-then-write.md).
- **run**: here, several words in a row that are the same, in the same order, as the original.
- **synonym**: a word with the same or nearly the same meaning: *began* for *started*.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A copy with small changes keeps the **shape** of every sentence: the same order, the same length,
and the same small words in between. A reader sees the shape before the words. That's why changing
words isn't enough. A retelling has new sentences, not new words in old sentences.

| Kind | What a reader with the original sees |
| --- | --- |
| a copy with synonyms swapped in | the same sentences, with some words crossed out |
| a real retelling | the same facts, in different and usually shorter sentences |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate page is your own story, and later phases ask for summaries, reports and letters in
your own words. A writer who has never seen their copying underlined doesn't know they do it. This
part shows you once, on your own work.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:** [Paraphrase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paraphrase).
It says that swapping in words with the same meaning isn't enough on its own: "Some believe that
using synonyms in paraphrasing can be a helpful learning tool; however, it is important to teach
students how to restructure sentences to avoid plagiarism."

**Step one: write the deliberate copy.** Take the real original from
[part 2.2](2.2-retell-a-real-story-on-paper.md). With the original open, write version **C**: go
through it sentence by sentence and swap one or two words in each for a synonym. Label it
*deliberate*.

**Step two: take your real retelling**, version **R**, from part 2.2, written with the page closed.

**Step three: the underline test.** Give a reader the original, C and R, and a pen. Ask:

> Can you underline every place where five or more words in a row are the same as the original?

Names, numbers and titles don't count. *Eleven kilometres* is a fact, not copying.

**Step four: fill the table.**

| C (deliberate copy) | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| R (my retelling) | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader underlines any run of five
or more in R, your real retelling. It is also RED when they underline nothing in C, because then
the copy wasn't a copy. It is **GREEN** when C is full of runs and R has none.

**Step five: the repair.** For every run underlined in R, close the original, read your own notes,
and write that sentence again in a different shape: shorter, joined differently, or in a different
order. Give the repaired R to a second reader with the original and ask the same question.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who reads only your version**. Without the
original next to it, a copy looks like fine writing. The test needs both on the table.

The second is **counting names as copying**. *The Sharma Sweet House on Station Road* has to stay
the same. Facts aren't runs.

The third, in real writing, is **the one sentence you liked too much**. The retelling is all yours
except for the one beautiful sentence from the original, which you kept word for word. It's usually
the sentence a reader notices first.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Teachers, editors and managers spot copying mostly by shape, not by searching: a sentence that
sounds like somebody else wrote it, in the middle of a page that sounds like you.

Under pressure, a deadline for an application or a report, the temptation is to lift sentences and
swap a few words. It looks easier, and it costs trust. The close-the-page method is also the
easier way in the end.

If you really want someone's exact words, use them as a quote, in speech marks, with their name.
That's honest. A copy wearing synonyms isn't.
