## One-line answer

To talk about a mistake you made, say three things in order: *I made a mistake, I did this*, then
*and because of it, this happened*, then *so now I always do this*. Owning it, the cost and the
change are what make a listener trust you rather than feel sorry for you.

## The story

A new colleague starts at your office. In her first week, she asks you quietly at lunch:

> Did you make mistakes when you started?

You could say *oh, lots*, and laugh. Instead you tell her one:

> Yes. In my second week I sent a customer's bill to the wrong email address. Another customer
> saw all his details, and my manager had to phone both of them to say sorry. So now I always
> check the address twice before I press send.

She's quiet for a moment, and then says:

> Thank you. I'll check twice too.

You gave her a real mistake, what it cost, and what you do now, and she can use all three.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **mistake**: something you did wrong, usually by accident.
- **make a mistake**: the English phrase for it. You *make* a mistake. You don't *do* one.
- **cost**: here, the bad thing that happened because of the mistake.
- **the change**: what you do differently now because of it.
- **own it**: to say clearly that *you* did it, with *I*.
- **beat**: one step in a spoken story, from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-went-wrong-and-what-you-did.md).

On day 48 you told problem stories: something went wrong *to* you, and you did something about it.
A mistake story is different. Here the thing that went wrong was *you*. That changes what the
listener wants to hear.

A mistake story has three beats:

| Beat | What it says | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. what I did | the mistake, with *I*, in the past | *I sent the bill to the wrong address.* |
| 2. what it cost | what happened because of it | *Another customer saw his details.* |
| 3. what I do now | the change, in the present | *Now I always check the address twice.* |

Beat three changes tense: from the past to the present. *Now I always …* tells the listener this
is a habit today, not just a story.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a story from your life, and many of the best ones are mistakes. In phase 5,
*tell me about a mistake you made* is one of the standard interview questions, and the three beats
here are what an interviewer is listening for.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[make a mistake](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/make_a_mistake). It defines the phrase as "To err,
to be wrong", with the past form "made a mistake".

**Beat one opens with *I*.** Say the mistake plainly:

> I made a mistake with a customer's bill.
> I forgot my sister's birthday last year.

Push the word that carries the mistake: *I forgot my sister's BIRTHday*.

**Beat two is one or two sentences, not ten.** The cost is what makes the mistake real. Keep it
short and concrete: who was affected, and how.

**Beat three starts with *so now* or *since then*.** Then the present simple and a how-often word:

> So now I always check twice.
> Since then, I never leave it to the last day.

**Don't beat yourself up in the middle.** One short line about how you felt is fine:

> I felt terrible.

After that, go straight on to beat three.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **do a mistake**:

> I did a mistake in my second week.

A listener understands, but it's one of the first things a native speaker notices, because the
phrase is fixed: *make a mistake*, *made a mistake*.

The second is **the missing beat three**. You tell the mistake and the cost and stop. The listener
is left holding something bad about you, with nothing to balance it. They say *oh dear* and
change the subject.

The third is **the mistake with no *I***:

> The bill went to the wrong address.

That's how people talk when they don't want to own it, and a listener hears it straight away.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

People who tell mistake stories well are usually trusted more afterwards, not less. The three beats
say *I know what I did, I know what it cost, and I've changed*. That is exactly what a manager, an
interviewer or a friend wants to hear.

Under pressure, for example when someone has just found your mistake, people jump to excuses and
leave out *I*. Start with *I* even then: *I made a mistake. I sent it to the wrong address.* It
stops the argument before it starts.

A native listener notices tone as much as words. A mistake told flatly and quickly sounds like you
don't care. Told slowly, with one real pause after the cost, it sounds honest.
