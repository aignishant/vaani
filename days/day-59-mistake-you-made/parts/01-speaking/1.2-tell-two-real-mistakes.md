## One-line answer

Tell two real mistakes, a small one and a bigger one, to two real people, using the three beats.
Then ask each listener to say back what you did and what you do now. If they can say both, your
mistake story worked.

## The story

Your new colleague is having a bad week. On Thursday she forgot to save a long document and lost
it. She's sitting at her desk looking at the screen.

You tell her a small one first, to make her smile:

> I once made tea for the whole office with salt instead of sugar. Six people tasted it at the same
> time. Now I taste it first.

She laughs. Then you tell her a bigger one:

> In my first year I lost a whole day's work, just like you. I didn't save anything. I had to stay
> until ten at night to do it again. Since then, I save after every page. It's automatic now.

She asks: *How do you remember to save?* The mistake story didn't just comfort her. It gave her
something to do.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the three beats**: what I did, what it cost, what I do now, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-i-did-what-it-cost-what-i-do-now.md).
- **make a mistake**: the fixed phrase, past *made a mistake*, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-i-did-what-it-cost-what-i-do-now.md).
- **size**: how big a mistake is: tiny, medium or serious.
- **say back**: when a listener tells you in their own words what they understood.

The size of the mistake changes the tone, not the shape. A tiny mistake can be told with a smile. A
serious one needs a slower voice and a real pause after the cost. Both have all three beats.

| Size | Tone | The pause after the cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| tiny | light, you can smile | short |
| serious | slower, lower | a real one, a breath or more |

**Step four: listen back** for three things: an *I* in the first sentence, no *did a mistake*, and a

**Step five: tell each one to a different real person** in a real moment, when they're having a
problem or when the talk turns to mistakes. Afterwards, ask:

> What did I do wrong, and what do I do now?

Write their answer down as they say it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the mistake that isn't one**: *my biggest mistake is working too
hard*. Everyone hears that as a boast. Choose a real mistake with a real cost.

The second is **the long cost**: five sentences about how angry your manager was. The listener starts
feeling bad for you, and beat three gets lost. One or two sentences for the cost.

The third is **the wrong size of tone**: a serious mistake told with a laugh, which sounds like you
don't care, or a tiny one told slowly and heavily, which sounds strange.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Sharing a mistake is one of the fastest ways to make somebody trust you. It's why experienced people
often tell new people about their own early mistakes. Those stories teach and comfort at the same
time.

Under pressure, in an interview for example, people either pick a fake mistake or tell a real one
without beat three. The interviewer is listening for beat three more than anything.

A native listener often answers a mistake story with one of their own. That's a very good sign. It
means your story made them feel safe.
