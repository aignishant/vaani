## One-line answer

Tell one real mistake on purpose in the vague way (*I made some mistakes with money when I was
younger*) and once with the three beats, to two different listeners, and ask each *what exactly did
I do?* A vague mistake doesn't sound modest. It sounds like you're hiding something, and the
listener can't say what it was.

## The story

At a family dinner, your uncle asks your cousin, who has just started his first job:

> Any advice for your younger brother?

Your cousin says:

> Well, I made a lot of mistakes in my first year. Things with money, and with people. You learn.

Everyone nods. Nobody learns anything. His younger brother asks, *like what?*, and your cousin says
*oh, you know, just things.*

Later, in the kitchen, he tells you the real one: he lent his first month's salary to a friend who
never paid it back, and he had to borrow from his father for rent. *So now I never lend more than I
can lose.*

That was the advice. He had it all along. He just didn't say it at the table.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **vague**: not clear or exact, so the listener can't picture it.
- **concrete**: clear and exact enough to picture: a number, a name, a place, a day.
- **the three beats**: what I did, what it cost, what I do now, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-i-did-what-it-cost-what-i-do-now.md).
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A vague mistake leaves out every beat that would let the listener picture it:

| Vague | Concrete |
| --- | --- |
| *some mistakes* | *I lent my first salary to a friend* |
| *things with money* | *he never paid it back, and I had to borrow rent* |
| *you learn* | *now I never lend more than I can lose* |

People go vague to protect themselves, and it has the opposite effect. The listener fills the gap
with something worse, or decides you aren't being honest.

## Why Vaani needs it

At the phase 3 gate, and at the phase 5 interview, *tell me about a mistake* answered vaguely is a
failed answer. Hearing, on purpose, that a listener can't say back a vague mistake is how you learn
to hear yourself going vague.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[make a mistake](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/make_a_mistake): "To err, to be wrong", past form
"made a mistake".

**Step one: choose one real mistake** from [part 1.2](1.2-tell-two-real-mistakes.md), or a new one.

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **V**, deliberately vague: *some mistakes*, *things*, *you know*, no cost you can picture, and *you
   learn* in place of beat three.
2. **C**, concrete: the three beats, with one thing the listener can picture in each.

**Step three: the say-back test.** Play V to one listener and C to a different listener. Neither
should know the story. Ask each:

> What exactly did I do, and what do I do differently now?

Write down every word of their answer.

**Step four: fill the table.**

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| V | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| C | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when C's listener can't say back both your
mistake and your change. That means your concrete version still isn't concrete enough. It is also
RED if V's listener *can* say it back, because then the vague version wasn't really vague. It is
**GREEN** when C's listener says back both and V's listener can't.

**Step five: the repair.** If C failed, find the beat the listener couldn't say back and add one
picture to it: a number, a name, a place, a day. Record it and try it on a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a vague version that tells the story anyway**. You
can't stop yourself adding the detail. Write V down first and stick to it.

The second is **one listener for both versions**. After C, they know. Use two people.

The third, in real speech, is **going vague at the cost**. Beat one is clear and beat three is
clear, but the middle is *and it caused some problems*. The listener doesn't believe the change
without the cost.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Interviewers are trained to hear vague mistakes. Their next question is always *can you give me a
specific example?*, and if the second answer is vague too, the interview is over.

Among friends and family, vagueness just wastes the story. The younger brother at the table needed
the lent salary, not *things with money*.

Under pressure, when the mistake is embarrassing, choose a smaller mistake and tell it concretely,
rather than a big one told vaguely. A small real one is worth more.
