## One-line answer

Write *used to* plus the base form of a verb for something that was true, or happened again and
again, in the past and isn't true now: *I used to live in Nagpur*. After *did* and *didn't*, the *d*
disappears: *I didn't use to*, *Did you use to?*

## The story

You're sorting old family photos, and your daughter wants to know about each one. You decide to
write a line on the back of each photo, so the story stays with it.

A photo of a small blue house:

> We used to live here. Your grandfather used to grow tomatoes by the gate.

A photo of you at twelve, very thin, on a bicycle:

> I used to ride this bicycle to school every day. I didn't use to like school much.

Your daughter reads them all. Then she asks: *Do you still ride a bicycle?* You laugh. The photo
line said *used to*, and she heard it right: *you did then, and you don't now*. Two words carried
the whole *then and now*.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **base form**: the plain form of a verb, with nothing added: *live*, *go*, *be*, from
  [day 42](../../../day-42-three-sounds-of-ed/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-verbs-that-refuse-ed.md).
- **auxiliary**: a helper verb like *do*, *does* or *did* that makes questions and negatives, from
  [day 44](../../../day-44-time-words-aloud/parts/02-writing/2.1-did-takes-the-past-away.md).
- **habit**: something you did again and again: every day, every week.
- **state**: something that was simply true for a time: where you lived, what you liked, what you
  were.
- **then and now**: the difference between the past and today.

*Used to* does one job: it says **this was true then, and it isn't true now**. It works for two
kinds of past:

| Kind | Example | Now |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a **habit**, done again and again | *I used to ride to school every day.* | I don't any more. |
| a **state**, simply true | *We used to live in the blue house.* | We live somewhere else. |

With the plain past simple, *I rode to school*, the reader doesn't know if you still do. With *used
to*, they do.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate is a one-page story in the past. Most real stories begin with how things used to
be before something changed: *we used to live near the river, until the flood*. And the whole of
the phase 4 writing about change, opinion and experience uses *used to* to set up the *before*.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Past habits: 'used to', 'would' and the past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/past-habits-used-to-would-past-simple)
says: "We can use *used to* to talk about past states that are not true any more", and "We can also
use *used to* to talk about past habits (repeated past actions) that don't happen any more". Its
examples:

> They used to live in London.
> I didn't use to like olives.
> Did you use to have a garden?

[Different uses of 'used to'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/different-uses-of-used-to):
"We use *used to* + infinitive to talk about a past situation that is no longer true." *Infinitive*
here means the base form.

**The three shapes on paper:**

| Shape | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yes | subject + *used to* + base form | *I used to walk to work.* |
| no | subject + *didn't use to* + base form | *I didn't use to walk to work.* |
| question | *Did* + subject + *use to* + base form + ? | *Did you use to walk to work?* |

**The missing *d*.** In the yes-sentence you write *used*, with a *d*. After *did* or *didn't*, you
write *use*, with no *d*. It works the same way as any past verb after *did*: *I walked*, but *I
didn't walk*. On
[day 44](../../../day-44-time-words-aloud/parts/02-writing/2.1-did-takes-the-past-away.md) you saw
that *did* takes the past away from the verb. It takes the *d* away from *used* too.

**The same for everyone.** *I used to*, *she used to*, *they used to*. No *-s*, ever.

**The verb after it is always the base form.** *Used to live*, *used to go*, *used to be*. Never
*used to lived* or *used to went*.

**Only for the past.** There is no present *use to*. For a habit today, write the present simple:
*I usually walk to work.*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the missing *d* in the yes-sentence**:

> I use to live in Nagpur.

A reader who knows English reads it as a mistake at once. It isn't a word shape English has.

The second is **the extra *d* after *didn't***:

> I didn't used to like school.

This one is very common, even among native writers, but the reference page's form is *didn't use
to*, and a careful reader marks it.

The third is **the past verb after it**:

> We used to went to the river on Sundays.

*Used* is already the past. The verb after it stays in the base form: *used to go*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

*Used to* is everywhere in real writing about people's lives: letters, messages to old friends,
captions under photos, the first line of a memory. Short and plain: *I used to live here.*

Under pressure, people drop the *d* in writing because it disappears in speech. *Used to* sounds
the same as *use to* when said quickly. The ear can't tell you, so the eye has to check.

A formal reader, a teacher or an employer reading an application, notices *use to* in a yes
sentence and *used to went* straight away. They're small, and they're marked every time.
