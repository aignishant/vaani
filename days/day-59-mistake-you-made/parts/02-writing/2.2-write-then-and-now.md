## One-line answer

Write a real *then and now* paragraph about one part of your life: what you used to do, what you
didn't use to do, and what is true today. Use *used to* for then and the present simple for now,
and check every *used* and *use* one at a time.

## The story

Your old school friend finds you online after fifteen years and writes: *What's your life like now?
Remember when we used to sit on the wall behind the bus stand?*

You want to answer properly. So you choose one part of your life, your mornings, and write it then
and now:

> We used to sit on that wall every morning before school! I used to eat a samosa there instead of
> breakfast. I didn't use to drink tea at all.
>
> Now I get up at six, I drink two cups of tea before I say a word, and I never eat samosas for
> breakfast.

Your friend writes back: *Two cups of tea! You used to hate tea!* The *then* and the *now* were so
clear that she could see the change and laugh at it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **used to**, **didn't use to**, **did you use to**: the three shapes from
  [part 2.1](2.1-used-to-and-the-missing-d.md).
- **present simple**: the verb form for what is true or usual now: *I drink*, *she works*, from
  [day 15](../../../day-15-daily-routine/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-s-on-he-she-it.md).
- **how-often word**: *always*, *usually*, *never*, from
  [day 15](../../../day-15-daily-routine/parts/02-writing/2.2-always-usually-never.md).
- **contrast**: setting one thing against another, here then against now.

A *then and now* paragraph has two halves. The first half is in *used to*. The second is in the
present simple. The contrast is the point, so the same subjects should appear in both halves:
mornings then, mornings now.

| Half | Verb form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| then | *used to*, *didn't use to* + base form | *I didn't use to drink tea.* |
| now | present simple, often with a how-often word | *Now I always drink two cups.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 3 gate page is a story in the past, and stories often start by describing how life used
to be. This part is that opening, practised on its own, with the change at the end made clear.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Past habits: 'used to', 'would' and the past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/past-habits-used-to-would-past-simple):
*used to* is for "past states that are not true any more" and "past habits (repeated past actions)
that don't happen any more", with "I didn't use to like olives" and "Did you use to have a garden?"

**Step one: choose one part of your life** that has really changed. It could be your mornings, where
you lived, your work, what you ate, how you travelled, or who your friends were. Choose a real
reader who knew you then, or who would like to know.

**Step two: make two lists** of three or four items each.

Try to match the rows: the same subject then and now.

**Step three: write the *then* paragraph.** Four to six sentences, with at least three *used to*
and one *didn't use to*.

**Step four: write the *now* paragraph.** Start with *Now* or *These days*. Present simple, at
least one how-often word. Show the change on the same subjects.

**Step five: the *used* check.** Circle every *used* and *use*. For each circle, ask:

- Is there a *did* or *didn't* in front of it? Then it's *use*, with no *d*.
- If not, it's *used*, with a *d*.
- Is the next word *to* and then a base form?

**Step six: add one question** for your reader at the end: *Did you use to …?*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***used to* in both halves**: *I used to drink tea, now I used to
drink coffee*. *Used to* can't describe now. The *now* half is present simple.

The second is **no contrast**. Then: mornings. Now: your job. The reader sees two paragraphs about
different things and no change. Match the subjects.

The third is **every sentence *used to***. Six *used to*s in a row read like a list. Once the
reader knows you're talking about then, one sentence can be past simple: *We used to sit on the wall.
One day, a bus nearly hit it.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had
rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

*Then and now* writing is how people reconnect: messages to old friends, the paragraph in a family
letter, a post under an old photo. It's warm, it's easy to answer, and it invites the other person
to write their own.

Under pressure, a short message typed fast, people write only the *now*, and the reader who knew
them then misses the change. One *used to* sentence first makes the *now* mean something.

A reader notices a wrong *used* on paper every time, because the sound gives nothing away. The
circle check in step five is the whole defence.
