## One-line answer

Plant four *used to* errors in a copy of your real *then and now* page. Include *use to* for
something you still do today, which tells the reader the wrong fact: that it stopped. Ask a reader
what is still true about you now. A wrong *used to* doesn't only look wrong. It can change what the
reader believes about your life.

## The story

You write a short profile for a new job application. One line says:

> I use to work on weekends when the shop is busy.

You meant: *I usually work on weekends*, and you still do. The manager reads it as *I used to work on
weekends*, a habit you gave up, and writes a note: *not available weekends*. You don't get the
weekend shifts you wanted.

*Use to* isn't a way to say *usually*. The reader had to guess, and guessed *used to*, the only real
English that looks like it.

Another line in the same profile:

> I used to managed the stock room.

Nobody misreads that. But the manager circles it in red, and it's the first thing she says in the
interview.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They
are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **plant**: to put an error into a text on purpose, from
  [day 46](../../../day-46-describing-a-trip/parts/02-writing/2.3-there-was-two-beds.md).
- **used to**, **didn't use to**: the shapes from [part 2.1](2.1-used-to-and-the-missing-d.md).
- **present simple**: the verb form for what is true or usual now, from
  [day 15](../../../day-15-daily-routine/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-s-on-he-she-it.md).
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The four common errors are not equally dangerous:

| Error | Example | What the reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *use to* for a habit now | *I use to work on weekends.* | the wrong fact: that you stopped |
| no *d* in a yes-sentence | *I use to live in Pune.* | an error, meaning mostly clear |
| extra *d* after *didn't* | *I didn't used to like tea.* | a small error a careful reader marks |
| past verb after *used to* | *I used to managed the stock room.* | an error, meaning clear |

The first is the one that changes facts, so it's the one to hunt hardest.

## Why Vaani needs it

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Past habits: 'used to', 'would' and the past simple](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/past-habits-used-to-would-past-simple):
*used to* is for things "that are not true any more" and habits "that don't happen any more", and
the negative is "I didn't use to like olives".
[Different uses of 'used to'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/different-uses-of-used-to):
"We use *used to* + infinitive to talk about a past situation that is no longer true."

**Step one: take your real page** from [part 2.2](2.2-write-then-and-now.md). Keep a clean copy.

**Step two: plant four errors** in a new copy, one of each kind in the table, and keep a hidden list.
The first must be in the *now* half: change one present-simple sentence about something you still do
into *I use to …*.

**Step three: the what's-true test.** Give the planted copy to a reader who doesn't know you well.
Ask:

> From this page, what does this person still do today?

Write their list down. Then ask them to circle anything that looks wrong.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader leaves your planted *use to*
habit out of their *still does today* list, because then the error changed the facts. It is also RED
when they miss two or more planted errors. It is **GREEN** when a second reader, given the repaired
copy, lists every present habit correctly and circles nothing.

**Step five: repair.** Fix all four. The *now* sentence goes back to the present simple with a
how-often word: *I usually work on weekends.*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **planting the *use to* in the *then* half**. There it
reads as a spelling slip of *used to*, and it doesn't change any fact. It has to be in the *now* half,
about something still true.

The second is **a reader who knows your life**. They'll correct the fact from memory. Choose
somebody who doesn't.

The third, in real writing, is ***use to* as a word for *usually***. It's a very common beginner
pattern, and there is no such meaning in English. For a habit now, write *I usually*, *I always* or
*I often*, or just the present simple: *I work on weekends*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Forms, profiles, applications and cover letters are where *use to* does real damage, because the
reader is deciding something about you from each line: *available weekends or not*, *still does this
or not*.

Under pressure, filling in a form quickly, people write what they hear, and *used to* and *use to*
sound the same. The fix is one rule you can check with your eyes: **no *did*, then *used*. *Did* in
front, then *use*. Anything true now, no *use* at all.**

Native writers make the *didn't used to* slip too, and many readers forgive it. Nobody forgives a
wrong fact on an application.
