# The empty hand

`IN-14` · Day 59

## The one-line answer

Somebody asks you for something and your hand is empty, and there are exactly three honest
things to do with an empty hand — none of which is closing your fist so it looks full.

## The model

Somebody asks you for something. You reach, and there is nothing there. Your hand comes back
empty and everybody is looking at it.

There are three honest moves, and they are all good moves. Then there is a fourth thing people do
instead, which is to close their fingers around nothing, hold it out confidently, and hope
nobody looks until later. That fourth thing is today's failure and we will come to it.

First, why the fourth thing happens at all. It is not dishonesty. It is that a question creates a
hole in the air and your mouth is trained to fill holes. In a second language the pull is stronger,
because *I don't know* sounds, from inside your own head, like it might be heard as *I don't
understand the question* or *my English ran out*. It is not heard as either of those. But the fear
of it is why people talk.

So: three routes, and each one has two halves. **The second half is the whole difference.** A route
without its second half is a dead end, and a dead end is where bluffing gets invented.

**Route one — you don't know, and here's what you'll do.**

Day 4 gave you *I'll get back to you on that*, and it is still a good sentence, but on its own it
is a promise with nothing inside it. Everybody has heard that sentence and had nothing come back.
Put something in it: who, or how, or when.

> I don't know — but I know who does.
> What I can do is find out — {{by when}}.
> That's outside what I've worked on.

*That's outside what I've worked on* is a drawn line, and you say it flat and unapologetically.
It is not a confession. It is information they needed and did not have — they were asking because
they didn't know where the edge of your knowledge was, and now they do. That is a useful answer to
a question, which is what they wanted.

**Route two — answer the part you can.**

Most unanswerable questions are not fully unanswerable. There is a slice you can speak to. The
danger is that answering a slice, without saying so, is indistinguishable from dodging — you sound
like a person who heard the question and answered a different one on purpose.

So name the slice out loud. That single act converts it from evasion into an answer.

> I can answer half of that.
> The part I can speak to is {{…}} — the rest I'd only be guessing at.

**Route three — ask them to sharpen it.**

This is the one people skip, and it is right more often than either of the others. A question you
cannot answer is very often a question you have not understood — too broad, or two questions
wearing one coat, or a word in it you took the wrong way. Sending it back is not stalling. It is
the fastest route to the answer, for both of you.

> Are you asking {{X}} or {{Y}}?
> What would you do with the answer?

That second one is the sharpest tool in this day. It asks what they actually need, and once you
know that, the question usually shrinks to something you *can* answer. Somebody asking a huge
question is often after one small piece of it, and they have wrapped it in the huge version
because they don't know what's in your hand either.

**Choosing between them, fast.** You get no thinking room, so make it mechanical.

- You know nothing about it → route one.
- You know a slice → route two.
- You don't know what they're actually asking → route three.

**The audible rule, and it is countable.** An honest route is short. A bluff is long.

That is not a moral observation, it is mechanics: an honest route has content and stops, and a
bluff has to keep going, because stopping would expose that nothing arrived. So on tonight's tape
there is a measurable thing. **The answer you didn't have should not run longer than the answers
you did.** If it does, you bluffed, whatever your intentions were.

And one more, from your own body: **a bluff ends on a rising pitch.** Day 26 gave you the rise as
a question. When you don't believe your own answer, your voice puts one in at the end without
being asked — you are checking whether it landed. An honest *I don't know* falls at the end,
because there is nothing to check.

**Today's phrases** — the three honest routes, both halves of each. All of these go into
`docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I don't know — but I know who does. | Route one, handing it to somebody who has it |
| What I can do is find out — {{by when}}. | Route one's second half: a promise with something in it |
| That's outside what I've worked on. | Drawing the edge of what you hold, flat and without apology |
| I'd only be guessing. | Naming the bluff a beat before you make it |
| I can answer half of that. | Route two, announced, so it doesn't read as dodging |
| The part I can speak to is {{…}} | Marking exactly which slice you are answering |
| Are you asking {{X}} or {{Y}}? | Route three, splitting a question that is two questions |
| What would you do with the answer? | Route three, finding the small question inside the big one |

## Say it now

Get somebody to write you six hard questions about your own work and shuffle them, or write them
yourself and leave them face down until you draw one. You need to not know what is coming; that is
the rep.

Draw one at a time and answer it out loud with the named route, and nothing else.

1. *I don't know — but I know who does. {{who}}.*
2. *What I can do is find out — {{by when}}.*
3. *That's outside what I've worked on.*
4. *I can answer half of that. The part I can speak to is {{…}}.*
5. *{{the slice, in one sentence}} — the rest I'd only be guessing at.*
6. *Are you asking {{X}} or {{Y}}?*
7. *What would you do with the answer?*

Then run all seven again with one change: start each line **inside a beat** of the question ending.
No *hmm*, no *that's a good question actually* — you have that from Day 9 and today it is a place
to hide. Straight in. Speed is what proves the route was ready rather than assembled, and a route
assembled on the spot is a route you will not reach for in a room.

- **With a partner:** they ask, and their one instruction is to ask a follow-up straight after
  every answer — *right, so what does that mean for {{…}}?* The follow-up is where a bluff
  collapses, and where an honest route holds up fine.

## The deliberate failure

Draw one question you genuinely cannot answer. Then bluff it, on purpose and on tape. Full
confidence, no hedging, no *I think*. Produce an answer the way you would if stopping were not
allowed.

**What breaking sounds like:** play it back and listen for three things, in this order.

It is the longest answer on the recording. Longer than the ones you knew. You can hear that without
counting anything, and it is the tell that shows up first.

It has almost no nouns in it. Honest answers are full of specific things; a bluff is built from
*generally*, *it depends*, *there are a few factors*, *broadly speaking*, and it restates the
question back in new words at least once, because the question is the only material you had.

And the last word goes up. There it is — the rise from Day 26, arriving uninvited, asking the other
person whether that was alright.

Now the uncomfortable part. **While you were saying it, it felt fine.** It felt fluent. It probably
felt like your best English of the session, because bluffing runs on phrases you are very
comfortable with and never once puts you near a word you might not have. That is precisely why
people bluff in a second language more than in their first: it is the most comfortable thing your
mouth can do, and it is the only answer on this tape that is worth nothing to anybody. A closed
fist and a full hand look identical from the outside and sound identical for about a sentence.

Then answer the same question with route one, and hear how short it is.

## When it breaks in the wild

A room with people in it, and somebody senior asks you a direct question about your own work that
you cannot answer. Everybody looks over. Saying *I don't know* right there feels like it costs
something in front of an audience — so you produce something, and it is long, and the person who
asked knows within a sentence that nothing is coming. Now you have spent the not-knowing *and* the
credibility.

**The repair:**

> I'm not going to guess at that in front of everyone. What I can do is find out — {{by when}}.

Saying you will not guess is the strongest available move in a room, and it is the opposite of
what it feels like. A person who names the edge of what they know is a person whose other answers
can now be trusted, and everybody watching does that arithmetic without being told to.
