## One-line answer

The phase 3 speaking gate is a recorded story from your life, with a beginning, a middle and an
end, of at least two hundred spoken words. This part teaches nothing new, because a gate that
teaches isn't a gate.

## The story

At a family wedding, late in the evening, somebody's grandmother turns to you and says:

> Tell me something that happened to you. A real story.

No notes, no second try, no one to help. You tell it, she listens, and at the end she either says
*and then what?* or she laughs, or sighs, or says *that's a good one*.

That's today. Twenty days of this folder have taught the pieces: what you did yesterday, the three
sounds of *-ed*, the three parts a listener waits for, time words, childhood, trips, problem stories,
what somebody said, the long stop between parts, retelling, mistakes. None of it is repeated here,
and none of it is made easier.

What this part does is set things up so that when you press record, there's nothing left to decide.

**This is a rehearsal, and it says so.** This day was written before your error ledger had rows in
it. Until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked, the checks below are the general
ones rather than your own recurring mistakes, and the gate is not yet a gate.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate**: the check at the end of a phase, which the whole phase was for, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-the-gate-asks.md).
- **take**: one recording, from start to finish, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/01-speaking/1.2-record-the-introduction.md).
- **transcript**: every word of a recording, written down as it was said.
- **rehearsal**: a practice of the real thing, done as if it were the real thing.

The gate wording is in the plan's §7, and it's copied here without a word changed:

> Speaking: a recorded story from your life with a beginning, middle and end, at least two hundred
> spoken words.

Three conditions, and all three can be checked.

| Condition | What it means | How it's checked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| from your life | it happened to you: not a film, not a retold story | you can say when and where |
| a beginning, middle and end | setting, problem, ending: the three parts a listener waits for | a stranger names the three parts after one listen |
| at least two hundred spoken words | counted, not guessed | from a transcript of the recording |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the point of the phase. Nineteen days of the past, stories and what happened exist so that
one real story can be carried from its first line to its last without stopping, to a listener who
wasn't there. Until it's recorded, nobody knows whether it can be.

And phase 4 builds on it. An opinion with reasons is a longer turn than a story, and it assumes
you can already hold a listener for two hundred words.

## The rule

There's no new rule today. What follows is the setup.

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Tell a story or personal anecdote](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/tell-story-or-personal-anecdote).
Among its tips:

> Give the background to your story. Say when and where it took place...
> Finish your story or anecdote by saying why it is important to you...

And one of its *don'ts*: "Use a flat or bored voice."

**Choose the story before anything else.** It must be yours, and it must have a problem in it. Good
kinds: a journey that went wrong, a mistake and what you learned, the day something changed, a
misunderstanding with a stranger, a small emergency. A story with no problem in it will run out
before two hundred words.

**Write five lines of notes, no more.**

| Line | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| when and where | `TODO(me)` |
| who | `TODO(me)` |
| the problem | `TODO(me)` |
| what happened next | `TODO(me)` |
| how it ended, and why it matters to you | `TODO(me)` |

**How two hundred words sounds.** Two hundred spoken words is about fifteen to twenty ordinary
sentences. It's short: a story told at a family table is often longer.

**How to count.** After the take, play it back and write every word down, sentence by sentence,
including *um* only if you want to see it. Count the words, not the *um*s. That's your transcript.

```text
   1  It was the summer before last, in Pune.          (10)
   2  I was going to my cousin's wedding by bus.       (9)
   3  ...
                                                  total: ___
```

**What must be in the recording**, because these are what the phase taught:

- the past, with regular and irregular verbs, and the *-ed* sounds;
- a setting, a problem and an ending, with a real stop between them;
- at least two time words;
- at least one thing somebody said;
- at least one *because* or *so*.

**Set the phone down and don't stop it.** One take, recorded straight through. Mistakes stay in.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the story with no problem**:

> Last year I went to Goa. It was very nice. We went to the beach. The food was good.

Four sentences, and nothing more to say. It stops at forty words because nothing happened.

The second is **the retold story**. Your father's story, your friend's, a film. The gate says *from
your life*.

The third is **the stop-and-restart**. A word goes wrong in sentence three, you stop, and you start
again. Nine tries later you have nine openings and no gate. Record through.

The fourth is **counting from memory**. *It felt long, it must be two hundred.* It's usually a
hundred and twenty.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate recording. This day was written before your
own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

A real story told to a real person usually has a setting, a pause before the problem, and a small
laugh or sigh at the end. That's the shape the gate asks you to produce, and that's not an accident.

Under pressure, stories shrink: the setting disappears and the ending is one word. That's worth
knowing, because the recorder is pressure, and the notes are your protection.

What a native listener notices in a story is whether anything happened in it, and whether you
cared. The voice tells them the second.
