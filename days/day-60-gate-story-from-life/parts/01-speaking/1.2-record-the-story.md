## One-line answer

Record the whole story in one take from your five lines of notes, write the transcript and count
the words, and play it to one person who wasn't there. Ask them to name the beginning, the middle
and the end. The count and their answer are the gate. Your feeling about the take is not.

## The story

You've told the story of the night your train was cancelled a dozen times at home. It always gets a
laugh at the part where the stationmaster gave you his own blanket.

Tonight you sit on the bed with your notes, press record, and tell it once, all the way through.
You stumble twice and don't stop. The blanket gets its laugh, from you, alone in the room.

You play it back and write it down, line by line. Two hundred and thirty-one words. Then you play it
to your neighbour's son, who has never heard it. He says:

> So you were stuck at the station, the train was cancelled, and the old man gave you his blanket
> and you slept on the bench. That's funny.

Setting, problem, ending, in his own words. Two hundred and thirty-one. That's the gate, counted.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **take**: one recording, start to finish, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/01-speaking/1.2-record-the-introduction.md).
- **transcript**: every word of a recording written down as it was said, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-the-gate-asks-for.md).
- **the three parts**: setting, problem, ending, from
  [day 43](../../../day-43-story-with-three-parts/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-three-parts-a-listener-waits-for.md).
- **the stop**: the long silence between the parts, with a higher start after it, from
  [day 57](../../../day-57-pausing-right-place/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-lengths-of-silence.md).

This part is the gate take itself. There are four steps and one rule: **don't stop the recording**.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Tell a story or personal anecdote](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/tell-story-or-personal-anecdote):
"Give your story an introduction. Say briefly what your story is about." and "Keep the action moving!"

**Step one: the setup.** Notes on the table, not in your hand. Phone face up, recording. One sip of
water. Say the first line of the setting once, quietly, before you press record.

**Step two: the take.** Tell the story straight through, from the setting to why it matters to you.
If a word goes wrong, say it again and carry on. Don't stop the recording for any reason except
somebody walking in.

**Step three: the transcript and the count.** Play it back and write down every word. Then fill the
table from the transcript alone.

**Step four: the stranger's ear.** Play the take to one person who wasn't there and doesn't know the
story. Ask:

> Could you tell me what happened, in three parts: how it started, what went wrong, and how it ended?

Write their answer down word for word.

**If the count is under two hundred, or the stranger can't give three parts,** don't cut or edit the
recording. Record a new take and keep both. The gate is one take that passes, not the best bits of
three.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **editing the take**: cutting the stumble out, or joining two takes.
The gate is a recorded story, told once. Stumbles stay.

The second is **the story read from a script**. You wrote it all out and read it. The words are
there, and the listener hears reading: flat, even, no stops. The notes are five lines for a reason.

The third is **playing it to somebody who knows the story**. They'll give you the three parts from
memory. It has to be a stranger to the story.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate recording. This day was written before your
own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Nobody in real life gets a second take. The gate asks for one take because that's the real
condition, and a take with two stumbles that reaches the end is much closer to real speech than a
clean one made of pieces.

Under pressure, stories speed up in the middle and die at the end. If yours did, the transcript will
show it: long sentences in the problem, and a three-word ending.

A stranger who can give back your three parts has proved the thing the gate exists for. Their words
are worth more than your count, and you need both.
