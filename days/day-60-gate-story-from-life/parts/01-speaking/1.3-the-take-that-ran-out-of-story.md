## One-line answer

Record a second take of your gate story with no notes and a listener asking *and then?* only once.
Find the exact line where it ran out, and ask a stranger what they'd want to know at that point. A
story that runs out doesn't end. It stops, and the listener is left waiting.

## The story

Your friend's little sister asks for a story at dinner. You start the train story without thinking:

> Once my train was cancelled, at night, and I was stuck at the station. And there was an old man,
> the stationmaster, and he gave me a blanket. So I slept there. Yeah.

Forty-one words. She waits. Then:

> And then? Why did he give you a blanket? Were you scared?

Everything she asks is in your two-hundred-word version: the cold, the empty platform, the man
saying *you'll freeze, take this*, and the tea he brought at five in the morning. Without the notes
and without a stop to breathe, you went straight from the problem to the end, and the middle fell
out.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce in a
first gate attempt. They are not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **transcript**: every word of a recording written down, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-the-gate-asks-for.md).
- **run out**: to stop because there's nothing more ready to say, not because the story is over.
- **the collapse line**: the exact line in the transcript where the story ran out.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A story usually runs out in one of two places:

| Where | What it sounds like | What's missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| after the problem | *… and he gave me a blanket. So yeah.* | what happened next, and how it felt |
| at the ending | *… and then I went home. That's it.* | why it matters to you |

The listener's first question tells you which one. *And then?* means the middle is missing. *So?*
means the ending is.

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate asks for two hundred words, and a take that runs out at eighty fails it. More importantly,
real listeners don't wait politely at eighty words. They ask *and then?*, or they start talking. This
part shows you, once, where your story runs out when there's no support.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Tell a story or personal anecdote](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/tell-story-or-personal-anecdote).
Its *don'ts* include "Take too long telling the story or your listeners will get bored". Its *do's*
include "Say what happened step by step" and "Keep the action moving!" A story that runs out breaks
the second, not the first.

**Step one: record take B**, deliberately without notes and without the long stops. Tell the same
story as fast as it comes out, straight through, and stop when you feel finished.

**Step two: transcript and count.** Write it down, count the words, and mark **the collapse line**:
the last line before the story jumped or stopped.

**Step three: the stranger's question.** Play take B to a person who hasn't heard either take. Stop
it at the end and ask:

> What would you want to ask me now?

Write their question word for word.

so you know where your story breaks without support.

**Step five: the repair drill.** At your collapse line, record just the missing part, three times:
the next thing that happened, one detail you could see or hear, and one line somebody said. Then,
if the gate was red, record a new full take with the notes.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take B that's secretly take A**. You know the
story too well now and tell the long version anyway. Tell it fast, with no notes and no stops, the
way you would at a noisy table.

The second is **a stranger who's too polite** and says *no, that was great*. Ask the question
exactly: *what would you want to ask me now?* Everyone has a question.

The third, in a real gate attempt, is **padding to reach two hundred**: *so yeah, anyway, like I
said, so basically*. That's words, not story. Padding counts in the transcript and fails the stranger.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate attempt. This day was written before your
own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Everybody's stories run out sometimes. Fluent speakers recover with a question to themselves: *and
you know what he said?* or *so what did I do?*, and the middle comes back. That's a habit you can
build from your repair drill.

Under pressure, the middle is what disappears, because it's the part you have to build while you
talk. The setting and the ending you remember. The notes exist to hold the middle.

A listener who asks *and then?* isn't criticising you. It's the best sign there is that the story
was worth hearing. Answer it.
