## One-line answer

The phase 3 writing gate is a one-page story in the past, in paragraphs, with speech marks used
once. This part teaches nothing new, because a gate that teaches isn't a gate.

## The story

Your niece's school is making a small book called *Stories from Our Families*. Each child brings
one page from somebody at home: a real story, written by the person it happened to. Your niece
asks you.

The page will be printed and read by people you'll never meet: teachers, other parents, children.
None of them can ask you what you meant. They'll only have the page.

That page is the gate. It isn't a test of new English. It's a test of whether the English of the
last nineteen days can carry a real story from its first line to its last for a reader who has only
the page.

**This is a rehearsal, and it says so.** This day was written before your error ledger had rows in
it. Until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked, the checks below are the general
ones rather than your own recurring mistakes, and the gate is not yet a gate.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate**: the check at the end of a phase, which the whole phase was for, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-the-page-must-carry.md).
- **paragraph** and **break**: a block of one idea, and the place where it ends, from
  [day 57](../../../day-57-pausing-right-place/parts/02-writing/2.1-a-new-idea-a-new-paragraph.md).
- **speech marks**: the marks around somebody's exact words, from
  [day 49](../../../day-49-what-someone-said/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-marks-around-the-words.md).
- **rehearsal**: a practice of the real thing, done as if it were the real thing.

The gate wording is in the plan's §7, and it's copied here without a word changed:

> Writing: a one-page story in the past, in paragraphs, with speech marks used once.

Four conditions, and each can be checked by looking.

| Condition | What it means | How it's checked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a one-page story | one side of a notebook page; typed, this course counts it as no more than about three hundred and fifty words | the page, or the word count |
| in the past | every event told in the past simple or past continuous | underline every verb outside the speech marks |
| in paragraphs | at least three, with a visible break where the idea changes | a reader can name each paragraph |
| speech marks used once | at least one line of somebody's exact words, punctuated correctly | the marks, the comma and the capital, checked |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the point of the phase. Every writing part since day 41 exists so that one real story can be
written for a stranger: the past forms, the questions and negatives, the memory, the topic sentence,
the past continuous, the speech marks, the time words, the paragraph break, the retelling.

And phase 4 builds on it. An argument is longer than a story, and it assumes you can already hold a
reader for a page.

## The rule

There's no new rule today. What follows is the setup.

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[A short story](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/short-story).
Its tips for a story include "the past simple, past continuous and the past perfect" and "direct
speech with reporting verbs and punctuation". Its example story has three paragraphs and uses a line
of direct speech:

> 'Where are they?' she thought.

That page uses single marks, and so does this course, as on day 49. Double marks aren't wrong.
Switching between them halfway down the page is.

**Choose the story first.** It must be yours, and it must have a problem in it. Any real story
will do. Choose a real reader too: someone who will actually read
the page.

**Plan three paragraphs, minimum**, one idea each:

| Paragraph | Its idea | Its first line names it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | the setting: when, where, who | `TODO(me)` |
| 2 | the problem, and what happened | `TODO(me)` |
| 3 | the ending, and why it matters | `TODO(me)` |

A fourth paragraph is fine if the middle has two parts.

**Choose the one line of speech now.** The line somebody said at the most important moment. It will
sit in paragraph 2, usually.

**Remember the verb rule for speech.** Inside the speech marks, the words are what the person said,
so they can be in the present: *'Take this blanket,' he said.* Outside them, everything is past.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the story that slips into the present** halfway: *Then the man
comes and he says …*. It happens because stories are told that way out loud. On the page, outside the
speech marks, it breaks the gate's second condition.

The second is **the page in one block**: a good story, no breaks. It fails *in paragraphs*, however
good the sentences are.

The third is **the speech with no marks**: *He said take this blanket you'll freeze.* The words are
there, and the reader can't see where his words start.

The fourth is **the page that is two pages**: five hundred words because the story was good. The gate
says one page, and cutting is part of the skill.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate piece. This day was written before your own
error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A one-page story is what real people write for school books, family albums, community newsletters,
and letters to grandchildren. It is short enough to be read at once and long enough to have a middle.

Under pressure, a deadline for the school book, people write the setting at length and rush the
end. Plan the three paragraphs first, and give the middle the most lines.

A stranger reading your page notices three things before any grammar: whether they know where they
are, whether something happened, and whether they heard a voice. The line of speech is often where
they hear it.
