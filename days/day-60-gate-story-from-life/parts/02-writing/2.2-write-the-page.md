## One-line answer

Write the page from your paragraph plan in one sitting, then check it in four passes: the page, the
verbs, the breaks, the speech marks. Give it to the real reader it's for. The four passes and the
reader's answer are the gate. Your feeling about the page is not.

## The story

You write the page for your niece's school book at the kitchen table: the night the power went out
during your sister's wedding, and the band played on without microphones.

The first version is a page and a quarter, and the whole middle is one block. You check it in four
passes. The first pass cuts the long description of the hall. The second finds *the drummer starts
shouting* halfway down, present tense, and fixes it to *started*. The third splits the middle where
the lights come back. The fourth finds that your one line of speech has no comma before the closing
mark.

The printed page reaches your niece's teacher. She writes one line at the bottom: *I could hear the
drums.*

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the four conditions**: one page, in the past, in paragraphs, speech marks once, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-what-the-page-must-do.md).
- **pass**: one read-through of your page looking for one thing only.
- **past simple** and **past continuous**: *walked*, *went*; *was walking*, *were playing*, from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/02-writing/2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **fair copy**: the final clean version, written out neatly, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-the-page.md).

The page is written once and checked four times. Each pass looks for **one thing only**, because an
eye looking for four things at once finds none of them.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[A short story](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/short-story):
a story uses "the past simple, past continuous and the past perfect" and "direct speech with reporting
verbs and punctuation", and its example is in three paragraphs.

**Step one: write the draft** from your plan in one sitting. Don't check anything while you write.

**Step two: pass one, the page.** Is it one page? If not, cut from the setting first, then from the
description. Never cut the ending, and never cut the line of speech. Write the word count at the
bottom.

**Step three: pass two, the verbs.** Underline every verb outside the speech marks. Each must be past
simple or past continuous. Circle any that aren't and fix them.

**Step four: pass three, the breaks.** Is there a visible break where the idea changes? Does each
paragraph's first line name its idea? At least three paragraphs?

**Step five: pass four, the speech.** Look at your line of speech:

- a capital letter on the first spoken word;
- the comma, question mark or exclamation mark inside the closing mark;
- a small letter on the reporting verb after it: *he said*;
- the same kind of marks you used anywhere else on the page.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **checking while writing**. You stop after every sentence to fix it,
and the story comes out stiff and the ending is rushed. Draft first, check after.

The second is **one pass for everything**. You read it once, *looking for mistakes*, and find two.
Four passes, each looking for one thing, find far more.

The third is **the page never given to anybody**. It's checked, counted and copied out, and it sits in
the notebook. The gate is a page a stranger reads.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate piece. This day was written before your own
error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Everybody who writes for other people works this way: a fast draft, then separate checks. Newspapers
have different people do different passes for the same reason.

Under pressure, people skip pass two, the verbs, because the story *reads* fine to its writer. It
reads fine because you know when it happened. The stranger only has the verb.

The reader's answer to *which line did you remember?* tells you where your voice came through. It's
often the line of speech.
