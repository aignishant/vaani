## One-line answer

Make a copy of your gate page where the middle paragraph slips into the present tense, the way a
story does when you tell it aloud. Give it to a reader and ask *when did all this happen?* A story
that slips into the present doesn't only look wrong. The reader can't tell if it's over.

## The story

A neighbour writes a page for the building's newsletter about the night the water tank overflowed.
It starts well:

> It was two in the morning. I was asleep when I heard water on the stairs.

Then, as it gets exciting, the page slips:

> I open the door and the whole corridor is wet. Mr Rao comes out in his towel and he shouts,
> 'Who left the motor on?'

The newsletter goes out. Three people knock on her door the next day to ask *is the tank still
leaking?* They read *the corridor is wet* as *now*. Her story had ended a month ago. Her page never
said so.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce in a
first gate piece. They are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **past simple**, **past continuous**: from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/02-writing/2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **present simple**: the verb form for what is true or usual now, from
  [day 15](../../../day-15-daily-routine/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-s-on-he-she-it.md).
- **slip**: a change of tense in the middle of a story that the writer didn't choose.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Out loud, people often tell the exciting part of a story in the present: *so I open the door, and he
comes out …*. Listeners know the story is over because you're sitting there telling it. A page has no
face. The only thing that tells a reader *this is over* is the past tense. Take it away, and they
can't be sure.

| Where the present is | What the reader thinks |
| --- | --- |
| inside the speech marks: *'Who left the motor on?'* | it's what somebody said then: correct |
| outside the speech marks: *the corridor is wet* | it might be true now |

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate's second condition is *in the past*. A single slipped paragraph fails it, and the writer
rarely sees it, because they know when it happened. This part shows you, once, what a reader does
with a slip.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[A short story](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/short-story):
a written story uses "the past simple, past continuous and the past perfect". The present belongs
only inside the speech marks.

**Step one: take your fair copy** from [part 2.2](2.2-write-the-page.md). Keep it safe.

**Step two: make copy S**, deliberately slipped: rewrite only the middle paragraph with every verb
outside the speech marks in the present simple. Label it *deliberate*.

**Step three: the when test.** Give copy S to a reader who hasn't seen your fair copy. Ask two
questions:

> When did this happen?
> Is any of it still going on?

Then ask them to underline any verb that looks wrong.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the fair copy's reader says any part is

and the gate is red until it's fixed. It is **GREEN** when the fair copy's reader says it's all over,
and copy S's reader hesitates, asks, or underlines the slipped verbs.

**Step six: the hunt.** Whatever the result, read your fair copy once more, only for verbs outside
the speech marks, starting from the last line and going up. Reading backwards stops the story
carrying you past a slip.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **slipping the speech too**. Changing *'Who left the
motor on?'* to the past makes it wrong in the other direction. Speech stays as it was said.

The second is **a reader who heard the story** already. They know it's over. Use a stranger to it.

The third, in real writing, is **the slip in one verb only**: one *says* in a paragraph of *said*.
That's the one writers miss, and the backwards hunt in step six is how you catch it.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate piece. This day was written before your own
error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Some published stories are written in the present on purpose, all the way through. That's a choice,
kept for the whole page. A slip is a change in the middle, and readers feel it as a jolt even when
they can't say why.

Under pressure, the slip comes at the exciting part, because that's where you're telling it to
yourself in your head. Check the middle paragraph first.

A formal reader, a teacher or an editor, marks a tense slip every time. A friend reading a message
just gets confused, and may ask *is it still leaking?*
