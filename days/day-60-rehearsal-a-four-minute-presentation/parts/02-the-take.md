# The take, and counting the seams

`CF-13` · Day 60

## The one-line answer

This is the eleventh piece in your portfolio and the first one where you are the only voice on it
and somebody is scoring you, and the only thing you judge tonight is the seams — the places the
talk moved from one part to the next.

## The model

Portfolio recording 11. A four-minute presentation, standing, on one thing you know, in three
parts.

You are not being judged on whether it was interesting. Some of the most useful presentations
anybody has ever sat through were about very dull things and you could follow every step. Those
two facts are unrelated, and tonight only the second one is on trial.

Set it up like this.

**Three parts, decided before the recorder goes on.** Say the map out loud once to the empty chairs
— *here's where we're going* — and then start.

**The map only, on the card.** Three part names, nothing else. No sentences anywhere on it. A card
with sentences on it is a card you will read, and Day 58 told you exactly what reading sounds like
on tape: chin down, ends dropped, no gaps anywhere. If your card can be read aloud, rewrite it.

**Standing, to the chairs, with the recorder across the room.** Same rig as Day 58. You want the
file to hold what the room heard, not what your chin heard.

**Keep the first take.** Same rule as every rehearsal since Day 6, and the same reason. The first
one is the honest one. You may record a second afterwards and it may well be better; the first one
is the one that goes in the row and it is not deleted.

**Stalls stay in.** You will lose the thread somewhere. You rehearsed both recoveries in part 01.
Use them out loud and do not stop the recorder.

**If you have a partner, two questions at the end.** They ask two things you did not prepare for,
and at least one of them should be something you cannot answer. That is Day 59 arriving on tape,
and it belongs on this recording — a presentation that stops the instant the speaker stops talking
is not the version you will ever actually give.

**Two lines you say during, and one you say to close.**

> The point of that is {{…}}

That is the recovery for a section that wandered. You get to the end of a part and can hear that it
went somewhere loose. Rather than doubling back, land it: state what it was for, in one sentence,
and move on. It sounds like control and it costs one sentence.

> So, to pull that together —

The close. Not a fourth part, not new material — the three names again, in order, and then you
stop. This sentence is what stands between you and the thing part 01 warned about: finishing,
finding the silence unbearable, and adding something.

**Then the playback, once, all the way, without stopping.** You know the job from Day 6: listen
with a task, never for a verdict. Tonight's task is one thing, counted.

**Count the seams.** A talk in three parts has two seams — the place part one becomes part two, and
the place part two becomes part three. If your partner asked questions, the move into the questions
is a third. For each seam, one of three words:

- **Signposted** — there is a signal, and there is audible silence on both sides of it. You can
  find it on one listen without knowing in advance where to look.
- **Blurred** — the signal is in there, you said the words, but it is buried inside a run of speech
  with no gaps around it. You know it is there because you wrote it. A stranger would not find it.
- **Missed** — the part changed and nothing marked it at all.

Two or three numbers. They go into row 11 of `docs/RECORDINGS.md` tonight, beside the date.

Count blurred honestly. Blurred is the interesting one and it is the one you will want to score as
signposted, because you can hear the words perfectly well — you have the map. That is exactly the
mistake this count exists to catch.

**Today's phrases** — appended tonight with the ones from part 01.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Here's where we're going. | The map sentence, said once, before any content at all |
| The point of that is {{…}} | Landing a section that wandered, instead of doubling back |
| So, to pull that together — | Closing on the three names, so nothing gets added after |

## Say it now

Before the recorder goes on, standing, say each of these three times — flat, unhurried, aimed at
the far wall — so they arrive without a run-up when you need them.

1. *Here's where we're going: {{one}}, {{two}}, {{three}}.*
2. *The point of that is {{…}}.*
3. *So, to pull that together — {{one}}, {{two}}, {{three}}. That's me.*

Then say all three as one run, twice. These are the only lines you rehearse. Everything between
them in the take is live, and it is meant to be.

## The deliberate failure

After the take is finished, record one extra stretch on the end. Deliver a seam twice.

First: say the signpost at full flow, mid-sentence, no gaps around it — *and that's the first one
so moving on to the second thing which is* — all in one breath, the way it comes out when you are
going well and don't want to break the momentum.

Second: the same seam, with a real silence before the signal and a real silence after it.

**What breaking sounds like:** play the first one back and try to find the signpost. You said it.
Every word is on the tape. And on one listen you cannot locate it, because a signal with no gaps
around it is not a signal — it is texture, and the ear files it with everything else.

That is the uncomfortable finding, and it is specific to this day: **you will score that seam as
signposted while you are speaking, and it will not be signposted on the tape.** Which means the
count you are about to do is not optional bookkeeping. It is the only way you find out that the
version of your talk in your head has structure the recorded one does not.

Then listen to the second version, and notice how little you had to do. Two silences. No new words.

## When it breaks in the wild

You reach the end of your third part and stop, and the room says nothing, because rooms say
nothing. The silence sits there, and it is unbearable, so you fill it — with a
fourth thing you had not planned, or with a summary of the summary, or with *so, yeah, that's
kind of it, really*. And that is the last thing anybody hears from you, and it is easily the worst
sentence in the whole talk.

**The repair:**

> So, to pull that together — {{one}}, {{two}}, {{three}}. That's me.

Then close your mouth and let the room have its silence. Whoever is running the room will end it,
and if nobody does, it ends anyway. The one thing that never helps is you ending it with more
talking.
