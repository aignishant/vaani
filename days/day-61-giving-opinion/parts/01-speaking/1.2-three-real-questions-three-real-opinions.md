## One-line answer

Choose three real questions that people around you are really asking, give your opinion on each with
an opener, a reason and an example, and then ask *what do you think?* If the other person answers
your reason instead of asking *why?*, the opinion worked.

## The story

Your building's residents have a question going round: should the building pay for a night guard?
Everybody has an opinion, and nobody has said it properly.

At the gate one evening, a neighbour asks you directly. You've thought about it:

> Personally, I think we need one, because two scooters were stolen last month. And the gate is open
> all night, so anybody can walk in.

He doesn't ask *why?* He answers your reason:

> The scooters were from the back lane, though, not the gate.

Now you're really talking. Your reason gave him something to answer.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **opener**, **reason**, **example**: the three pieces of a spoken opinion, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-opener-reason-example.md).
- **real question**: a question people around you are actually asking, not an exercise.
- **hand back**: to give the other person the next turn, from
  [day 38](../../../day-38-hobbies-and-free-time/parts/01-speaking/1.2-say-how-often-and-ask-back.md).

An opinion is only finished when the other person has answered it. So every opinion in this part
ends with a question that hands the turn back:

> What do you think?
> What's your view?

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 4 gate is a recorded opinion with reasons, and later parts of this phase add agreeing,
disagreeing and answering the other side. All of that starts with an opinion that a real person
answers.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the practice question**: *Is tea better than coffee?* Nobody around
you is asking it, so nobody answers. Choose questions people care about.

The second is **no hand back**. You give the opinion and stop. The other person nods. Always finish
with *what do you think?*

The third is **three reasons in a pile**: *because it's cheaper, and safer, and my cousin says, and
also the gate …*. The listener can't choose one to answer. One reason, one example.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Most real opinions are given in passing, at a gate, over tea, in a message, and they're short. The
reason and the example are what make them stick.

Under pressure, when you know the other person disagrees, people often hide the opinion or give it
with no reason. Give the reason anyway, calmly. It turns a clash into a conversation.

A native listener who answers your reason is treating you as an equal. That's the goal of every
opinion in this phase.
