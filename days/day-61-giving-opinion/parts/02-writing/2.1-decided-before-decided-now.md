## One-line answer

Write *am/is/are going to* + base form for a plan you had already decided (*I'm going to visit you in
May*). Write *will* + base form for something you decide as you write, a promise, an offer or a
prediction (*I'll call you on Sunday*). The reader sees which one was already settled.

## The story

Your mother writes on the family group: *When are you coming home? And did you remember your
father's medicine?*

You reply:

> I'm going to come home on the 14th. I booked the ticket last week. Oh no, the medicine! I'll buy it
> tomorrow morning and I'll send it with Ravi.

Your mother reads two different kinds of future. *I'm going to come on the 14th* was decided before,
and there's a ticket. *I'll buy it tomorrow* was decided just now, as you remembered, and it's a
promise. She replies: *Good. Don't forget again.*

The two forms told her which part was settled and which part was a promise she might need to check.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **future**: the time after now.
- **base form**: the plain form of a verb: *go*, *buy*, *call*, from
  [day 42](../../../day-42-three-sounds-of-ed/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-verbs-that-refuse-ed.md).
- **plan**: something you have already decided to do.
- **promise**: telling somebody you will certainly do something.
- **offer**: saying you're ready to do something for somebody.
- **prediction**: saying what you think will happen.
- **contraction**: a short form with an apostrophe, like *I'll* for *I will*, from
  [day 35](../../../day-35-contractions-aloud/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-mark-that-holds-the-missing-letters.md).

English has more than one way to write the future. Two of the most common are *going to* and *will*,
and they tell the reader different things:

| Form | Use it for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *am/is/are going to* + base form | a plan decided **before** you write | *I'm going to come home on the 14th.* |
| *will* + base form | a decision made **now**, as you write | *Oh no! I'll buy it tomorrow.* |
| *will* + base form | a promise or an offer | *I'll send it with Ravi.* |
| *will* + base form | a prediction | *It will be hot in May.* |
| *going to* + base form | a prediction you can see coming | *Look at those clouds. It's going to rain.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Phase 4 is about opinions, plans and explaining. Plans need a future form, and messages, notes and
emails full of plans and promises are what you'll write most often in real life. A reader who
can't tell a firm plan from a quick promise may plan around the wrong one.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Future forms: 'will', 'be going to' and present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/future-forms-will-be-going-present-continuous)
says *will* is for "spontaneous plans decided at the moment of speaking" and *be going to* for "plans
decided before the moment of speaking", with:

> Oops, I forgot to phone Mum! I'll do it after dinner.
> I'm going to phone Mum after dinner. I told her I'd call at 8 o'clock.

[Talking about the future](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/talking-about-future)
gives *will* for predictions, "It will be a nice day tomorrow.", and for "Offers and promises: I'll see
you tomorrow.". It gives *be going to* for "Plans or intentions" and "Predictions based on evidence:
Look at those black clouds. I think it's going to rain."

**The shapes on paper.**

| | *going to* | *will* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yes | *I'm going to call.* · *She's going to call.* · *They're going to call.* | *I'll call.* · *She'll call.* · *They'll call.* |
| no | *I'm not going to call.* · *He isn't going to call.* | *I won't call.* (*won't* = *will not*) |
| question | *Are you going to call?* | *Will you call?* |

Three things to see:

1. ***Going to* needs *be* in front**: *am*, *is* or *are*. *I going to* is missing a word.
2. ***Will* is the same for everyone.** *She will*, never *she wills*. And *will* never has *to* after
   it: *I will call*, never *I will to call*.
3. **The verb after both is always the base form.** *Going to buy*, *will buy*. Never *going to
   bought* or *will buying*.

**In messages, use the contraction.** *I'll*, *she'll*, *won't*, *I'm going to*. In formal letters,
write it out: *I will*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **both at once**:

> I will going to call you tomorrow.

The reader sees two futures stuck together. Choose one: *I'm going to call* if it was already your
plan, *I'll call* if you're promising it now.

The second is **the missing *be***:

> I going to visit my uncle.

It reads as a message typed too fast, with a word missing.

The third is ***to* after *will***:

> I will to send the money.

*Will* takes the base form straight after it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

In everyday messages, people mix both forms constantly: *I'm going to be late. I'll call you from the
bus.* The first is the situation, the second is the promise.

Under pressure, typing fast, people write *will* for everything. Readers mostly understand, but a firm
plan written as *I will come on the 14th* can read like a decision made just now, and the reader may
ask *are you sure?*

A formal reader, a manager or an office, reads *I will* as a promise, and remembers it. Only write
*will* for what you'll really do.
