## One-line answer

Write a real message about the next few weeks with at least three plans in *going to* and three
promises or offers in *will*. Then ask the reader which things are already fixed and which are
promises. If they sort them the way you meant, your future forms did their job.

## The story

Your friend is getting married next month, and you're one of the people helping. She sends a long
voice message full of worry: the hall, the sweets, her aunt's train, the photographer.

You write back:

> Don't worry! I'm going to collect your aunt from the station on the 3rd. I've told my boss already.
> Raju is going to book the photographer this week. I'll call the sweet shop tomorrow and ask about the
> order, and I'll send you their answer. If you want, I'll come with you to see the hall on Sunday.

She replies with a list:

> Fixed: aunt, photographer. Tomorrow: you call the sweet shop. Sunday: hall, if I want. THANK YOU.

She sorted your message exactly as you wrote it. The *going to* sentences went into *fixed*, and the
*will* sentences went into *promised*.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- ***going to*** and ***will***: the two future forms, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-decided-before-decided-now.md).
- **plan**, **promise**, **offer**: something already decided; something you say you'll certainly do;
  something you're ready to do for somebody, from [part 2.1](2.1-decided-before-decided-now.md).
- **sort**: to put things into groups.

A good message about the future does two jobs. It tells the reader what's already fixed, so they can
stop worrying about it. And it tells them what you're promising, so they know what to expect from
you and when.

## Why Vaani needs it

Messages and emails about plans are some of the most common things you'll write in English. Later in
this phase you'll write a plan step by step, and in phase 5 a status update at work. Both depend on
the reader telling *fixed* from *promised*.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Future forms: 'will', 'be going to' and present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/future-forms-will-be-going-present-continuous):
*will* for "spontaneous plans decided at the moment of speaking", *be going to* for "plans decided
before the moment of speaking".

**Step one: choose a real reader and a real situation** with things coming up in the next few weeks: an
event, a visit, a move, a family matter, something at work.

**Step three: write the message.** At least three *going to* plans and three *will* promises or offers,
each with a day or a time. Start with a line to your reader, and end with one kind line.

**Step four: the form check.** Underline every future form. For each one:

- *going to*: is there *am*, *is* or *are* in front?
- *will* or *'ll*: is there no *to* after it?
- after both: is the next verb in the base form?

**Step five: the promise check.** Read every *will* again. Is it something you'll really do, by the day
you wrote? If not, change it, or take it out.

**Step six: the sorting test.** Give it to your reader, or somebody like them, and ask:

> Which things in this are already fixed, and which am I promising?

Write their two lists down and compare them with your table.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **all *will***: *I will collect your aunt, Raju will book the
photographer, I will call the shop*. Every line reads as a promise made just now, and the reader can't
tell what's already settled.

The second is **the promise with no day**: *I'll call the sweet shop.* When? The reader can't plan
around it, and it's easy for you to forget.

The third is **the promise you can't keep**. It's easy to type *I'll* for everything someone's worried
about. Each one is a promise the reader will remember.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

People sort messages this way all the time, often without noticing: *OK, that's done, that's
tomorrow*. A message that does the sorting for them gets a quick, happy reply.

Under pressure, a worried friend or a boss asking for an update, people write a wall of *I'll*s to
calm them down. One *going to* line for what's already done calms them more.

A reader who later finds that *I'll call tomorrow* didn't happen remembers that. Fewer promises, all
kept, is worth more than many.
