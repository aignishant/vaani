## One-line answer

Plant four future-form errors in a copy of your real message, including one that swaps a firm plan
into *will*. Give it to a reader and ask what's fixed and what's promised, and to circle anything that
looks wrong. Some errors only look wrong. The swap changes what the reader thinks is settled.

## The story

You write to your landlord about the broken water heater:

> I will going to be at home on Saturday. The plumber will come at ten, I think. I going to pay him and
> send you the bill.

Your landlord reads it twice. He replies:

> Has the plumber confirmed ten or not? And do you want me to pay or will you?

Three future forms, three problems. *Will going to* is two forms stuck together. *The plumber will
come, I think* reads like a guess, although you had booked him for ten. *I going to pay* has a word
missing. Your landlord couldn't tell what was fixed, so he asked about everything.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **plant**: to put an error into a text on purpose, from
  [day 46](../../../day-46-describing-a-trip/parts/02-writing/2.3-there-was-two-beds.md).
- ***going to***, ***will***: the two future forms, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-decided-before-decided-now.md).
- **fixed**: already decided and arranged.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The four errors aren't equally dangerous:

| Error | Example | What the reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| both forms at once | *I will going to call you.* | an error, meaning mostly clear |
| missing *be* | *I going to pay him.* | an error, meaning clear |
| *to* after *will* | *I will to send the bill.* | an error, meaning clear |
| a fixed plan written as a *will* guess | *The plumber will come at ten, I think.* | the wrong fact: that it isn't settled |

The last one has no spelling mistake at all, and it's the one that makes the reader act differently.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every message about plans in this phase, and every status update and request in phase 5, is read by
someone deciding what to do next. A reader who thinks a fixed plan is a guess will check, chase or
re-arrange. This part shows you once what they do.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **planting the *will* guess on something really
unsure**. Then the reader is right to call it unsure, and nothing is learned. It must be something
you've actually fixed.

The second is **a reader who knows the situation**. They sort it from what they know. Use somebody who
doesn't.

The third, in real writing, is ***I think* on everything** to sound polite: *I will come, I think.* To a
reader it sounds unsure. If it's fixed, say so plainly.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Landlords, managers and offices read messages to decide what they have to do. Every line that sounds
unsure is a line they'll chase you about. Clear future forms mean fewer questions back.

Under pressure, a message about a problem, people pile *will*, *going to* and *I think* together.
Write the fixed things first in *going to*, then the promises in *will*.

A formal reader marks *will going to* and *I going to* every time. They're small errors, and they're
the ones people notice first.
