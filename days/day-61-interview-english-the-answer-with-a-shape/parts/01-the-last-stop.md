# Knowing your last stop before you set off

`FN-18` · Day 61

## The one-line answer

An answer about something you did is a short ride with a named last stop, and if you don't know
where you're getting off before you set off, you'll either never leave the first stop or you'll
ride past your own ending and keep going.

## The model

Interviews are made almost entirely of one question, asked in different clothes. *Tell me about a
time you —.* *Give me an example of —.* *What's something you've worked on that —.* Same question.
It is asking you to take the listener on a short ride and then get off somewhere useful.

There are exactly two ways this goes wrong, and you have almost certainly done both.

**It dies at the first stop.** They ask, and you give one sentence. *Yeah, we had a problem with
the reports, so I fixed it.* True, complete, and over. What happens next is the giveaway: a
silence, then they ask a follow-up which is really the same question again, and now you're
telling the story backwards under a bit more pressure than you had a moment ago.

**It never gets off.** They ask, and you set off, and you keep going — another detail, another
person, another thing that happened after that — because there's no stop coming up and no obvious
place to stand up. It ends when you run out of energy, usually on something like *…yeah, so,
that's, yeah.* The uncomfortable part is that this one felt fine while you were doing it. You were
talking, and talking felt like the opposite of failing.

Both have the same cause. You got on without knowing where you were getting off.

**The four stops.** Every good answer to this question passes through the same four, in order, and
only the third one gets to be long.

> **One — where you were.** One sentence. Not two. Where, roughly when, what your job was in it.
> **Two — what was hard.** The thing that made it not automatic. Without this stop, whatever you
> did next has nothing to push against and sounds like admin.
> **Three — what you did.** The long one. *You*, not the team. This is the whole reason they asked.
> **Four — how it came out, and what stayed true afterwards.** This is your last stop.

Say a full run of it out loud now, on something small and real from your own week:

> This was back when I was covering the second site on my own. The tricky part was that nobody had
> written any of it down — it was all in one person's head, and he'd left. So what I did was sit
> with the two people who used it most and get them to talk me through a normal week, and I wrote
> it as I went. In the end we had six pages that anyone could pick up. And that's still how we do
> it now.

Four stops. Nothing clever. Notice how short stop one is, and notice that stop four is not *and
then it was finished* — it's something that outlived the story.

**Stop four is the one people skip**, and it's the one that does the work. A result is what
happened; a last stop is what was still true afterwards. *We hit the date* is a result. *And
that's still how we do it* is a last stop, and the difference is audible — the second one closes,
the first one leaves the listener waiting for more.

**The bell.** You also have to tell them you're getting off, and you do that with your voice, not
with words. The last sentence goes short, and it goes down at the end, and then you stop and let
the silence sit. Most people ruin a good four-stop answer in the last breath by adding *…so, yeah,
that was that one, I don't know if that's what you wanted*. You've already got off the bus. Don't
climb back on to apologise for the journey.

You've had the raw material for this since `FN-15` on Day 50 — setup, turn, point. This is that
shape, put under a question, with the ending made explicit because in an interview nobody rescues
you.

Stop one you already own. **Day 35** gave you *This was back when I was …* for placing a story with
no dates, no names and no run-up, and it does exactly that job here. Use it — it isn't relearned
today, it's spent.

**Today's phrases** — the sentences that get you on and off a long answer cleanly. All of these go
into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| How much detail do you want? | Checking the size of the ride before you set off |
| The tricky part was … | Stop two — giving what you did something to push against |
| So what I did was … | The handover from the situation to you |
| In the end, … | Turning towards the last stop |
| And that's still how we do it. | A last stop that outlived the story |
| The bit I'd do differently is … | Volunteering the honest edge before they dig for it |
| That's probably the clearest example I've got. | Getting off, and handing the turn back |

## Say it now

Say each line twice, filling the slots from something you actually did. Nothing written down.

1. *How much detail do you want — {{the headline}}, or the whole thing?*
2. *This was back when I was {{where you were and what your job was}}.*
3. *The tricky part was {{what made it not automatic}}.*
4. *So what I did was {{the first thing you actually did}}.*
5. *In the end, {{how it came out}}.*
6. *And that's still how we do it — {{the thing that stuck}}.*
7. *The bit I'd do differently is {{the honest edge}}.*
8. *That's probably the clearest example I've got.*

Then run stops one to four as one unbroken stretch, three times, about three different things you
did. No pause between the stops — the pauses are where you start adding a fifth stop.

Now the awkward version, and do not skip it: pick a fourth thing, and say your **last stop first**.
Out loud, one sentence, before anything else. Then go back to stop one and ride to it. That's the
whole skill, stripped of everything else. If you can't say the last stop before you start, you
don't have an answer yet — you have a topic.

> Three questions, answered back to back into one file: **a time you fixed something that wasn't
> your job**, **a time you got something wrong**, and **the piece of work you'd point to first**.
> Four stops each. Get off at stop four and stop talking.

- **Solo form:** before each answer, say the last stop out loud on its own — one sentence — then
  set off from stop one and try to arrive at it. Leave that spoken last stop on the tape; you want
  to hear later whether you actually got there.
- **With a partner:** they say nothing at all when you finish. No *mm*, no nod, no next question.
  Just silence. If you start talking again into it, you hadn't got off.

## The deliberate failure

Take one question — *a time you got something wrong* — and record it twice, deliberately broken.

First, kill it at the first stop. One sentence, then stop dead and let the recorder run on the
silence. Don't rescue it.

Then run the same question with no stop four at all. Ride past the ending on purpose. Every time
you feel the answer wanting to close, add another thing that happened afterwards. Keep going until
you genuinely don't know how to stop, then trail out however you trail out.

**What breaking sounds like:** the one-sentence version leaves a hole, and on the tape you can
hear how fast you wanted to fill it — most people can't hold that silence and start again
unprompted, which is exactly what happens in the room. The runaway version is the more useful
finding, though. Play it back and try to say, out loud, what the answer was about. You'll struggle,
and you were the one talking. Listen to your own voice through the last stretch: it flattens out,
every sentence ends at the same pitch, and none of them go down, because a voice that goes down is
a voice that's finished and yours wasn't allowed to be. That flat run is what a listener hears as
*this person can't answer a question*, and it's not a language problem — you had all the words.

## When it breaks in the wild

You're across a table from two people. One of them asks for an example of something you handled
badly. You give a sentence and a half, because being brief felt safer than rambling. There's a
pause. The second one says, *and how did you approach that?* — which is the same question, asked
again, more slowly, and now you're rebuilding the story out of order while they watch you do it.
By the time you reach the end you've said *sorry, I'm going back a bit* three times.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — let me take that from the start. This was back when I was {{…}}.

Go back to stop one on purpose, out loud. Announcing the restart costs you nothing; you built that
move on Day 17 with `CF-05`. Half-rebuilding an answer from the middle costs you the answer.

If an answer failed on the second condition, don't re-record the whole thing — record just that
answer again with one instruction: stop your mouth after the last stop. That's a mouth job, not a
thinking job, and it takes a handful of goes rather than a rethink. Re-recording is normal. It is
not a failed day.
