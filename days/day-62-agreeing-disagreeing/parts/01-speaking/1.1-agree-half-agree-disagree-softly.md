## One-line answer

To disagree without offending, first show you heard the other person (*I see what you mean*), then
say *but* gently and give your own reason. Keep your voice at the same level. Never louder. The soft
start tells them you're still on their side, even though you think something different.

## The story

Lunch break at work. Your colleague says:

> This new timetable is terrible. We should all complain to the manager.

You don't agree. You think the new timetable is better for most people. Your first thought comes out
flat and fast:

> No, that's wrong. It's better.

He goes red, picks up his plate, and moves to another table. For the rest of the day he doesn't speak
to you.

The next week, a different colleague says the same thing. This time you say:

> I see what you mean, the mornings are harder. But I think it's better overall, because we finish
> earlier.

She thinks for a second and says, *Hm. That's true, actually.* You disagreed completely, and the
conversation carried on.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **agree**: to think the same as somebody.
- **disagree**: to think differently from somebody.
- **half agree**: to agree with part of what somebody said and not the rest.
- **soften**: here, to make a spoken disagreement sound gentler, with a few extra words and a calm
  voice.
- **opener** and **reason**: the start that shows an opinion is coming, and the *because*, from
  [day 61](../../../day-61-giving-opinion/parts/01-speaking/1.1-opener-reason-example.md).

When you disagree, you're saying two things at once: *I think something different*, and *I still
respect you*. The first comes from your words. The second comes mostly from how you start and how your
voice sounds.

A scale from full agreement to full disagreement:

| How much you agree | What you can say |
| --- | --- |
| fully | *I agree.* · *That's true.* · *Exactly.* |
| partly | *That's true, but …* · *I see what you mean, but …* |
| not really | *I'm not sure about that.* · *I don't really agree.* |
| not at all, and stopping | *Let's agree to disagree.* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Different opinions](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/speaking/b1-speaking/different-opinions)
shows two friends disagreeing and staying friends, with the lines:

> I see what you mean, but...
> OK, let's agree to disagree.

[agree to disagree](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/agree_to_disagree): "To accept each other's opinions
and cease arguing; to peacefully acknowledge that an agreement will not be reached."

**Three steps when you disagree.**

1. **Show you heard them.** Say back one true thing from their side: *I see what you mean, the mornings
   are harder.*
2. **Say *but*, gently.** Not louder. *But* is a small word. Don't push it.
3. **Give your view and your reason.** *I think it's better overall, because we finish earlier.*

**The voice.** When people disagree, the voice usually gets louder and faster. Do the opposite: same
level, a little slower, and a small pause before *but*.

```text
   I see what you mean │ (small pause) but I think │ it's BETTER overall.
   (warm, level)                      (level)        (push the view word)
```

**Soft words make it gentler still.** *I'm not sure*, *I don't really agree*, and *maybe* in front of
your view:

> I'm not sure that's right. Maybe it's just the first week.

**Agree to disagree** when neither of you is going to change your mind. Say it with a smile, and then
change the subject.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the flat no**:

> No, that's wrong.

To a native listener this is very strong. It can sound like an insult, even when you only meant *I
think differently*.

The second is ***but* with nothing before it**:

> But it's better.

The listener hears only the disagreement, and none of the respect.

The third is **the louder voice**. The words are soft, *I see what you mean, but*, and the voice is
loud and fast. The listener believes the voice.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers disagree softly almost all the time, especially at work. They agree with a small part
first, then *but*, then their view. It's so common that a direct *no, you're wrong* stands out as rude
or angry.

Under pressure, when you feel strongly or you've been interrupted, the soft start is the first thing
to go. Practise it until *I see what you mean, but* comes out on its own.

In some languages and families, direct disagreement is normal and honest. In English, especially with
people you don't know well, the same words can sound hostile. That's not a judgement of your language.
It's a difference you need to hear.
