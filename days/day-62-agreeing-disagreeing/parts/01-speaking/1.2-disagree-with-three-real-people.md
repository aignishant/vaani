## One-line answer

Find three real opinions you disagree with, from three real people, and answer each one with the three
steps: show you heard them, a gentle *but*, and your view with a reason. Afterwards, notice whether they
answered your reason or defended themselves. An answer to your reason means you disagreed without
offending.

## The story

Your uncle thinks your cousin should study engineering, not music. Your neighbour thinks the new
bus route is a waste of money. Your friend thinks you should never lend money to family.

You disagree with all three, and until now you've said nothing, because the last time you disagreed
with your uncle there was a scene at dinner.

This week you try the three steps with each of them. With your uncle:

> I see what you mean, engineering is safer. But I think she'll work harder at music, because it's what
> she loves.

He doesn't agree. But he says, *Loving it doesn't pay the rent*, and that's an answer to your reason,
not a scene. A little more real talk follows, which hasn't happened in years.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the three steps**: show you heard them, a gentle *but*, your view with a reason, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-agree-half-agree-disagree-softly.md).
- **defend**: to protect yourself when you feel attacked, by arguing back harder or leaving.
- **answer the reason**: when the other person replies to *why* you think something, not to the fact
  that you disagree.

The goal isn't to win. It's to disagree in a way that keeps the other person talking. You can tell
which happened from their first sentence:

| Their first sentence back | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| an answer to your reason: *Loving it doesn't pay the rent.* | they heard you, and the talk goes on |
| the same point, louder: *Engineering is safer!* | they felt attacked and are defending |
| silence, or a change of subject | they felt attacked and left |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the fake first step**: *I see what you mean, but* said quickly, then
straight on, with nothing true said about their side. The listener hears that the words are empty.

The second is **the pile of reasons**: *but* followed by five reasons. It sounds like an attack. One
reason, clearly.

The third is **forgetting to stop**. If they don't change their mind after two turns, it's time for
*let's agree to disagree*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Most real disagreements don't end with anyone changing their mind. They end well or badly. Ending well
means both people still talk the next day. The three steps are for that.

Under pressure, with family or an older person, or when you feel strongly, people either say nothing
or say too much. The table in step two is protection: you decided your one true thing and your one
reason before you felt anything.

A native listener gives *I see what you mean* a lot of weight, if the next few words prove you did.
