## One-line answer

Record the same real disagreement three ways: flat and blunt, soft words in a loud voice, and the three
steps in a level voice. Play them to three listeners and ask *how would you feel if I said that to you?*
A blunt disagreement doesn't sound honest. It sounds like an attack, and the listener stops hearing
your reason.

## The story

In a shop, the man at the counter tells you your phone can't be repaired and you'll need a new one.
Your friend fixed the same problem last month for a few hundred rupees. You say:

> No. That's wrong. It can be repaired.

The man folds his arms. *Then go to your friend.* The conversation's over.

At a second shop the next day, you say:

> I'm not sure that's right. My friend had the same problem last month, and it was fixed for a few
> hundred. Could you check again?

The second man shrugs, opens the phone, and finds the loose part. Same fact, same disagreement. The
first version sounded like an accusation. The second sounded like a question he could say yes to.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **blunt**: said directly, with no softening at all.
- **the three steps**: show you heard them, a gentle *but*, your view with a reason, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-agree-half-agree-disagree-softly.md).
- **mixed message**: soft words said in a hard voice, so the words say one thing and the voice another.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Three ways to say the same disagreement:

| Version | Words | Voice | What the listener feels |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **B**, blunt | *No, that's wrong.* | flat or loud | attacked |
| **M**, mixed message | *I see what you mean, but …* | loud and fast | the voice wins: attacked |
| **S**, soft | *I see what you mean, but …* | level, pause before *but* | heard, and disagreed with |

## Why Vaani needs it

In the phase 4 gate you answer a counter-argument out loud. A listener judges your answer by how it
sounds before they judge what it says. Hearing, on purpose, how a blunt version lands is how you learn
to stop it before it comes out.

**Step two: record B, M and S**, the three versions in the table. B and M are deliberate.

> How would you feel if I said that to you, from 1 (fine) to 5 (really annoyed)?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a B that's secretly soft**. You can't bring yourself to
say it bluntly, so B sounds like S. Say it the way you'd say it on your worst day.

The second is **telling the listeners which version is which**. They'll score what they expect. Don't
say anything about the versions.

The third, in real speech, is **M**: the right words in an angry voice. It's the most common version of
all, because when you feel strongly your voice rises by itself. The words don't save you.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Shopkeepers, officials, managers and relatives all decide in the first second whether you're attacking
them. After that, a person who feels attacked defends, and doesn't listen to reasons.

Under pressure, when you're right and they're wrong, the blunt version feels honest. In English it
usually sounds rude, and it usually gets you less of what you want.

A native listener forgives grammar mistakes in a disagreement easily. They don't forgive the tone
easily, and they may not tell you what went wrong. They just end the conversation.
