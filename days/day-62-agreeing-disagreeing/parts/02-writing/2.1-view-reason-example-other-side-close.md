## One-line answer

An opinion paragraph has five moves: your view in the first line, a reason with *because*, one real
example, one sentence for the other side (*Some people think …, but …*), and a last line that says your
view again in new words. The reason is the part a reader will argue with, so it has to be real.

## The story

The parents' group at your son's school sends a question round: *Should children be allowed to bring
phones to school?* The head teacher wants each family to write a paragraph before the meeting.

Your first draft:

> Phones are bad. Children should not bring phones. Phones are very bad for children. I think no phones.

The head teacher reads forty paragraphs. Yours says *no* four times, and she can't find a single thing
to discuss.

Your second draft:

> In my opinion, children under twelve should not bring phones to school. This is because they stop
> talking to each other at break time. For example, my son's class got phones last year, and the
> playground went quiet. Some parents think phones keep children safe, but the school office has a
> telephone for emergencies. For these reasons, I believe the phones should stay at home.

At the meeting, she reads it out. Three parents argue with your example and two agree with it. That's
what the paragraph was for.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **opinion**: what you think or feel, as opposed to what happened, from
  [day 55](../../../day-55-passing-on-news/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-most-important-thing-first.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, which says what it's about, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- ***because***: the joining word in front of a reason, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-four-joining-words.md).
- **evidence**: something real that proves what you said, from
  [day 51](../../../day-51-describing-people/parts/02-writing/2.1-say-it-then-prove-it.md).
- **the other side**: what people who disagree with you think.
- **closing line**: the last sentence, which brings the reader back to your view.

A paragraph that only says your view again and again gives the reader nothing to think about. An
opinion paragraph gives them a view, and then everything they need to judge it:

| Move | What it does | Words that often start it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. view | the topic sentence: what you think | *In my opinion, …* · *I think …* · *I believe …* |
| 2. reason | why you think it | *This is because …* · *… because …* |
| 3. example | real evidence that the reason is true | *For example, …* |
| 4. other side | what the other side says, and your answer | *Some people think …, but …* |
| 5. close | your view again, in new words | *For these reasons, …* · *So I believe …* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 4 writing gate is a two-paragraph argument, one for and one against, with a topic sentence
in each. This paragraph is the building block of both. Later in the phase you'll write a hundred-and-
fifty-word opinion piece, and in phase 6 an argumentative essay, all made of paragraphs like this.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[An opinion essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/opinion-essay).
Its tips are to write "in clear paragraphs", to express your view with phrases like "I think," "In my
opinion," or "I believe," and to mention other viewpoints with "Some people think". Its example gives a
reason with *because*:

> These games are fun and interactive because you can play them with friends …

**Move 1: the view goes first**, in one sentence, with an opener. The reader should know your view from
this line alone.

**Move 2: one reason**, with *because*. Not three. One strong reason with an example beats three with
none.

**Move 3: the example is real.** Something you saw, did or know: a number, a place, a person, an event.
*For example, my son's class …*, not *For example, children use phones a lot.*

**Move 4: the other side in one sentence**, then your answer after *but*:

> Some parents think phones keep children safe, but the school office has a telephone.

**Move 5: the close doesn't add anything new.** It says your view again, in different words, so the
reader finishes where you started.

**Punctuation to check:** a comma after *In my opinion*, *For example* and *For these reasons* at the
start of a sentence, and a comma before *but* when it joins two full sentences.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the view said four times**, as in the first draft. No reason, no
example, nothing to discuss.

The second is **the example that isn't an example**:

> For example, phones are bad for children.

That's the view again, with *for example* in front. An example is a real case.

The third is **the other side with no answer**:

> Some parents think phones keep children safe.

Full stop. Now the reader thinks you agree with them. The *but* and your answer are the point.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Letters to the council, comments in a residents' group, a paragraph for a school meeting, a review, a
complaint: real opinion writing is almost always one or two short paragraphs, and the ones that change
minds have a real example.

Under pressure, angry about something, people write the view in capitals and skip the reason. Readers
skip it too. The calmest paragraph with the best example usually wins.

A formal reader looks for move 4. A writer who mentions the other side fairly sounds reasonable, and
reasonable writers get listened to.
