## One-line answer

Rewrite your real opinion paragraph on purpose so that the reason is only the view again in other words
(*We should keep the tree because the tree is important*). Then ask a reader *why does the writer think
this?* A circular reason doesn't look wrong on the page. It looks like a reason, until someone tries to
say what it is.

## The story

Your colleague writes a paragraph for the office suggestion box:

> I think we should have a longer lunch break. This is because a longer break is better. For example,
> more time for lunch is good. Some people think the break is long enough, but I think it should be
> longer. So I believe the lunch break should be longer.

Every move is there. The opener, *because*, *for example*, *some people think … but*, the close. It even
looks like the model from the school parents' group.

The manager reads it and writes one word on it: *Why?*

Every sentence was the same sentence: *longer is better*. The paragraph had the shape of a reason and
nothing inside it.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the five moves**: view, reason, example, other side, close, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-view-reason-example-other-side-close.md).
- **circular**: going round in a circle. A circular reason is the view again, in new words.
- **empty move**: a sentence that starts like a move (*For example, …*) but says nothing new.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A real reason answers *why?* with something *different* from the view. A circular reason answers *why?*
with the view again:

| View | Circular reason | Real reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *We should keep the tree.* | *because the tree is important* | *because it keeps the car park cool* |
| *The lunch break should be longer.* | *because a longer break is better* | *because half of us eat at our desks* |

**The test:** can you picture the reason without the view? *It keeps the car park cool* is something
you can picture. *The tree is important* is just the view wearing a different hat.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 4 gate paragraphs need reasons a stranger can weigh. A circular reason fills the space and
passes a quick look, which is why it's dangerous: the writer thinks they've given a reason. This part
shows you once what a reader does with one.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[An opinion essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/opinion-essay).
Its example reason says something *new* after *because*: "These games are fun and interactive because you
can play them with friends". The part after *because* is a fact you can check, not the view again.

**Step one: take your real paragraph** from [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-opinion-paragraph.md). Keep a clean
copy.

**Step two: make copy C**, deliberately circular. Keep every opener, *because*, *for example*, *some
people think … but*, and the close. Replace what comes *after* each with your view again in other words.
Label it *deliberate*.

**Step three: the why test.** Give copy C to one reader and your real paragraph to a different reader.
Ask each:

> Why does the writer think this? Tell me in your own words.
> Could you check any of it?

**Step four: fill the table.**

| Version | Their *why* | Anything they could check? | Did their *why* say something new, not the view again? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C (deliberate) | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| my real paragraph | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader of your real paragraph can only
give your view back as the *why*, or finds nothing they could check. Then your real reason is circular
too. It is **GREEN** when the real paragraph's reader gives a *why* that's different from your view and
names something checkable, and copy C's reader can't.

**Step five: the repair.** If the real paragraph failed, ask yourself *why?* about your own view once more,
out loud, and write the first concrete answer after *because*. Then find a real example for it. Test it
on a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a copy C that sneaks a real reason in**. It's hard to
write nothing on purpose. Check each *because* in C: if you can picture what follows it, it isn't circular
yet.

The second is **a reader who knows your view already** and fills in your reasons. Choose somebody who
doesn't.

The third, in real writing, is **the reason that's only a feeling**: *because I like it*, *because it's
nice*. It isn't quite circular, but it's close, and a reader who doesn't share the feeling has nothing to
weigh.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Managers, councils and teachers read a lot of opinion writing, and circular reasons are the first thing
they learn to spot. The word they write in the margin is always the same: *why?*

Under pressure, when you feel strongly, circular reasons come easily, because to you the view *is* the
reason. Asking yourself *why?* one more time is usually enough to find the real one.

A reader who can say your reason back in their own words, differently from your view, has been given
something. That's the test for every argument you'll write in this phase.
