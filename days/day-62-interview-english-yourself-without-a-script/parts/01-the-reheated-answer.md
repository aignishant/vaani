# The reheated answer, and the one you make in the room

`FN-19` · Day 62

## The one-line answer

A prepared answer about yourself is a meal cooked yesterday and warmed through, and everybody in
the room can taste it — so you stop preparing dishes and start keeping ingredients, and you cook
the answer while they watch.

## The model

There is a small set of questions about you that everybody rehearses. *Tell me about yourself.
What are you like to work with? What are you not good at? Why do you want this? Where do you want
to be?* You've written answers to these. Possibly in your head, possibly on paper, possibly in
another language first.

And that rehearsing is exactly why those are the worst answers you give. They're the only ones
you've memorised, so they're the only ones that sound memorised. The rest of the conversation
sounds like a person, and then a paragraph arrives that sounds like a paragraph.

**Here's what reheating actually sounds like on a recording.** Five things, and you can hear all
five without knowing anything about English:

**The rhythm goes flat and even.** Real talking is lumpy — a rush, a hang, a rush. A remembered
sentence comes out at one steady rate all the way through, because you're not choosing the words,
you're retrieving them.

**The stress goes to the wrong places.** When you mean something, you hit the word you mean. When
you're reciting, the hits land where the *sentence shape* puts them, not where you care. You built
this ear on Day 13 with `PR-06`, and this is where it earns its keep.

**It speeds up.** Memory runs faster than thought, so a recited stretch is quicker than the
speaking either side of it. That change of speed is the loudest clue in the whole thing.

**Nothing interrupts it.** A person thinking about themselves out loud always breaks their own
sentence a little — a beat, a *well*, a half-correction, a *no, that's not quite it*. A memorised
block has none of that. It is suspiciously clean.

**It answers a slightly different question.** The one you prepared for, not the one they asked.

Together those are the **seam** — the join where you drop into the prepared block and the join
where you come back out. You cannot hear the seam from inside your own mouth. You can hear it on
the tape immediately, and so can the person opposite you, which is the uncomfortable bit: the most
polished English on the tape is the least convincing part of it.

**So keep ingredients, not dishes.** Ingredients are things so true you cannot get them wrong, and
none of them is a sentence. Five is plenty:

> **A moment** — one small thing that actually happened. Not an achievement. A Tuesday.
> **A preference** — a kind of work you'd take over another kind, and why, in your own words.
> **A limit** — something you're genuinely not good at, and know you're not.
> **A change** — one thing you think differently about now than you used to.
> **A want** — more of what, less of what.

Those five will answer nearly any question about you, in any order, in whatever words the room
gives you. The same ingredient goes into different dishes. Watch one moment do two jobs:

> *What are you like to work with?* — I suppose the honest answer is I ask a lot of questions
> early on. There was a job last year where I spent the first week just going round asking people
> what actually happened in a normal week, and it drove one of them slightly mad. But we caught two
> things that would've bitten us later.

> *What are you not good at?* — Moving before I understand something. Same job, actually — I spent
> the first week asking everybody what a normal week looked like when a braver person would've just
> started. I'd struggle to do it any other way, and that costs me sometimes.

One ingredient, two answers, both true, neither written down. And the words are different both
times, which is the whole point — because they were made in the room, at the moment of asking.

**And give yourself permission to think out loud.** A beat before you start. A false start you
correct. A *no, that's not quite what I mean*. You have been taught, somewhere, that these are
faults. In an answer about yourself they are the evidence that a person is speaking. Take them out
and you don't sound fluent, you sound rehearsed — and rehearsed is worse, because rehearsed is a
thing the listener has to forgive.

**Today's phrases** — the joins you use to assemble an answer about yourself while you're saying
it. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I suppose the honest answer is … | Starting an answer about yourself you have not pre-written |
| Two things, really — | Announcing the size of your answer before you have the wording |
| I'm at my best when … | Describing yourself by a condition rather than an adjective |
| Where that started was … | Going back to the beginning of your own thread |
| I've been doing it long enough now that … | Letting the years do the boasting instead of you |
| Nobody's ever asked me that in those words. | Buying an honest beat on a question about yourself |
| That's the part I'd want to keep. | Saying what matters to you without claiming to be good |
| I'd struggle to do it any other way. | Owning a preference as a plain fact about you |

## Say it now

Say each line twice, out loud, with your own material. Do not write the material down first — if
it's true you already have it.

1. *I suppose the honest answer is {{what you actually think}}.*
2. *Two things, really — {{one}}, and {{the other}}.*
3. *I'm at my best when {{the condition}}.*
4. *Where that started was {{the moment}}.*
5. *I've been doing it long enough now that {{what's become automatic}}.*
6. *Nobody's ever asked me that in those words. {{Your first honest thought}}.*
7. *That's the part I'd want to keep — {{the part}}.*
8. *I'd struggle to do it any other way, and {{what that costs you}}.*

Now the second pass, and this is the real drill. Take **one** of your five ingredients and run it
through lines 1, 3 and 7 — three different sentences, same underlying material, different words
each time. Then do it again with a second ingredient. You are not practising sentences today. You
are practising the thing where a fixed piece of truth comes out in whatever words the line needs.

- **With a partner:** they ask the five in an order you don't know, and they're allowed to ask a

  answer it was.

## The deliberate failure

Write one answer out, word for word — *what are you not good at* is the easiest to over-prepare —
and learn it properly. Say it until you can do it with your eyes shut and no stumbles at all.

Then start the recorder and answer a **different** question with it. Ask yourself *why do you want
this*, and deliver the memorised paragraph anyway, jammed in whole with a sentence of your own on
either side.

**What breaking sounds like:** the seam, and it is unmistakable on playback. Your own speech goes
in one shape, then the block starts and everything changes at once — quicker, flatter, no beats,
no self-corrections — and then it stops and your own voice comes back, slower and lumpier, and
sounds like a different person entering the room.

Now the finding, and sit with it: the memorised block is your **best** English on that tape. The
grammar is right, the words are better than the ones you'd have found live, nothing collapses. And
it is the only part a listener would not believe. That is a genuinely awkward thing to learn,
because it means the effort you've been putting into preparing these answers has been making them
worse, and it has been making them worse in a way you could never hear from the inside.

## When it breaks in the wild

It isn't the interview. It's the moment before it, or after it. Someone walks with you to the lift
and says, *so what's your background?* — casually, filling a gap. And out comes the paragraph.
Full length, fully formed, in a corridor. You watch their face do a small thing, and then they say
*right, great,* and the conversation is over. Nothing was wrong with the English. The size was
wrong, and the temperature was wrong.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — that was the prepared version. The real one's shorter: {{…}}.

Naming it is the repair. A rehearsed answer that gets acknowledged out loud stops being a
performance and becomes a joke you're both in on, and the next thing you say gets heard as you.

If you found a seam, the fix is not to prepare that answer better. It's to throw the sentences away
and keep only the ingredient underneath, then answer it again cold. Re-recording is normal. It is
not a failed day.
