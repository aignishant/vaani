# Both of you are holding the same rope

`IN-15` · Day 63

## The one-line answer

Pushing back isn't saying no — it's saying *not that, this* — and the two halves have to arrive in
the same breath, because a refusal with nothing attached to it drops the rope you were both
holding.

## The model

Picture a rope with one of you at each end. A negotiation is not a fight over the rope; it's the
two of you keeping enough tension in it to stay upright. That's the whole image and everything
today comes out of it.

**Dropping your end.** *No, that doesn't work for me.* Full stop. You haven't won anything —
there's just no tension anywhere and the rope's on the floor. What actually happens next is that
the other person has to invent your alternative for you, and they'll invent one they prefer.

**Pulling flat out.** *Absolutely not, that's not happening.* The rope snaps. You may be right.
There is now nothing to hold.

**Keeping tension.** You pull, and you leave enough slack that they can pull too. That's a
push-back, and in English it's built from three moves that live in one sentence.

**Move one: take the weight before you resist it.** Show you actually received what they asked
for. *I can do that —* costs you nothing and buys everything, because it removes the thing they
were braced for. You are no longer someone refusing; you are someone with a condition.

**Move two: refuse the term, not the request.** This is the move people get wrong, and it's the
expensive one. *I don't want to do that* refuses the whole thing. *Just not by then* refuses one
word of it — the date — and leaves the rest of the deal alive. The narrower the refusal, the more
survives. So find the actual term that doesn't work. Is it the date? The amount? Who does it? How
much of it? Name that one and nothing else.

> I can do that — just not by then.

Hear how much of that sentence is agreement. Almost all of it. And nobody is in any doubt that
you've said no.

**Move three: put something back on the rope, in the same breath.** *What I could do instead is —*
and then a real alternative. Not a wish, not a complaint. Something you would genuinely do. This
is the move that separates a negotiation from a refusal, and the timing of it is not optional: it
goes in the same breath, before they've had a chance to react to the no. Leave a gap and the no
lands on its own, and now you're arguing about whether you're being difficult instead of about
the date.

> I can do that — just not by then. What I could do instead is get you the first half by then and
> the rest after.

**The trade.** Once you're in the middle of it, one sentence does more work than any other in this
whole day: *if we can {{their move}}, then I can {{your move}}*. It attaches your concession to
theirs so that nothing goes across for free. Every time you give something without attaching it,
you've taught them that things are free here, and the next ask will be bigger.

> If we can drop the second site, then I can do the date.

**Then stop talking.** This is where it actually breaks for most people, and it is not a language
problem at all. You put out a good counter, there's a silence, the silence feels like disapproval,
and you fill it — and what comes out to fill it is a concession nobody asked for. *…or, I mean, I
could probably manage it if I moved some things.* You have just negotiated against yourself out
loud, and it took one breath.

The silence after a counter **is** the tension in the rope. Whoever speaks first has let go.

**And one thing about the voice**, because the same words do different jobs at different settings.
A counter that comes out fast and rising is a question, and a question invites a no. The same
words at your normal speed, with the voice coming down at the end, are a position. You built the
fall on Day 26 with `PR-11` — this is the situation it was for.

One more, cheap and worth having: **give one reason, not two.** Two reasons sound like one weak
one, every time. If you find yourself adding a second, you're persuading yourself.

**Today's phrases** — the sentences that keep tension in a rope you'd rather not drop. All of these
go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I can do that — just not by then. | Taking the weight, then refusing one term and only that one |
| What I could do instead is … | The alternative, in the same breath as the no |
| If we can {{…}}, then I can {{…}}. | The trade — your yes attached to theirs |
| Is there any movement on …? | Testing whether a term is actually fixed or just stated firmly |
| That's further than I can go. | Naming your limit without arguing for it |
| Where does that leave {{…}}? | Making them look at what their version costs |
| Can we meet somewhere in the middle? | Opening the middle deliberately, before either of you drifts there |
| I'd want something back for that. | Refusing to let a concession go across free |
| Let's park that and come back to it. | Taking a stuck term off the table without conceding it |

## Say it now

Say each line twice, with something real in the slots — something someone has actually asked of
you this week.

1. *I can do that — just not by {{the term that doesn't work}}.*
2. *What I could do instead is {{the alternative you'd genuinely do}}.*
3. *Is there any movement on {{the term}}?*
4. *If we can {{their move}}, then I can {{your move}}.*
5. *That's further than I can go — {{your one reason}}.*
6. *Where does that leave {{the thing their version breaks}}?*
7. *I'd want something back for that. {{What}}.*
8. *Let's park {{the stuck term}} and come back to it.*

Then join lines 1 and 2 into a single sentence with no pause between them, and say it six times on
six different asks. One breath, both halves. If you can hear a gap opening up before *what I could
do instead*, close it — that gap is where the argument gets in.

Now the hard second pass. Say line 1 and 2 as one breath, then **close your mouth and keep it
closed until you've taken one full breath in through your nose.** Do that on all six. The mouth has to learn to stop
there, and it will not learn it by you agreeing that it's a good idea.

- **With a partner:** they hold the other end and don't accept the first counter on any of the
  three. They just say *hm* and wait. Your job is to not improve your own offer into the gap.

## The deliberate failure

Take the same three asks and refuse them bare. No weight taken, no term named, no alternative.
Just *no, that doesn't work.* Say it three times, to three asks, into the recorder — and then let
the tape keep running each time. Don't stop it and don't plan what comes next.

**What breaking sounds like:** listen to what you do in the silence, because you will not have
been able to stay in it. On the tape, after the second bare no, your voice drops in volume and
you start explaining. Then you explain again. Then — and this is the one to catch — you offer
something. Nobody asked. Nobody pushed. You conceded into your own pause.

And here's the finding, which is not the one you expect: the bare no doesn't sound rude on
playback. It sounds *weak*. You'd braced for it to sound aggressive, which is exactly why you've
been avoiding it, and the tape says it isn't aggressive at all — it's a person with nothing to
hold, filling a silence, giving ground. The politeness you thought you were protecting was never
at risk. The position was.

## When it breaks in the wild

Six people in a room, and the date comes up. You say the date doesn't work. Somebody says, without
any heat at all, *well, we've already committed to it externally* — and that's it, that's the whole
push, and you fold on the spot. Not because you were convinced. Because you had a no and nothing
behind it, and *we've already committed* left you with no next sentence.

Afterwards you'll think of four things you could have said. All four of them are alternatives, and
alternatives are the thing you have to be holding **before** you open your mouth.

**The repair:**

> I can do that — just not by then. What I could do instead is {{…}}. Where does that leave us?

Then close your mouth. The question at the end is not a softener; it hands them the rope and makes
the next move theirs.

normal. It is not a failed day.
