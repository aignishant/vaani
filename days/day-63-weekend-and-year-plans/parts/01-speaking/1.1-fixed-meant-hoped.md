## One-line answer

Say a plan in the form that matches how sure it is. *I'm meeting Anu on Saturday* for something fixed
with somebody else. *I'm going to paint the kitchen* for something you've decided. *I'd like to* or *I
hope to* for something you want but haven't arranged. The listener hears from the form alone how much to
count on it.

## The story

Monday morning at work. Your colleague asks:

> What are you doing this weekend?

You say:

> On Saturday I'm taking my mother to the eye doctor, at eleven. On Sunday I'm going to clean the
> balcony. And I'd like to see the new film, if there's time.

She hears three different kinds of plan. The doctor is fixed: there's a time and another person. The
balcony is decided, but it's just you. The film is a wish. When she asks *want to see the film on
Sunday evening?*, she's asking about the one plan she heard was still open.

Later, somebody asks *what are your plans for this year?* You say:

> I'm going to look for a bigger flat. And I hope to start a course in the evenings.

The year is mostly *going to* and *hope*. Almost nothing a year away is fixed yet, and your English said
so.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **plan**: something you're going to do in the future.
- **arrangement**: a plan that is fixed with another person, with a time or a place.
- **intention**: something you've decided to do, but haven't fixed with anyone.
- **hope**: something you want to happen, but can't be sure of.
- **-ing form**: the verb with *-ing* on the end. You said *I'm eating* and *they're walking* on
  [day 27](../../../day-27-happening-right-now/parts/01-speaking/1.1-say-what-you-can-see-happening.md)
  for what's happening now. Here the same shape talks about the future.

Plans aren't all equally sure. English has a form for each level, and a listener hears the difference:

| How sure | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **fixed**: arranged with someone, with a time or a place | *I'm* + *-ing* | *I'm meeting Anu on Saturday.* |
| **meant**: decided, not arranged | *I'm going to* + verb | *I'm going to clean the balcony.* |
| **hoped**: wanted, not decided | *I'd like to* · *I hope to* · *maybe* | *I'd like to see the new film.* |

The weekend usually has more fixed plans. The year usually has more *going to* and *hope*.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every conversation about plans, with friends, family, a manager, a landlord, needs this. People plan
around what you tell them. Later this phase you'll talk about what might happen, make suggestions and
choose, and describe a plan step by step. All of that starts with saying one plan at the right level of
sure.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Future forms: 'will', 'be going to' and present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/future-forms-will-be-going-present-continuous)
says the present continuous is used "when the plan is an arrangement – already confirmed with at least
one other person and we know the time and place", and *be going to* for "plans decided before the moment
of speaking", with:

> I'm meeting Jane at 8 o'clock on Saturday.

It also says "the present continuous is often used to ask about people's future plans", as in *What are
you doing this weekend?*

[Talking about the future](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/talking-about-future)
gives *be going to* for "Plans or intentions: I'm going to drive to work today."

**Fixed plans get a time or a person.** That's what makes them fixed, and saying them makes the plan
sound real:

> I'm flying to Kochi on the 12th.
> I'm having dinner with my brother on Friday.

***Going to* is quick and quiet in speech.** The loud word is the verb after it: *I'm going to CLEAN
the balcony.*

**Hopes need a soft word.** *I'd like to*, *I hope to*, *I'm thinking of* + *-ing*, or *maybe*:

> I'm thinking of changing jobs.
> Maybe I'll visit my aunt in June.

**Answering *what are you doing …?*** Start with the most fixed plan, then the less sure ones.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **everything in one form**:

> This weekend I go to the doctor, I go clean the balcony, I go see the film.

The listener can't tell what's fixed. They might plan around the film, or ask about the doctor.

The second is **the hope said as fixed**:

> This year I'm moving to Pune.

If you've only thought about it, the listener now believes it's arranged. They might tell others.

The third is ***I will* for every plan**: *I will meet Anu, I will clean, I will see the film.* It sounds
like a list of promises, or decisions made right now.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

*What are you doing this weekend?* is one of the most common questions at work in English. People
answer it with a mix of forms, almost always starting with something fixed.

Under pressure, when a manager asks about your plans for leave, or a landlord asks when you're moving
out, the level of sure matters. Say *I'm hoping to* if you're not sure, even if it sounds less
confident. A plan said as fixed that then changes costs more than an honest *hope*.

A native listener doesn't notice the forms. They just know, from the form, how much to count on you.
