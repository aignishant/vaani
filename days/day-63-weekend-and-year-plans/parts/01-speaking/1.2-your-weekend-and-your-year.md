## One-line answer

Tell two real people your real plans, one about this weekend and one about this year, with each plan
in the form that matches how sure it is. Then ask each listener *which of those can you count on?* If
they pick out your fixed plans and leave out your hopes, your forms told them the truth.

## The story

Your sister calls on Thursday and asks what you're doing this weekend, because she wants to visit.

> I'm working on Saturday morning, till one. Saturday afternoon I'm going to do the shopping. Sunday
> I'd like to rest, but I'm not sure yet.

She says: *So Sunday then? I'll come Sunday.* She found the one gap, the hope, and filled it.

At the end of the call she asks about the year. You say:

> I'm going to ask for a transfer to the Chennai office. I'd like to be nearer Amma. And I hope to save
> enough for a scooter.

She says: *Tell me when you hear about the transfer.* She understood that the transfer is decided but
not yet fixed. She'll wait for the news instead of telling your mother you're moving.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **fixed**, **meant**, **hoped**: the three levels of plan, and their forms, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-fixed-meant-hoped.md).
- **count on**: to be sure something will happen, and to plan around it.
- **gap**: here, time in your plans that isn't fixed yet.

In a real conversation about plans, the listener is often looking for something: a free day, a
date, whether you'll be there. Your forms are how they find it. The fixed plans tell them what not to
touch. The *going to* and *hope* plans tell them where there might be room.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the flat list**: every plan said the same way, fast. The listener
can't find the gaps.

The second is **no times on fixed plans**: *I'm meeting my cousin.* When? A fixed plan without a time
sounds less fixed than it is.

The third is **too much hope**: *I hope to, I'd like to, maybe, I'm thinking of* for every plan,
including the doctor's appointment. The listener can't count on anything, and may think you're
avoiding them.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Weekend plans are small talk at work and a real negotiation at home. Year plans come up with family,
at interviews and at reunions. People listen for different things in each: gaps in the weekend,
seriousness in the year.

Under pressure, a manager asking about next year, people often make hopes sound fixed to seem
confident. Then the plan changes, and it looks like a broken promise. *I'm going to* and *I hope to* are
confident enough.

A native listener who says *let me know how the transfer goes* heard you exactly right.
