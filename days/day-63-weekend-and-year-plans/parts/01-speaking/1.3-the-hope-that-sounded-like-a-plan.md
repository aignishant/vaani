## One-line answer

Record one real hope twice: once deliberately as a fixed plan (*I'm moving to Pune in March*) and once
honestly as a hope (*I'd like to move to Pune, maybe in March*). Play them to two listeners and ask what
they'd say back. A hope said as fixed doesn't sound confident. The listener believes it and acts on it.

## The story

At a family wedding your aunt asks what's new. You've been thinking about a job in Pune, and you're
excited, so you say:

> I'm moving to Pune in March.

She says *Congratulations!* and hugs you. By the end of the evening, four relatives have asked about
your new job. Your mother phones the next day, upset that she heard it from your aunt.

You haven't applied yet.

What you meant was:

> I'm thinking of moving to Pune. I'd like to go in March, if I get the job.

Your aunt would have said *Good luck!* and nothing would have spread.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **fixed**, **meant**, **hoped**: the three levels of plan, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-fixed-meant-hoped.md).
- **over-sure**: said as more certain than it is.
- **act on**: to do something because you believe what you heard.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

In English, *I'm* + *-ing* with a date sounds arranged. A listener hears *I'm moving to Pune in March*
the way they'd hear *I'm meeting Jane at 8 on Saturday*: done, fixed, safe to repeat.

| What you say | What the listener does |
| --- | --- |
| *I'm moving to Pune in March.* | congratulates you, tells others, plans around it |
| *I'm going to move to Pune.* | asks when, and what's next |
| *I'd like to move to Pune, if I get the job.* | wishes you luck, waits for news |

## Why Vaani needs it

Every plan you talk about this phase, and every answer about your future at an interview in phase 5,
is heard by someone who might act on it. Hearing, on purpose, what a listener does with an over-sure
plan is how you learn to hear it in your own voice.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Future forms: 'will', 'be going to' and present continuous](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/future-forms-will-be-going-present-continuous):
the present continuous for an arrangement "already confirmed with at least one other person and we know
the time and place".
[Talking about the future](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/english-grammar-reference/talking-about-future):
*be going to* for "Plans or intentions".

**Step one: choose one real hope**: something you'd like to do this year that isn't arranged. A move, a
course, a trip, a job, a big purchase.

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **F**, deliberately over-sure: *I'm* + *-ing* + a date, said confidently.
2. **H**, honest: *I'd like to* or *I'm thinking of* + *-ing*, with *if* or *maybe*.

**Step three: the what-would-you-say test.** Play F to one listener and H to a different listener. Tell
each to imagine you said it to them at a family event. Ask:

> What would you say back to me?
> Is it arranged yet?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when H's listener thinks it's arranged, or
congratulates you. Then your honest version still sounds fixed, often because the date was too firm or
*maybe* was too quiet. It is **GREEN** when H's listener says *no, not yet* and wishes you luck, and F's
listener thinks it's arranged.

**Step five: the repair.** If H failed, move the date after *if* or *maybe*, and push the soft word: *I'd
LIKE to*, *MAYBE in March*. Try it on a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **choosing something already arranged**. Then F is simply
true, and nothing is learned. It has to be a real hope.

The second is **a listener who knows your plans** and corrects F from memory.

The third, in real speech, is **the over-sure plan said out of excitement**. It's usually about
something you want very much. That's the one to check before you say it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

News travels fast in families and offices. A plan said as fixed becomes something *everyone knows* by
the next day. Then, if it doesn't happen, you have to explain it to everyone.

Under pressure, at an interview or with a manager, people make plans sound fixed to sound serious.
*I'm planning to* and *I'm hoping to* sound serious and honest at the same time.

A native listener takes your form at its word. They won't ask *is that arranged or just a hope?* They'll
decide from how you said it.
