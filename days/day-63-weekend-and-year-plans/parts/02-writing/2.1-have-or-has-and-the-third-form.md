## One-line answer

To write about experiences, things that happened at some time in your life with no date given, use
*have* or *has* + the past participle: *I have worked with children*, *Have you ever driven a bus?*,
*She has never flown*. The past participle is the verb's third form, and for irregular verbs you have to
learn it.

## The story

You're filling in a form to volunteer at a health camp in your area. The questions are all the same shape:

> Have you ever worked with elderly people?
> Have you ever given first aid?
> Have you ever used a blood pressure machine?

For each one there's a box for *Yes, I have* or *No, I haven't*, and a line for details.

The form doesn't ask *when*. It asks whether, at any time in your life, you have done these things. That's
what *have you ever* means, and your answers have to be in the same shape: *Yes, I have. I have given first
aid twice.*

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **experience**: something you have done or that has happened to you, at some time in your life.
- **present perfect**: the verb form *have* or *has* + past participle: *I have seen*, *she has worked*.
- **past participle**: the third form of a verb, the one after *have*: *worked*, *seen*, *given*.
- **base form**: the plain form of a verb: *see*, *give*, from
  [day 42](../../../day-42-three-sounds-of-ed/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-verbs-that-refuse-ed.md).
- **auxiliary**: a helper verb that makes questions and negatives, from
  [day 44](../../../day-44-time-words-aloud/parts/02-writing/2.1-did-takes-the-past-away.md). Today the
  helper is *have* or *has*.

Every verb has three forms you need for writing about the past:

| Base form | Past simple | Past participle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *work* | *worked* | *worked* |
| *see* | *saw* | *seen* |

For regular verbs, the second and third forms are the same: add *-ed*. For irregular verbs, they're often
different, and the third form is the one that goes after *have*.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Present perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect)
gives the form as "have or has + past participle", and for experiences says: "We often use *never* in
negative sentences and *ever* in questions". Its examples include:

> He's been to ten different countries.
> Have you ever dyed your hair a different colour?

[List of English irregular verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_English_irregular_verbs) gives the
three forms. Some you'll need often:

**The shapes on paper.**

| Shape | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yes | *I/you/we/they have* + participle · *he/she/it has* + participle | *I have driven a truck.* · *She has flown twice.* |
| no | *have/has never* + participle · *haven't/hasn't* + participle | *I have never eaten sushi.* · *He hasn't seen it.* |
| question | *Have/Has* + subject + *ever* + participle + ? | *Have you ever ridden a camel?* |
| short answer | *Yes, I have.* · *No, I haven't.* · *Yes, she has.* | |

Four things to see:

1. ***Has* for he, she and it.** *She has*, never *she have*.
2. **The third form after *have*.** *Have seen*, not *have saw*. *Have gone*, not *have went*.
3. ***Ever* in questions, *never* in negatives.** *Never* already means *not ever*, so don't add *not*:
   *I have never*, not *I haven't never*.
4. **In messages, the contraction.** *I've*, *she's*, *haven't*. On forms and in formal writing, write it out.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the second form after *have***:

> I have went to Delhi.
> Have you ever saw the sea?

The reader sees a mistake at once. *Went* and *saw* are past simple, and they never follow *have*.

The second is ***have* with he or she**:

> She have never flown.

The third is **the double no**:

> I haven't never been abroad.

*Never* is already the no.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Forms, job applications and questionnaires ask *have you ever* all the time, and the person reading them
reads hundreds. A wrong participle is one of the most noticed errors in written English, because it
jumps out on a form.

Under pressure, filling in a form quickly, people write the past simple after *have* because it's the
form they know best. Check every *have* and *has* for the word after it.

A formal reader expects *I have* written out on a form or application, and *I've* in a message to a
friend.
