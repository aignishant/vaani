## One-line answer

Write eight real *Have you ever …?* questions you'd like to ask a real person, and your own true answers
to all eight in short-answer form with one detail each. Check every participle one at a time. Then send
the questions and see whether their answers use the same shape back.

## The story

A new flatmate is moving in next week. You don't know her well, and you want to find out if you'll get
along. So you write her a message with some questions, to start a conversation:

> Hi Meena! Some questions before you move in 😊
> Have you ever shared a flat before?
> Have you ever cooked for more than four people?
> Have you ever had a pet?

And you answer them yourself, so it's fair:

> Me: Yes, I have. I've shared with two people. No, I haven't. I've never cooked for a crowd! Yes, I have.
> I've had a cat.

She replies in the same shape: *Yes, I have, twice. No, never. No, but I've always wanted a dog.* A
conversation, in writing, built from one form.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **present perfect**, **past participle**: *have/has* + the third form, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-have-or-has-and-the-third-form.md).
- **short answer**: yes or no, followed by the person and the small verb the question started with.
  Here: *Yes, I have.* · *No, I haven't.*
- **detail**: one extra fact that makes an answer real.

A *have you ever* question and its answer are a matching pair on the page. The question has *have* or
*has* and *ever*. The short answer repeats *have* or *has*. The detail adds one fact, often in the
present perfect too:

> Have you ever lived abroad?
> No, I haven't. I've never left India.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the question with the second form**: *Have you ever went to Goa?* It's
the error readers notice first, because *went* never follows *have*.

The second is **the answer that changes shape**: *Have you ever cooked for a crowd? — No, I don't.* The
question used *have*, so the short answer uses *have*: *No, I haven't.*

The third is **the detail with a date in the present perfect**: *Yes, I have. I have been there in 2019.*
A date belongs to the past simple, which the next writing day is all about. For now, keep your details
without dates.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

*Have you ever* questions are how people get to know each other in writing: messages to new
colleagues, first chats with flatmates, forms for volunteering, dating profiles, application forms.
Short answers make them quick to reply to.

Under pressure, a long form, people answer *yes* or *no* alone. A short answer with one detail is
much more useful to the reader.

A reader who sees *Yes, I have. I've given first aid twice.* on a volunteer form knows exactly what you
can do.
