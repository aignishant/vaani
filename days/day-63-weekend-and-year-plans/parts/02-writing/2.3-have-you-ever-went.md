## One-line answer

Plant five present-perfect errors in a copy of your real questions and answers: the second form after
*have*, *have* with *she*, the double *no*, a base form after *have*, and a wrong short answer. Give it to
a reader and ask them to circle what looks wrong. These errors don't hide the meaning. They're visible,
and on a form they're the first thing a reader marks.

## The story

A friend applies to volunteer at the health camp and asks you to check her form before she hands it in.
Her answers:

> Have you ever worked with elderly people? — Yes, I have. I have took care of my grandmother.
> Have you ever given first aid? — No, I don't. I haven't never done a course.
> Has anyone in your family work in health? — My sister have been a nurse.

Every answer is true, and the organiser will understand all of them. But there are five errors in three
lines, and the organiser reads a hundred forms. The careful forms go on top.

You mark them together and she rewrites it. Now the form looks like it was written by someone who
pays attention, which is what a health camp wants.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **plant**: to put an error into a text on purpose, from
  [day 46](../../../day-46-describing-a-trip/parts/02-writing/2.3-there-was-two-beds.md).
- **present perfect**, **past participle**: from [part 2.1](2.1-have-or-has-and-the-third-form.md).
- **visible error**: a mistake a reader sees on the page, even when the meaning is clear.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The five common errors, and what they look like to a reader:

| Error | Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| second form after *have* | *I have took care of her.* | *I have taken care of her.* |
| *have* with he, she, it | *My sister have been a nurse.* | *My sister has been a nurse.* |
| double no | *I haven't never done a course.* | *I have never done a course.* |
| base form after *have* | *Has anyone work in health?* | *Has anyone worked in health?* |
| wrong short answer | *No, I don't.* | *No, I haven't.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Forms, applications and CVs in phase 5 are written in this form, and they're read by people who are
choosing between you and other people. A visible error there costs more than anywhere else.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who doesn't read English well**. They won't see
the errors, and the test says nothing. Choose somebody who reads forms or letters in English.

The second is **doing your own hunt straight after planting**. You'll remember where they are. Do it in a
later sitting.

The third, in real writing, is ***have* + a regular past that looks right**: *I have work there*, missing
the *-ed*. It's easy to miss because the word looks familiar. Check every regular verb after *have* for its
*-ed*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

People who read forms, whether an employer, an office or a school, often sort by care before they sort by
content. A form with five visible errors goes lower, however good the answers are.

Under pressure, a form to finish at the counter, fix the one error you make most. Step six tells you
which one that is.

On a form, a *have went* is marked every time, however clear the meaning is.
