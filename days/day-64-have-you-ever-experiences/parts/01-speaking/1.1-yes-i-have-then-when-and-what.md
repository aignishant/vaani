## One-line answer

Answer *Have you ever …?* in three moves: the short answer (*Yes, I have*), then switch to the past
for the story, with a when and one thing that happened (*I went in 2019, and I got lost on the first
day*), then hand the question back (*Have you?*). The switch to the past is where the conversation
comes alive.

## The story

At a wedding you're sitting next to a man you don't know. After a quiet moment he asks:

> Have you ever been to Kerala?

You say:

> Yes, I have.

He nods and looks at his plate. The talk has stopped.

A little later, his wife asks you something similar:

> Have you ever tried the food here before?

This time you say:

> Yes, I have. I came to a wedding in this hall two years ago, and the biryani was so good I asked for
> the cook's number. Have you been here before?

She laughs and says she knows the cook. The three of you talk until the dessert.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **experience**: something you have done, or that has happened to you, at some time in your life.
- **short answer**: yes or no, followed by the person and the small verb the question started with,
  from [day 12](../../../day-12-yes-no-questions/parts/01-speaking/1.2-short-answers.md). Here: *Yes,
  I have.* · *No, I haven't.*
- **past form**: the form of a verb for a finished action: *went*, *ate*, *got*, from
  [day 41](../../../day-41-what-you-did-yesterday/parts/01-speaking/1.1-yesterday-in-six-sentences.md).
- **switch**: to change from one verb form to another in the middle of an answer.
- **hand back**: to give the other person the next turn, from
  [day 38](../../../day-38-hobbies-and-free-time/parts/01-speaking/1.2-say-how-often-and-ask-back.md).

*Have you ever …?* asks about your whole life, with no time given. It uses *have* + a verb's third form:
*have you ever been*, *have you ever eaten*. Your short answer uses the same *have*.

But once you say **when**, the question is about one finished moment, and you switch to the past form:

| Move | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. short answer | *have* | *Yes, I have.* |
| 2. when and what | past form | *I went in 2019. I got lost on the first day.* |
| 3. hand back | *have* | *Have you?* |

## Why Vaani needs it

*Have you ever …?* is one of the most common ways people start talking to strangers, and one of the
most common interview questions: *have you ever worked in a team?*, *have you ever dealt with an angry
customer?* The phase 5 interview answers are built on the same three moves.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Present perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect).
It says the present perfect is used "to talk about life experiences, as our life is also an unfinished
time period", and that "If we say when something happened, or we feel that that part of our life is
finished, we use the past simple", with:

> We visited Russia for the first time in 1992.

**Move 1: the short answer, and no more.** Two or three words, with *have* or *haven't*:

> Yes, I have. · No, I haven't. · Yes, once. · No, never.

**Move 2: switch to the past form**, as soon as you say when or tell what happened:

> I went there in 2019.
> I tried it at my cousin's wedding. It was very spicy!

The switch is audible. You stop saying *have* and start saying *went*, *tried*, *was*. Listen for it
when you play yourself back.

**Move 3: hand back.** A short question that turns it round:

> Have you? · How about you? · Have you ever been?

**The *have* is small and quiet.** The loud words are the experience: *Have you ever been to KERALA?*
— *Yes, I HAVE.* In the short answer, *have* carries the stress because it's the answer.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the answer that stops at move 1**:

> Yes, I have.

It's correct. The conversation dies anyway, because the other person has to think of a new question.

The second is **staying in *have* for the story**:

> Yes, I have. I have been there in 2019 and I have got lost.

A native listener understands, but it sounds wrong, because *in 2019* needs the past form.

The third is **the wrong short answer**:

> Have you ever been to Kerala? — Yes, I did.

The question started with *have*, so the answer is *Yes, I have*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers do this switch without thinking: *Have you ever been to Goa? — Yeah, I went last
year, it was packed.* The *have* is gone after two words, and the rest is a small past story.

Under pressure, with a stranger or an interviewer, people often freeze at move 1 because they're not
sure a story is wanted. It is. One sentence of *when* and *what* is almost always welcome.

A native listener hears *Yes, I have* on its own as a closed door, even when you didn't mean it that
way.
