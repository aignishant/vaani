## One-line answer

Sit with one real person and trade six *Have you ever …?* questions: you ask three, they ask three, and
every answer gets the three moves. Record it, then count how many turns each question produced. A
question that produced four or more turns started a real conversation.

## The story

Your cousin is staying for the weekend. You haven't really talked in years. After dinner there's
a quiet moment, and you say:

> OK, let's play a game. I ask you a *have you ever*, then you ask me one.

You ask: *Have you ever been on a plane?* She says: *Yes, I have. I flew to Delhi for my friend's
wedding. I was so scared I held the stranger's hand next to me! Have you?*

You tell her about your train that was cancelled. She asks: *Have you ever cooked for fifty people?*

By the fourth question, you've stopped counting. You find out more about her in an evening than in
five years of family weddings.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the three moves**: short answer, when and what in the past, hand back, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-yes-i-have-then-when-and-what.md).
- **trade**: to take turns, you then them.
- **turn**: one person's go at speaking, from
  [day 8](../../../day-08-greetings-and-goodbyes/parts/01-speaking/1.1-hello-and-how-are-you.md).
- **follow-up question**: a second question about the same answer, from
  [day 46](../../../day-46-describing-a-trip/parts/01-speaking/1.2-describe-a-real-trip.md).

Some *have you ever* questions start a conversation and some finish in one turn. You find out which by
counting turns on the recording.

| Turns after the question | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| one or two | an answer, and then nothing |
| three | an answer and a hand back |
| four or more | a real conversation: follow-ups, a story, laughing |

## Why Vaani needs it

Asking and answering about experiences is how you get to know people in English, and how interviews
in phase 5 work. In this part you do it with a real person, recorded, so you can hear which answers
opened the talk.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Present perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect):
the present perfect for "life experiences", and the past simple when "we say when something happened".

**Step one: choose a real person** you'd like to know better: a relative, a colleague, a neighbour, a
friend.

**Step two: write your three questions.** Choose ones they'll enjoy. At least one about something
brave or funny, and one about work, study or a skill.

**Step three: ask their permission to record**, and trade: you ask, they ask, three each.

**Step four: answer every one of their questions with the three moves.** Short answer, past when and
what, hand back.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the interview**: you ask all six and they answer. Nobody hands back,
and it feels like a form. Trade.

The second is **the question with no story in it**: *Have you ever eaten rice?* Everyone has, so the
answer is *yes* and nothing more. Ask about something some people have done and others haven't.

The third is **the long story**. Move 2 turns into ten sentences, and the other person never gets
their turn. Two or three past sentences, then hand back.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

*Have you ever …?* is how people fill a quiet moment at a table, on a long journey, or on the first day
of a new job. The questions that work best are ones with a small risk or a small laugh in them.

Under pressure, with someone older or more senior, people answer and never hand back. A hand back is
not rude. It's what keeps you from sounding like you're being interviewed.

A native listener remembers the story in move 2, not the *yes*. That's the part to practise.
