## One-line answer

Record your answers to four real *Have you ever …?* questions you'd answer *no* to, twice: once as a
bare *No, never.*, and once as *No, I haven't, but …* with something you have done or would like to do.
Play them to a listener and count what they say next. A bare *no* doesn't sound honest. It sounds like
*please stop asking*.

## The story

It's your first week at a new job. At lunch, a friendly colleague tries to get to know you:

> Have you ever worked in a big office before?
> No, never.
> Oh. Have you ever been to Mumbai?
> No.
> Right. Have you ever tried the canteen's dosa?
> No.

She gives up and turns to someone else. You weren't being rude. You simply hadn't done those things,
and you answered truthfully.

The next day, someone else asks the same first question, and you say:

> No, I haven't. But I've worked in a shop with twelve people, and that was busy enough! Have you
> always worked here?

She laughs, and tells you about her first job. A *no*, and the conversation still went on.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the three moves**: short answer, when and what, hand back, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-yes-i-have-then-when-and-what.md).
- **bare no**: *No* or *No, never* with nothing after it.
- **bridge**: a small turn after *no* to something you *can* talk about: *but I've …*, *but I'd like
  to …*.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Half of all *Have you ever …?* answers are *no*. A *no* doesn't have a past story in it, so move 2
from part 1.1 has nothing to say. It needs a bridge instead:

| Bridge | Example |
| --- | --- |
| to something close that you *have* done | *No, I haven't. But I've been to Pune, which is near.* |
| to something you'd like to do | *No, never. I'd love to, though. Is it nice?* |
| to a reason | *No, I haven't. I don't like flying, to be honest.* |

After the bridge, hand back as usual.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 interview will ask *have you ever …?* about things you haven't done. *No, I haven't, but
…* is exactly what an interviewer wants to hear. Hearing, on purpose, how a bare *no* ends a friendly
conversation is how you stop giving one.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Present perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect):
"We often use *never* in negative sentences and *ever* in questions", and the present perfect is used
"to talk about life experiences".

**Step one: write four real *no* questions**, things people might ask you that you haven't done.

**Step two: record two versions of each answer.**

1. **B**, deliberately bare: *No.* or *No, never.* and stop.
2. **R**, with a bridge and a hand back.

**Step three: the what-next test.** Play the eight answers, mixed up, to a listener. Before each, say
the question aloud. After each answer, ask:

> What would you say next?

Write down their answer word for word.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when any R answer gets *nothing*, *OK* or a
new question on a different subject. Then that bridge didn't work. It is **GREEN** when every R answer
gets a reply about what you said, and most B answers get *OK* or silence.

**Step five: the repair.** For every R that failed, try a different kind of bridge from the table and
test it on a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a B said with a smile and a long pause**, which
softens it into an invitation. Say B the way you would if you were tired.

The second is **a bridge that's a lie**. Don't claim something close you haven't done. *I'd like to*
and *I don't like* are always true bridges.

The third, in real speech, is **the apologetic no**: *No, sorry, never, sorry.* It makes the other
person feel they asked something wrong. There's nothing to apologise for.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Friendly people ask *have you ever* to find common ground. When the answer is *no*, they're still
looking for it, and a bridge helps them find it.

Under pressure, at an interview, *No, I haven't* can feel like failure. *No, I haven't, but I've …*
turns it into evidence of something else you can do.

A native listener hears *No, never.* with nothing after it as a closed door. They'll usually be polite
and move on, and not try again.
