## One-line answer

A paragraph about things you've done opens with a frame in the present perfect, which says what you
have done in your life (*I have worked with children in three different places*). Then it switches to
the past simple for the details, each with a when (*In 2019, I taught at a summer camp*). The frame
says *what*. The details prove it.

## The story

Your neighbourhood has started a skills-sharing group: people write a short paragraph about what they
can do, and others ask for help. Your first try:

> I have taught children. I have taught at a summer camp in 2019. I have helped at the school. I have
> made posters.

The group organiser writes back kindly: *Thanks! When was the school? What did you help with?*

Your second try:

> I have worked with children in three different places. In 2019, I taught English at a summer camp
> for children. Last year, I helped at my daughter's school every Friday, and I made all the posters
> for their sports day. I have never taught a full class alone, but I would like to.

Two neighbours ask you to help their children with homework. The frame told them *what*. The details
told them *enough to trust*.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **present perfect**, **past participle**: *have* or *has* + the third form, from
  [day 63](../../../day-63-weekend-and-year-plans/parts/02-writing/2.1-have-or-has-and-the-third-form.md).
- **past simple**: the past form for a finished action at a known time: *taught*, *helped*, *made*.
- **topic sentence**: the first line, which says what the paragraph is about, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **frame**: here, the present-perfect sentence that sums up an experience with no time given.
- **detail**: a past-simple sentence with a when that shows the experience is real.

The paragraph has a pattern that repeats:

| Part | Verb form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| frame (the topic sentence) | present perfect, no time | *I have worked with children in three different places.* |
| detail | past simple + a when | *In 2019, I taught at a summer camp.* |
| detail | past simple + a when | *Last year, I helped at my daughter's school.* |
| a *never* (optional) | present perfect | *I have never taught a full class alone.* |
| close | what you'd like, or can do now | *but I would like to.* |

**The switch rule:** as soon as a sentence has a **when**, like *in 2019*, *last year*, *when I was
twenty* or *on my first day*, the verb is past simple.

## Why Vaani needs it

CVs, cover letters, application forms and *about me* profiles in phase 5 are all written like this: a
general claim, then dated evidence. And the reader of those is deciding whether to trust you.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Present perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect).
The present perfect is used "to talk about life experiences, as our life is also an unfinished time
period", and "If we say when something happened, or we feel that that part of our life is finished, we
use the past simple", with:

> We visited Russia for the first time in 1992.
> I went to three different primary schools.

**Rule one: the frame has no time word.** *I have worked with children.* Adding *in 2019* to it breaks
it.

**Rule two: every detail has a time word, and a past-simple verb.** *In 2019, I taught …*

**Rule three: a comma after a time phrase at the front.** Short ones like *last year* can go without
one, but in this course, put the comma in so the reader sees where the time ends:

> In 2019, I taught English at a summer camp.

**Rule four: two or three details are enough.** More than that, and the paragraph becomes a list.

**Rule five: *never* belongs in the present perfect.** *I have never taught a class alone.* It's about
your whole life, with no time.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the time word in the present perfect**:

> I have taught at a summer camp in 2019.

A careful reader sees it at once. *In 2019* needs *I taught*.

The second is **all frame, no detail**: four present-perfect sentences, no times. The reader has a list
of claims and nothing to trust them with.

The third is **all detail, no frame**: *In 2019, I taught. Last year, I helped.* The reader has to work
out for themselves what all this adds up to.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Profiles, applications and letters of introduction are all built from a frame and dated details, and
the dates are what an employer or a stranger checks first.

Under pressure, a form with a small box, people write only frames: *I have experience with children.*
One detail with a date is worth three frames.

A formal reader marks a time word with *have* every time. It's also one of the easiest errors to fix,
because the time word shows you exactly where to look.
