## One-line answer

Write a real paragraph of about a hundred words about things you've done, for a real reader who might
need to know. Use a frame, two or three dated details and a close. Then ask the reader *what can this
person do, and how do you know?* If they give your frame and point to a detail, the paragraph worked.

## The story

A local clinic is looking for volunteers to help at a vaccination camp. The notice says: *Write a few
lines about your experience.*

You've never worked in a clinic. But you've done things that matter. You write:

> I have helped at large events many times. In 2022, I managed the queue at my building's blood
> donation day, and nearly two hundred people came. Last Diwali, I organised the food stall at our
> temple with four other volunteers. I have never worked in a clinic, but I am calm with crowds and I
> can speak Hindi, Marathi and some English.

The clinic's coordinator phones you and says: *You did the blood donation queue? Perfect, you're on
the entrance.* She didn't care about the clinic. She picked the detail that proved what she needed.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **frame** and **detail**: the present-perfect summary and the dated past-simple proof, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-the-frame-and-the-detail.md).
- **close**: the last sentence: what you can do now, or would like to do.
- **audience**: the reader a piece is written for, from
  [day 38](../../../day-38-hobbies-and-free-time/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-your-paragraph.md).

Choosing which details to include is most of the work. You've done hundreds of things. The paragraph
has room for two or three, and the right ones are the ones your reader needs.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the first piece in this course that's close to a CV line or a cover letter paragraph, which
come in phase 5. It's also the shape of every *about me* you'll ever write for a group, a job or a
course.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Present perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect):
the present perfect for "life experiences", and the past simple "If we say when something happened".

**Step one: choose a real reader and a real need**: a group, a job, a course, a volunteer role, a
community page. Write down in one line what they need to know about you.

**Step three: choose two or three** that best match the reader's need.

**Step four: write the paragraph.** A frame with no time word, the details each with a time word and
past simple, an honest *never* if it helps, and a close. About a hundred words.

**Step five: the verb check.** Underline every time word. Is the verb next to it past simple? Circle
every *have* and *has*. Is there no time word in that sentence?

**Step six: the trust test.** Give it to your reader, or somebody like them, and ask:

> What can this person do, and how do you know?

If they name your frame and point to a detail, it worked. If they only say *they've done a lot*, the
details weren't specific enough.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the detail that doesn't match the reader**: a paragraph for a clinic
about your cooking. True, and no use to them.

The second is **the vague detail**: *Last year, I did some volunteering.* Where? What? The reader can't
picture it. Add a place, a number or a job.

The third is **hiding the *never***. If the reader needs to know you haven't done something, it's better
to say it and bridge it than to be found out later.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Coordinators, managers and teachers read short experience paragraphs quickly and pick out one detail
to decide on. You can't know which one they'll pick, but you can make sure every detail is concrete.

Under pressure, applying for something you really want, people list everything. Two good details
persuade more than eight vague ones.

A reader trusts a paragraph with dates and numbers more than one without. The past simple and a time
word are how you give them.
