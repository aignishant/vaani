# The one place the bag gives

`PR-16` · `FL-09` · Day 64

## The one-line answer

Overload a bag and it doesn't fail all over — it fails in one place, and for any given bag it's
always the same place, and today you find out where your English gives when the weight goes on.

## The model

You already know pressure makes your English worse. What you probably believe is that it makes it
worse *generally* — everything sags a bit, the whole thing gets shakier. That is not what a
recording shows.

What a recording shows is a bag. Load it up and one thing goes: the handle, or the seam down the
side, or the bottom. Not all three. And which one it is has nothing to do with what you put in it
— it's a property of the bag. Two people can be given the same hard question and come out with
completely different damage, and each of them will get the *same* damage every single time.

So nobody can tell you what yours is. Not a teacher, not a partner, not this document. This is the
same job as Day 29, where you found the sounds your mouth substitutes without telling you — and
the same method, too. Put the load on, record it, and look at the wreckage afterwards.

**Six things that go, and how to hear each one.** You're looking for exactly one.

**Speed.** Everything gets quicker under load. Words start landing on each other's ends and you
arrive at the end of a sentence before you've decided how it finishes. *The test:* find a calm
stretch and a pressured stretch on the same tape and play them one after the other. Is the second
one noticeably quicker than the first?

**A sound.** The key you found on Day 29 goes straight back to its substitute, because pressure
takes attention and that sound was the one costing attention. *The test:* take the pair you know
is yours and find it in the pressured stretch. Are the two words still different there?

**Grammar.** The small endings go first, always in the same order — the *s* on *he does*, the *ed*
on the back of a past verb, then articles, then *have* and *has*. *The test:* write out one
pressured sentence off the tape, word for word, exactly as you said it. Then read it back. This is
uncomfortable and it is the most informative thing in the day.

**The filler.** One word multiplies. Not several — one, and it's yours. *The test:* pick your
filler, count it in the calm stretch, count it in the pressured stretch. Two numbers.

**Volume, and the ends of sentences.** You get quieter as the sentence goes on and the last two or
three words fall off it entirely. *The test:* can you make out the final word of every sentence on
the pressured stretch? Try it with the recording turned down a bit.

**The sentence itself.** You start one shape and finish another, or abandon it halfway and begin
again. *The test:* count the sentences you didn't finish.

Six candidates. **You are looking for one.** Not a list, not a general impression — the one that
went first, before the others, in the specific place on the tape where the load landed.

**Putting the load on.** You don't need a room full of people and you don't need to time anything.
Three loads, all available to you alone:

> **No thinking beat.** Record the questions in your own voice first, in an order you then forget.
> Play them back and start speaking the instant a question ends. Not a beat.
> **The one you'd least like.** Ask yourself the question you have actually been dreading. You know
> which one it is. It's the load, not the topic, that matters.
> **The interruption.** Partway through an answer, cut yourself off with a second question, out
> loud, and carry on from wherever you land.

**Now the second half, which is the half people skip.** Finding it is interesting; bracing it is
what changes a recording. And a brace is something you do with your **mouth**, in the moment, not
something you resolve to be better at.

> **If speed goes** — put a full stop in. Close your lips on the last word of a sentence and take
> one breath in through your nose before the next one starts. You cannot accelerate through a
> closed mouth. This is mechanical and it works immediately.
> **If a sound goes** — slow the one word, not the sentence. Land that consonant properly, then
> carry on at your normal speed. One word, not a general slowing down.
> **If grammar goes** — shorten. One clause and stop. Endings die inside long sentences, so stop
> building them while the load is on.
> **If the filler goes** — put it behind closed lips. What comes out of a shut mouth is silence,
> and silence was made legal on Day 45 by `FL-07`.
> **If the ends fall off** — aim the sentence at its last word instead of its first. Push the end.
> **If sentences get abandoned** — finish the wrong one anyway, then say the right one. That's the
> Day 17 move, and it costs less than an abandoned sentence does.

One brace, in one place, chosen tonight. Not six. And it goes into `docs/ERRORS.md` as a row
tonight, in plain words — the thing that goes and the brace you picked — because Day 67 reads that
ledger and works out what your top four actually are. A finding that stays in your head is a
finding this course cannot use.

**Today's phrases** — braces, said out loud, so the mouth has somewhere to go when the load lands.
All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Let me slow that down. | Announcing the brace out loud so that you actually apply it |
| Right — from the top. | Restarting a sentence that got away from you, without apology |
| One thing at a time. | Cutting a pressured answer down to a single item |
| I'll take that in two parts. | Splitting an answer so neither half has to be rushed |
| Sorry, I'm running away with myself. | Naming the speed while it's happening |

## Say it now

Say each line twice — once flat, once as though you'd genuinely just lost control of a sentence.
The second version is the one you'll actually need.

1. *Let me slow that down. {{The same point, one clause}}.*
2. *Right — from the top. {{Start the sentence again}}.*
3. *One thing at a time — {{the first thing}}.*
4. *I'll take that in two parts. First, {{part one}}.*
5. *Sorry, I'm running away with myself. {{Where you actually were}}.*
6. *Let me get this the right way round — {{the point, in order}}.*
7. *That came out faster than I meant. {{Say it again, slower}}.*
8. *I've lost the end of that sentence. {{Finish it}}.*

Then run all eight straight through, and this time close your lips completely at the end of every
line and take one breath in through your nose before the next one. Do the whole run that way,
twice. That closed mouth is the brace itself, and it needs to be in your body before it's needed,
not discovered on the day.

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d64-free.m4a`.**

> Two stretches in one file, with no gap between them. **First, calm:** talk about something easy
> and familiar until you've properly settled. **Then, loaded:** answer three hard questions with
> all three loads on — the question you least want, no thinking beat, and one interruption you
> impose on yourself partway through. Don't stop for anything.

- **Solo form:** as written. The calm stretch is not a warm-up; it's the control you'll compare
  everything against, so record it properly and don't skip it.
- **With a partner:** they ask the three, in an order you don't know, and they're allowed to
  interrupt once. They don't help, they don't nod, and they don't react.

## The deliberate failure

Now overload it on purpose. Take the hardest of the three questions and answer it again with one
instruction: **do not pause for anything.** No thinking, no hesitation, no going back. Whatever
arrives, say it. Push past the point where it stops being comfortable and keep going.

Then play it back with a single job, and do not let yourself do any other job: **find the first
thing that went, and the exact moment it went.** Not everything that's wrong with it. The first
thing.

**What breaking sounds like:** it's one thing, and everything else is fine. That's the finding, and
it's a strange one to sit with. You'd assumed pressure degraded the whole of your English, and here
is your worst recording proving that most of it held up perfectly well — the words came, the
meaning arrived, somebody would have understood you throughout. One thing gave. And there's a fair
chance it isn't the one you would have named beforehand, because the thing you *worry* about and
the thing that actually goes are rarely the same thing. What you worry about is what you know is
weak. What goes is whatever is costing you attention, and you don't get to know that from the
inside.

## When it breaks in the wild

You're on a call with no faces. A question comes in that you weren't ready for and the thing that
always goes, goes — and because there's no face to read, nothing tells you it's happening. So you
do the one thing that makes it worse: you keep going, and you go faster, because the silence on the
other end feels like something has gone wrong and speed feels like the fix. By the end of the
answer you've stopped being able to hear yourself at all.

**The repair:**

> Let me slow that down. One thing at a time — {{the first thing}}.

Say it out loud, to them, not to yourself. The out-loud version does something the internal version
doesn't: it forces your mouth to make the shape of a slow sentence, and the next sentence comes out
of the mouth that just made it.
