# The song and the singer

`PR-17` · Day 65

## The one-line answer

An accent is not an error — the only things on your list are the ones that turn a word into a
different word or put the beat on the wrong syllable, because only those cost the listener
anything, and everything else is your voice and there is nothing there to fix.

## The model

Anybody can sing a song. What makes it that song is two things: the words, and where the beat
falls. Get either badly wrong and people hear a different song, or no song at all — they sit there
trying to work out what you're singing. Everything else in a performance is the singer. The voice,
the colour of it, the way it leans. Nobody in the history of music has been told their voice was a
mistake.

That's the whole of today, and it needs saying plainly before anything else: **there is no
recording of English anywhere that is the definitive one.** No voice you should be moving towards.
People say this course is about losing something, and it isn't. You're doing one job — being
understood the first time — and most of what you have been quietly embarrassed about has nothing to
do with that job.

So there are three piles, and they are very different sizes.

**Pile one, change: a sound that lands on a different word.** Not *a sound that's unusual*. A sound
that produces a real other word, so the listener receives the wrong one and doesn't know it. This
is the pile you started building on Day 29 with `PR-12`, and it's small — usually two or three
items for anybody.

The test is mechanical. Say the word. Then say the word it turns into. **Are both of them real
words?** If they are, it's on the list, because there is now a sentence somewhere in your future
where the wrong one fits and nobody notices. If the second one isn't a word at all, the listener
gets there anyway from the rest of the sentence — it costs them a fraction of a moment and it goes
to the bottom of the list, not the top.

**Pile two, change: the beat on the wrong syllable.** This one is bigger than people think, and it
is the single most useful thing in today.

A listener finds a word partly by its shape — which syllable is loud, which are quiet. That shape
is what they're matching against. Move the beat and the word stops matching anything at all, so
they don't hear a mispronounced word. They hear *no* word, and then they have to go back and
rebuild your whole sentence around a gap. That is why this costs so much more than a wrong sound
does. A wrong sound leaves a word that's findable. A wrong beat leaves a hole.

Try it now, out loud, on a word you use every day at work. Say it with the beat where you normally
put it. Then say it again with the beat one syllable further along. Say both to yourself twice:

> {{your word}} — {{your word, beat moved}}

The second one probably feels wrong to your mouth, which is good — that means you already know
where the beat lives for that word. The words to worry about are the ones where you *don't* know,
and Day 8 gave you `PR-04` to find them.

**Pile three, keep: everything else, and it's nearly everything.** The colour of your vowels where
they don't collide with another word. Your rhythm. The way you land your own name. Two words on a
phone and somebody knows it's you before you've said who's calling — that is not a defect that
survived, that is the thing that makes you a person rather than a voice. Nobody in a real
conversation has ever been marked down for a vowel that cost them nothing, and if you go looking
you will not find a single moment in your life where one did.

**And the thing not to chase: sounding like somebody else.** An impression of a voice that isn't
yours. This is worth being specific about, because it doesn't fail for the reason people expect —
it isn't about pride, or authenticity, or anything you have to have a view on.

It fails because it makes you **less clear, not more.** Your mouth only has so much attention. Point
it at performing a voice and it comes off the consonants, which is where legibility actually lives.
You'll hear it on a tape today: while you're chasing, the speed drops, the fillers go up, and the
words get harder to catch — the exact thing you were trying to fix, made worse by the fixing.

**The test, and it is the only one you need.** Not *was that right* — you have no way to judge that
from inside your own head, and you never have. The question is:

> **Did that cost the listener anything?**

Which is answerable, because it's about events, not opinions. Did anyone ask you to repeat it? Did
anyone say a different word back to you? Did you have to say it twice? Did the conversation stop
and go backwards? If none of those happened, it cost nothing, and it is not on your list, however
different it sounds from somebody else's mouth.

**Today's phrases** — the small repairs for the things that do cost something, and the sentence for
the things that don't. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Did that word come through? | Checking one specific word instead of the whole sentence |
| Shall I spell it? | Handing over a word that keeps not landing, without a fuss |
| The stress goes on the second bit — {{…}}. | Repairing by moving the beat, not by repeating louder |
| Sorry, the weight's at the front: {{…}}. | The same repair the other way round |
| Is it {{…}} or {{…}}? I never remember. | Asking about a beat without apologising for your mouth |
| Not a different word — the same one. | Clearing up a mishearing caused by one vowel |
| Tell me if I lose you. | Setting the terms before a long stretch on a hard subject |
| That's just how I say it. | Declining, pleasantly, to treat your voice as a fault |

## Say it now

Say each line twice. For the slots, use words from your own life — the place you're from, the
technical words of your work, your own name.

1. *Did that word come through — {{your word}}?*
2. *Shall I spell it? {{Your word}}.*
3. *The stress goes on the second bit — {{your word}}.*
4. *Sorry, the weight's at the front: {{your word}}.*
5. *Is it {{your word, one beat}} or {{your word, the other beat}}? I never remember.*
6. *Not a different word — the same one. {{Your word}}.*
7. *Tell me if I lose you — {{the subject}} is a lot of new words at once.*
8. *That's just how I say it.*

Then take three words from your own life that carry real weight — your name, where you're from,
the thing you do — and say each one three ways: with the beat where you put it, one syllable
further along, and one syllable earlier. Nine goes altogether. You are not choosing a winner. You
are hearing that the beat moves and that moving it changes the word into a hole.

Last, say line 8 once more on its own, at your normal speed, with your voice coming down at the
end. Not defensively. It's a fact, said the way facts are said.

## The deliberate failure

Take one stretch of the same material and chase. Do the impression — copy a voice that isn't
yours, all of it, the vowels and the melody and the lot. Commit to it properly for the whole
stretch; a half-hearted version won't show you anything.

**What breaking sounds like:** two things at once, and it's the second one that matters.

The first is easy to spot: you sound like somebody doing an impression. Fine, expected, not very
interesting.

The second is the finding. Play it back listening only for the words, and you'll notice they're
**harder to catch than in your ordinary stretch**, not easier. The ends of words go missing.
Consonants get soft. Your speed drops, and the fillers come up, because you're now doing two jobs
with one mouth. You set out to be clearer and you made yourself less legible — and if you ran the
only test that matters over that stretch, it would cost a listener more than your own voice ever
has. That is the argument against chasing, and it isn't a moral one. It's on the tape.

## When it breaks in the wild

You say a word — a place, a name, a term from your work — and it doesn't land. They ask again. You
say it again and it still doesn't land, and then somebody helpfully says it back to you a different
way. And from there you go quiet. Not because of that one word: because you've just taken it as
evidence about your accent in general, and now every word you're about to say is being checked
before it leaves. The rest of that conversation is you saying much less than you know.

One word failed. You put the whole voice on the list.

**The repair:**

> Sorry — {{the word}}, weight at the front. Did that come through?

Fix the one word, at the level of the beat, and then carry straight on at your normal volume in
your normal voice. The carrying on is the repair. The word was a word; it was never a verdict.
