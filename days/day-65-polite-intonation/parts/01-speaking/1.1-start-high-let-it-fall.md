## One-line answer

To sound kind in English, start the important word a little higher and let your voice fall a long way
on it: *THANK you*, *PLEASE*, *SORry*. A voice that hardly moves sounds bored or cold, even when every
word is polite.

## The story

You're at the pharmacy counter. The woman finds your medicine after searching for a long time. You're
tired, so you say it the way you'd say a phone number:

> Thank you.

Flat. Low. She looks up and says, a little sharply, *You're welcome*, and turns away.

The next week the same woman helps you again. This time you start higher on *thank* and let your voice
slide down:

> THANK you. That's really kind.

She smiles and tells you the cheaper brand will be back on Monday.

Same two words. The first time they sounded like *finally*. The second time they sounded like
*thank you*.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **pitch**: how high or low your voice is, from
  [day 24](../../../day-24-voice-up-and-down/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md).
- **intonation**: the tune of your voice going up and down across a sentence, from
  [day 24](../../../day-24-voice-up-and-down/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md).
- **the turning word**: the last loud word, where the tune turns, from
  [day 52](../../../day-52-question-statement-intonation/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-tune-turns-on-the-last-loud-word.md).
- **range**: how far your voice moves between high and low.
- **warm**: sounding friendly and kind.
- **cold**: sounding unfriendly, bored or annoyed.

On day 24 you learned that the voice goes down at the end of a statement and up at the end of a
yes/no question. Today is about **how far** it goes.

The same fall can be small or big:

```text
   warm:     THANK                   cold:   thank you
                 \                           ───────── (small fall, low start)
                  \
                   you
```

A big fall from a high start sounds warm and interested. A small fall from a low start sounds flat,
tired, or annoyed. Nothing about the words changes. Only the range.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every thank-you, apology and request you say in English is heard through this. In phase 5 you'll deal
with angry customers, landlords and offices. A polite sentence in a cold voice makes those
conversations worse, and nobody will tell you why.

**The page checked for the rule:** [Intonation (linguistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_(linguistics)).
Among the jobs intonation does, it lists an "attitudinal function (for expressing emotions and
attitudes)", and gives this example:

> a fall from a high pitch on the 'mor' syllable of 'good morning' suggests more excitement than a
> fall from a low pitch

That's today's rule: **how high the fall starts changes what the listener feels.**

**Where the fall goes.** On the loud syllable of the important word, which is usually the last one:

> THANK you. · SORry. · Could you HELP me? · That's really KIND.

**Start that syllable higher than the rest.** Not louder. Higher. Then let it slide down.

**For a request, you can add a small rise at the end.** On day 52 you heard the voice go up on a
yes/no question. In a polite request the voice falls on the loud word and then lifts a little at the
end. This is the fall-rise, which the reference page describes: it "falls and then rises".

> Could you pass the SALT? ↘↗

It sounds softer than a plain fall, because it leaves room for *no*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the flat thank-you**:

> thank you

Low, level, fast. It sounds like *OK, go away*.

The second is **the flat request**:

> Can you give me the file.

Every word polite enough, no movement at all. It sounds like an order.

The third is **the sorry with no fall**: *sorry* said quickly and level while pushing past someone. It
sounds like you're not sorry.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers change their range all the time: a wide, warm range with customers and friends, a
narrow one when they're tired or annoyed. Listeners read the range before the words.

Under pressure, tired, stressed or speaking a language that isn't yours, the range shrinks. Your
voice goes flat without you noticing. That's why beginners are sometimes heard as rude when they're
only concentrating.

Many languages use a narrower range for everyday politeness than English does. If yours does, your
normal polite voice may sound cold in English. It's not your manners. It's your range.
