## One-line answer

Ask five real small favours and say five real thank-yous to real people, each with a high start and a
big fall. Record the lines first, then notice what people give back: a smile, an extra word, a small
extra help. Warm intonation shows up in what people do next.

## The story

You decide to try it for one ordinary day.

At the tea stall: *Could you make it LESS sweet today?* ↘↗ — the man laughs and says *for you, yes*.

At the bank: *THANK you, that was very CLEAR.* — the clerk writes her direct number on your slip.

On the bus: *SORry, could I just get PAST?* ↘↗ — the man with the big bag stands up and moves it.

None of it is big. But at the end of the day you count: five favours, five thank-yous, and three people
did a little more than they had to.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **high start and big fall**: the warm tune from [part 1.1](1.1-start-high-let-it-fall.md).
- **fall-rise**: the voice falling on the loud word and lifting a little at the end, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-start-high-let-it-fall.md).
- **favour**: a small thing you ask somebody to do for you.
- **reaction**: what a listener does while you speak, and here also just after, from
  [day 43](../../../day-43-story-with-three-parts/parts/01-speaking/1.2-tell-one-real-story.md).

This part takes the tune out of the recorder and into real life. You can't record other people without
asking, so you record your own lines first, practise them until the tune is automatic, and then write
down what happened when you used them.

## Why Vaani needs it

In spoken English, a lot of politeness is carried by the voice. Phase 5 is full of real people you need something from:
a manager, a landlord, a clerk, a customer. A warm tune is how they decide to help you a little more.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the performance**: the tune is so big it sounds like a children's
teacher or like sarcasm. A little higher is enough. If it sounds strange to you, ask a listener.

The second is **only the thank-yous**. People remember to be warm at the end and forget the request. The
request is where the fall-rise does its work.

The third is **warm words, looking away**. Look at the person when you say the loud word.
They read your face along with your voice.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Shopkeepers, clerks and drivers hear hundreds of flat thank-yous. A warm one stands out, and people
often reply to it with something extra: a tip, a cheaper option, a smile.

Under pressure, at the end of a long day, in a queue, the range shrinks first. The ten recorded lines
give your mouth a habit that survives a bad day.

A native listener rarely thinks *that person's intonation was warm*. They think *nice person*.
