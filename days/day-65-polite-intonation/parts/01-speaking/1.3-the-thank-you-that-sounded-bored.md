## One-line answer

Record six real polite lines twice, once flat on purpose and once with a high start and a big fall, and
play them mixed up to a listener who rates each one from *cold* to *warm*. Polite words in a flat voice
don't sound neutral. They sound bored or annoyed, and the listener trusts the voice over the words.

## The story

You call a delivery company because a parcel is late. You've practised your English carefully and every
sentence is polite:

> Good morning. Could you please check my order? Thank you very much. Sorry to trouble you.

But you're nervous, so it all comes out on one low, level note. The woman on the phone answers
shortly and says she'll *look into it*.

Your sister takes the phone and says almost the same words, with her voice moving: *Good MORning!
Could you possibly CHECK an order for me?* The woman's voice changes at once. She finds the parcel
while they're talking.

Your words were perfect. Your range was the problem.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **range**, **warm**, **cold**: how far the voice moves, and how friendly it sounds, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-start-high-let-it-fall.md).
- **flat**: said with almost no movement up or down.
- **blind test**: a test where the listener doesn't know which version is which.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The words say *polite*. The voice says *warm* or *cold*. When the two disagree, the listener believes the
voice:

| Words | Voice | What the listener hears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| polite | wide range, high start, big fall | kind |
| polite | flat, low, small fall | bored, annoyed, or *I don't mean it* |

## Why Vaani needs it

On the phone, in a complaint, at an interview or with an angry customer in phase 5, the voice is often
all the other person has. Hearing, on purpose, how cold your own polite English can sound is how you
learn to hear it before you say it.

## The rule

[Intonation (linguistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intonation_(linguistics)): intonation has an
"attitudinal function (for expressing emotions and attitudes)", and "a fall from a high pitch on the
'mor' syllable of 'good morning' suggests more excitement than a fall from a low pitch".
[please](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/please), [sorry](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sorry),
[thank you](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/thank_you): the three words with audio.

**Step one: choose six real polite lines**: two greetings or thank-yous, two apologies and two requests
you'd really say, for example on the phone.

**Step two: record each twice**: **F**, deliberately flat, and **W**, warm. That makes twelve. Mix them up
and keep a hidden list.

**Step three: the blind test.** Play the twelve lines to a listener who doesn't know which is which.
After each, ask:

> From 1 (cold) to 5 (warm), how did that sound?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an F that's secretly warm**. You can't bring yourself to
sound cold. Say F the way you'd read a number off a form.

The second is **telling the listener what you're testing**. They'll listen for it. Just ask for the score.

The third, in real speech, is **the warm start and the flat finish**: *Good MORning* warm, and then the
request flat, because you're concentrating on the words. The request needs the warmth most.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Call-centre staff, receptionists and shopkeepers react to the voice within the first line. A cold first line
often means a short, unhelpful call, and neither side knows exactly why.

Under pressure, a complaint or a problem, people go flat and fast because they're tense. Take one breath
and start your first loud word high. It changes the whole call.

A native listener who hears a flat *thank you* doesn't think *they're a learner*. They think *they're
annoyed*, and they react to that.
