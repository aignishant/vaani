## One-line answer

Write *should* for advice (*You should lock the top bolt too*), *must* for a strong rule you are setting
(*You must turn off the gas*), *have to* for a rule that comes from outside (*You have to show ID at the
gate*), *mustn't* for *don't do it*, and *don't have to* for *you don't need to*. The last two look alike
and mean opposite things.

## The story

Your friend is staying in your flat while you're away. You leave her a note on the fridge:

> You should water the plants every evening. You have to show your name at the gate, the guard is new.
> You must turn off the gas cylinder at night. You mustn't open the balcony door, the monkeys come in!
> You don't have to cook, the tiffin man comes at one.

She reads it and knows exactly how serious each line is. The plants: good idea. The gate: somebody
else's rule. The gas: your rule, and it's important. The balcony: don't. The cooking: she's free.

Five lines, five levels, and she didn't need to phone you once.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **modal**: a small helper verb like *can* that sits before another verb and never changes, from
  [day 19](../../../day-19-can-and-cant/parts/02-writing/2.1-can-with-the-plain-verb.md).
- **advice**: telling somebody what you think is a good idea. They can choose.
- **obligation**: something a person has to do. There's no choice.
- **prohibition**: a rule that says *don't do this*.
- **imperative**: a sentence telling somebody to do something, with no subject written, *Turn off the
  gas*, from
  [day 32](../../../day-32-giving-instructions/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-imperative-on-paper.md).

These five forms go from gentlest to strongest, with the two *not* forms separate:

| Form | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *should* | advice: a good idea, you can choose | *You should water the plants.* |
| *have to* | an obligation from outside: a rule, a law, a boss | *You have to show your name at the gate.* |
| *must* | a strong obligation, often from the writer | *You must turn off the gas.* |
| *mustn't* | prohibition: don't do it | *You mustn't open the balcony door.* |
| *don't have to* | no obligation: you don't need to, but you can | *You don't have to cook.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Notes, instructions, house rules and advice to friends all need these. In phase 5 you'll write rules and
instructions for work, and later this phase you'll reply to a friend's problem with advice. The reader
needs to know which lines are advice and which are rules.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Modals: permission and obligation](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/modals-permission-obligation)
says:

> *Have to* shows us that the obligation comes from outside the speaker.
> *Must* expresses a strong obligation or necessity. It often shows us that the obligation comes from
> the speaker.
> *Don't have to* shows that there is no obligation. You can do something if you want to but it's not
> compulsory.

with "You have to have a licence to drive a car" and "You don't have to have a licence to cycle on the
roads".

[Suggestions and obligations](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/suggestions-obligations)
says "We use should and shouldn't to make suggestions and give advice" and "We use mustn't for
prohibitions – to say that it is necessary to not do something", with:

> You should send an email.
> You mustn't make any noise in the library.

**The shapes on paper.**

| Form | After it | Right | Wrong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *should*, *must* | base form, no *to* | *You should go.* · *You must go.* | *You should to go.* · *You must to go.* |
| *have to* | base form | *You have to go.* · *She has to go.* | *She have to go.* |
| *mustn't* | base form | *You mustn't go.* | *You mustn't to go.* |
| *don't have to* | base form | *You don't have to go.* · *She doesn't have to go.* | *She don't have to go.* |

Two things to see:

1. ***Should* and *must* never take *to*** and never take *-s*: *she should*, *she must*.
2. ***Have to* changes like an ordinary verb**: *has to* for he, she and it, *doesn't have to* in the
   negative.

**The pair that trips everybody.** *Mustn't* = *don't do it*. *Don't have to* = *you don't need to*.

| Sentence | What the reader can do |
| --- | --- |
| *You mustn't come early.* | come early? No. |
| *You don't have to come early.* | come early? Yes, if they want. |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the swapped pair**:

> You mustn't bring anything. (meant: you don't need to)

Your guest reads *don't bring anything* and arrives with empty hands, feeling told off.

The second is ***to* after *must* or *should***:

> You must to lock the door.

The third is ***must* for everything**. The note becomes a list of orders, and the reader can't tell the
important rule from the small advice.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

House notes, school notices, office signs and messages from landlords all use these five. Friendly notes
lean on *should* and *don't have to*. Official rules lean on *must*, *have to* and *mustn't*.

Under pressure, a quick note before you leave, people write *must* on every line. Save *must* for the
thing that really matters, like the gas, and the reader will notice it.

A careful reader who meets *mustn't* where you meant *don't have to* won't ask. They'll follow the rule
you wrote.
