## One-line answer

Write a real note for somebody who will use your home, your work or your things without you: at least
one line with each of *should*, *have to*, *must*, *mustn't* and *don't have to*. Then ask the reader to
sort every line into *advice*, *rule* or *free*. If they sort it the way you meant, your modals did
their job.

## The story

A new colleague will cover your desk at the shop while you're at a wedding. You write her
a note:

> Hi Sneha, thanks for covering!
> You have to open the shutter by nine, the owner checks.
> You must count the cash twice before you close. We lost money last month.
> You mustn't give credit to anyone, even regular customers.
> You should keep the fan on low, the papers fly everywhere.
> You don't have to do the stock list, I'll do it on Monday.

When you come back, she's done exactly what the note said. She also tells you she kept the fan on high
one afternoon because it was so hot, which was fine. It was only advice.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- ***should***, ***have to***, ***must***, ***mustn't***, ***don't have to***: advice, an outside rule,
  a strong rule, don't, and no need, from [part 2.1](2.1-advice-rule-no-choice-no-need.md).
- **sort**: to put things into groups.
- **free**: here, allowed to choose.

A note like this is for someone who can't ask you. Every line has to tell them two things: what to do,
and how serious it is. The modal carries the second.

**Step three: write the note.** A greeting, one line per item, and a thank-you. Give every *must* and
*mustn't* a short reason. The reason makes a strong rule sound fair rather than bossy.

**Step four: the form check.** Circle every modal. No *to* after *should*, *must* or *mustn't*. *Has to*
and *doesn't have to* for he, she and it. The base form after every one.

**Step five: the *must* check.** Count your *must*s. If more than two, is every one really that
important? Change the others to *should* or *have to*.

**Step six: the sorting test.** Give the note to the reader, or someone like them, and ask:

> Could you sort these lines into three groups: things you must do, things that are just advice, and
> things you're free to do or not?

Compare their groups with your table.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **all orders**: *must* on every line. The reader can't find the one rule
that matters.

The second is **the rule with no reason**: *You must count the cash twice.* Why? A short reason turns an
order into something the reader agrees with.

The third is **the missing *no need***. People list everything to do and forget to say what the reader
*doesn't* have to do. Then the reader does it anyway, and wastes their day.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Good notes for someone covering for you are short, sorted by importance, and warm at both ends. The
modals do the sorting.

Under pressure, leaving in a hurry, people write the rules and forget the thank-you. Your reader is doing
you a favour. One line at the top and one at the bottom.

A reader who knows which lines are only advice feels trusted. That's worth saying with *should*.
