## One-line answer

Plant four modal errors in a copy of your real note, including a *mustn't* where you mean *don't have
to*. Then ask a reader *what are you allowed to do?* The swap doesn't look wrong on the page. It turns
*you're free* into *you're forbidden*, and the reader obeys.

## The story

You invite your neighbours to a small dinner for your son's birthday, and you write on the building
group:

> Please come at 7! You mustn't bring anything, we have enough food.

You meant: *don't worry, no need to bring anything*. The Guptas read it as a rule. They come with empty
hands and feel awkward all evening, because everyone else brought sweets. Mrs Gupta says quietly at the
door: *We didn't know if we were allowed.*

*Mustn't* and *don't have to* both have a *not* and both look polite. One forbids, and one frees.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **plant**: to put an error into a text on purpose, from
  [day 46](../../../day-46-describing-a-trip/parts/02-writing/2.3-there-was-two-beds.md).
- ***mustn't*** and ***don't have to***: don't do it, and no need, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-advice-rule-no-choice-no-need.md).
- **allowed**: permitted; it's OK to do it.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Four common errors, and what each does to a reader:

| Error | Example | What the reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *mustn't* for *don't have to* | *You mustn't bring anything.* | the wrong rule: forbidden |
| *to* after *must* or *should* | *You must to lock the door.* | an error, meaning clear |
| *have* with he, she, it | *She have to leave by six.* | an error, meaning clear |
| *must* for simple advice | *You must try the sweets.* | an order where you meant a suggestion |

The first and the last change what the reader does. The middle two only look wrong.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every note, rule and piece of advice you write in English is read by someone who will act on it. The
*mustn't* swap is the one error in this family that makes a reader do the opposite of what you meant,
without ever asking.

**Step two: plant four errors** in a new copy, one of each kind in the table, with a hidden list. For the

**Step three: the allowed test.** Give the planted copy to a reader who hasn't seen the clean one. Ask:

> For each line: are you allowed to do it, must you do it, or is it up to you?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who guesses your meaning from the reason**. *You
mustn't bring anything, we have enough food* can be rescued by the reason. For the test, take the reason
off the planted line.

The second is **a reader who knows you** and knows you'd never forbid guests from bringing sweets. Use
someone who doesn't.

The third, in real writing, is ***mustn't* used to sound extra polite**. It sounds serious and formal, so
beginners reach for it. In English it's the strongest *no* on the list.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Invitations, house notes and messages to guests are where this swap does the most harm, because the
reader is trying hard to be polite and will follow your rule exactly.

Under pressure, writing quickly, *mustn't* and *don't have to* feel the same, because both have *not* in
them. Before you send, read every *mustn't* and ask: *do I really mean don't?*

A reader who obeys a wrong *mustn't* won't tell you. You'll only find out from the empty hands at the
door.
