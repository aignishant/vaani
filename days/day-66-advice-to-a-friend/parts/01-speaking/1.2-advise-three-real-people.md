## One-line answer

When three real people tell you about a real small problem this week, use the four steps: understand,
check, one idea, hand back. Afterwards, write down whether they took your idea, changed it, or found
their own. Any of the three means you helped. *Yes, maybe* and a change of subject means you didn't.

## The story

On Monday your colleague says he can't sleep because of the neighbour's dog. You say *That sounds
awful*, and ask *Is it every night, or just some nights?* He says *just weekends*. You say *Perhaps you
could talk to them on a Friday, before it starts?* He thinks, and says *Actually, I'll leave a note*.

It wasn't your idea he used. But your check, *just weekends?*, is what got him there.

On Wednesday your niece is worried about a school speech. On Friday your mother can't decide whether to
visit her sister. By Saturday you've used the four steps three times. Two people took an idea, one found
her own, and nobody said *yes, maybe* and went quiet.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the four steps**: understand, check, one idea, hand back, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-listen-then-one-idea.md).
- **outcome**: what happened in the end.
- **brush-off**: a short reply like *yes, maybe* that means *I'm not going to use that* or *please stop*.

This part is real. You can't plan when someone will tell you a problem, so you prepare the phrases, and
then wait. When it happens, you write down what you said and what happened.

## Why Vaani needs it

Advice given for real, to real people with real problems, is the practice. A recorded model can't tell
you whether your sympathy line sounded real. The person in front of you can.

**Step one: prepare.** Record yourself saying three sympathy lines, three check questions and all three
soft phrases, *Perhaps you could*, *Why don't you* and *If I were you, I'd*, each with a real-sounding
idea. Listen back for a warm fall on the sympathy and a fall-rise on the ideas.

**Step two: wait for three real problems** from three different people this week. Small ones are best.

**Step three: use the four steps.** Don't record other people without asking. Afterwards, write down

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **waiting for a big problem**. Nobody tells you one, and the part never
happens. A late bus, a noisy neighbour, a hard choice about dinner: small problems are the practice.

The second is **skipping the check** because you think you know the problem. The check is where most of
the good advice comes from.

The third is **advice nobody asked for**. Sometimes a friend only wants sympathy. If they say *I just
needed to tell someone*, stop at step 1. That's also a good outcome.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

People go back to the friends who listen before they advise. After a few conversations like these, you
may notice people telling you more.

Under pressure, in a hurry or when the problem is uncomfortable, it's tempting to jump to step 3. Even
one sympathy line first changes how the idea is heard.

A native listener who replies to your idea with *Actually, I could …* has taken it and made it theirs.
That's the best outcome of all.
