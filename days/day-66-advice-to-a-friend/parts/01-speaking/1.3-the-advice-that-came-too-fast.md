## One-line answer

Ask a friend to tell you a real small problem twice. Answer the first time deliberately with *You should
…* straight away, and the second time with the four steps. Then ask them *how much did you feel heard,
from 1 to 5?* Fast advice doesn't sound helpful. It sounds like you weren't listening, even when the idea
is good.

## The story

Your brother tells you he's thinking of leaving his job because his manager shouts at him. You've heard
enough, and you say:

> You should talk to HR. Straight away.

It's good advice. He says *yeah, yeah* and changes the subject. Later your mother tells you he's upset,
and that *nobody listens* to him.

The next evening you try again:

> That sounds horrible. Does he shout at everyone, or just you?
> Just me. Since the new project.
> Hm. So it started with the project? Perhaps you could ask to talk to him first, calmly?

He says: *Maybe. Or I could ask to move to the other team.* He was ready to think, now that he'd been
heard.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the four steps**: understand, check, one idea, hand back, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-listen-then-one-idea.md).
- **feel heard**: feel that the other person understood what you said and how you feel.
- **fast advice**: advice given before steps 1 and 2.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The same idea, said two ways:

| Version | What they hear | What they do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| fast advice: *You should talk to HR.* | *you want this conversation to end* | *yeah, yeah*, and move on |
| four steps: sympathy, check, *perhaps you could*, hand back | *you understood me* | think, and often find their own idea |

## Why Vaani needs it

Every advice conversation you'll have in English, with friends, colleagues or customers, is decided at
the very start, before your idea arrives. This part lets someone tell you honestly how fast
advice felt, which people rarely say in real life.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Giving advice](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b2/giving-advice): in the
video, the colleagues listen to the problem before any idea, and then offer them softly, "Perhaps you
could try talking to Yuna about it?"

**Step one: find a willing friend.** Tell them it's an exercise. Ask them to tell you a real small
problem, and to tell it to you twice, the same way both times.

**Step two: the first time, answer with F**, deliberately fast advice: *You should …* within their
first few sentences, with no sympathy and no check.

**Step three: the second time, answer with S**, the four steps, with the same idea if it still fits.

**Step four: the heard test.** After each, ask:

> From 1 to 5, how much did you feel heard?
> Would you use my idea?

Record both conversations if they agree.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when S scores 3 or lower. Then your steps 1 and
2 still aren't doing their job: usually the sympathy line was too quick, or the check wasn't a real
question. It is **GREEN** when S scores 4 or 5 and scores higher than F.

**Step six: the repair.** If S failed, ask your friend which step felt rushed. Try once more with a
different friend, spending longer on that step.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a friend who scores kindly**. Tell them you need the
honest number, and that a low score is useful.

The second is **changing the idea between F and S**. Then you can't tell whether the steps or the idea
made the difference. Keep the same idea.

The third, in real speech, is **the sympathy line said in a rush**: *Oh-no-that's-bad-you-should …* The
words of step 1 are there, but there's no pause after them. Let the sympathy line land before you move
on.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Friends rarely say *you didn't listen*. They say *yeah, yeah*, and next time they tell someone else.
That's why a friend who agrees to score you honestly is so useful.

Under pressure, when the problem is one you've solved yourself, fast advice feels generous. It isn't
heard as generous.

A native listener who feels heard often says *exactly* or *that's it* at your check. When you hear
that, your idea will be welcome.
