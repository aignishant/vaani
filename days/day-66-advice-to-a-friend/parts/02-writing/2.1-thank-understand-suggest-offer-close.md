## One-line answer

A reply to a friend's problem has five parts: thank them for telling you, show you understood the
problem in your own words, suggest one or two things softly, offer something you can actually do, and
close warmly with a question. The second part matters most, because it proves you read their message.

## The story

Your cousin moved to a new city for work. She writes to you:

> I don't know anyone here. Everyone at work goes home at six and I just sit in my room. I'm starting to
> think I made a mistake. Sorry for complaining!

You reply:

> Dear Priya,
>
> Thank you for telling me. Please don't say sorry, that's what cousins are for.
>
> It sounds really lonely, being in a new city where the evenings are empty and nobody knows you yet. It
> doesn't mean you made a mistake. You've only just arrived.
>
> Maybe you could join something in the evenings, like a class or a walking group? You could also ask one
> person from work for tea at lunch. It's easier than the evenings.
>
> I'll call you on Sunday, and if you want, I can visit next month.
>
> How is the job itself? Is that part going well?
>
> Love, Anu

She writes back the same day: *I'm crying, but in a good way. I'll ask Meera for tea tomorrow.*

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the five parts of a friendly email**: subject, greeting, the reason you're writing, the middle,
  and a sign-off, from
  [day 29](../../../day-29-weather-and-small-talk/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-five-parts-of-a-friendly-email.md).
- ***should***: the word for advice, from
  [day 65](../../../day-65-polite-intonation/parts/02-writing/2.1-advice-rule-no-choice-no-need.md).
  *Could* does the same job more gently: *Maybe you could …*.
- **restate**: to say what somebody told you again, in your own words.
- **offer**: saying you're ready to do something for somebody.

A reply to a problem has five parts, each usually one short paragraph:

| Part | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. thank | they trusted you with it | *Thank you for telling me.* |
| 2. understand | say the problem back in your words | *It sounds really lonely, being in a new city …* |
| 3. suggest | one or two soft ideas | *Maybe you could join a class?* |
| 4. offer | something you will really do | *I'll call you on Sunday.* |
| 5. close | a warm question, then the sign-off | *How is the job itself?* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Written advice has no voice to make it warm. The five parts do the work a warm voice does in speech. The
same shape comes back in phase 5, where you'll reply to a customer's complaint.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[A letter to a friend](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/letter-friend):
its tips include addressing your friend by name, thanking them for their letter, answering their
specific questions directly, and ending with an invitation to respond. Its model opens and closes:

> Dear Sabrina. Thanks for your letter and it was great to hear from you.
> Tell me about your favourite day in your next letter. Hope to hear from you soon.

[Giving advice](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b2/giving-advice) gives
gentle advice phrases, including "Perhaps you could try talking to Yuna about it?" and "If I were you,
I'd email and ask her to stop sending them."

**Part 2 uses their details, not yours.** Pick two things from their message and put them in your
words: *new city*, *evenings empty*. That's what proves you read it.

**Part 3: soft words, one or two ideas.** *Maybe you could*, *Perhaps you could*, *Why don't you*, *If I
were you, I'd*. On paper, *You should* sounds stronger than it does in speech. Keep it for when they asked
*what should I do?*

**Part 4 is a real promise.** Only offer what you'll do. *I'll call on Sunday* is better than *I'm always
here for you*, because it can be counted on.

**Each part is its own short paragraph** in an email, with an empty line between them.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **advice straight away**:

> Hi Priya, you should join a gym and talk to people at work. Love, Anu

It's good advice, and it reads like you didn't hear *I'm starting to think I made a mistake*.

The second is **part 2 in general words**: *I understand, it's hard.* Anyone could write that about
anything. Use their words.

The third is **the reply that ends on advice**. The friend is left with instructions. End with a warm
question.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

People keep replies like this, and reread them. The part they reread is often part 2, where somebody
showed they understood.

Under pressure, a friend's message arriving while you're busy, people send one line of advice. Better a
short reply with parts 1, 2 and 5 now, and the ideas later.

A friend who replies *exactly* or *yes, that's it* to your part 2 has been heard. That's worth more than
any idea in part 3.
