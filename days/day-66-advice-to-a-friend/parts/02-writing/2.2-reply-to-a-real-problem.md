## One-line answer

Write a real reply of about a hundred and twenty words to a real problem somebody has told you, in the
five parts, and send it if it's for them. Before you send, ask a stand-in reader: *if this were your
problem, would you feel understood?* If they say yes and can point to the sentence that did it, the reply
is ready.

## The story

An old school friend messages you: his small tea shop is losing customers since a big chain opened across
the road. He writes three long paragraphs, and at the end: *Maybe I should just close.*

You write a reply, then show it to your sister before sending: *Would you feel understood if you were
him?* She reads it and points at one sentence:

> It must be hard to watch people walk past your door to a place that's only just opened, after ten
> years of serving the same street.

*That one*, she says. *That's the sentence that says you read it.* You send it. Your friend replies:
*Thank you. Nobody else mentioned the ten years.*

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the five parts**: thank, understand, suggest, offer, close, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-thank-understand-suggest-offer-close.md).
- **restate**: to say what somebody told you again, in your own words, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-thank-understand-suggest-offer-close.md).
- **stand-in reader**: a person who reads your writing in place of the real reader, before you send it.

The real reader can't check your reply before it arrives. A stand-in can. Ask them to imagine the
problem is theirs, and read your reply as the friend would.

## Why Vaani needs it

A reply to a problem is the first piece of writing in this course where the reader's feelings are the
whole point. Phase 5's replies to complaints use exactly this shape, with a stranger instead of a friend.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[A letter to a friend](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/letter-friend):
address your friend by name, thank them, answer their specific questions, and end with an invitation to
reply. [Giving advice](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/speaking/b2/giving-advice):
"Perhaps you could try talking to Yuna about it?"

**Step one: choose a real problem** somebody has told you or written to you about: a friend, a relative,
a colleague. Read or remember their words carefully.

**Step two: pick two details** from what they said, their own facts, and write them down.

**Step three: plan the five parts.**

| Part | My sentence, in a few words |
| --- | --- |
| 1. thank | `TODO(me)` |
| 2. understand (with the two details) | `TODO(me)` |
| 3. suggest (one or two soft ideas) | `TODO(me)` |
| 4. offer (something I'll really do) | `TODO(me)` |
| 5. close (a warm question) | `TODO(me)` |

**Step four: write the reply**, about a hundred and twenty words, with a greeting, each part as a short
paragraph with an empty line between, and a sign-off. Count the words.

**Step five: the soft-word check.** Circle every *should* and *must*. Change each to *maybe you could*,
*why don't you* or *if I were you, I'd*, unless they asked *what should I do?*

**Step six: the stand-in test.** Give it to someone who doesn't know the friend, tell them the problem in
one sentence, and ask:

> If this were your problem, would you feel understood? Which sentence made you feel that?

**Step seven: fix and send.** If they said no, or pointed to nothing, rewrite part 2 with more of the
friend's own details. Then send it, if it's for them.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **three ideas and no understanding**: part 3 fills the reply, part 2 is
one line. The stand-in says *it's helpful, but a bit cold*.

The second is **the offer you can't keep**: *I'll help you every weekend.* Your friend will remember it.

The third is **the reply that's longer than the problem**. Two paragraphs from them, eight from you. Keep
it about the same length as their message, or shorter.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Most people who write about a problem don't want a solution first. They want to know somebody read it. A
good part 2 is often the whole reason they write back.

Under pressure, a sad message late at night, a short reply with parts 1, 2 and 5 is enough. The ideas
can come in the morning.

A friend who writes back *nobody else mentioned …* is telling you that your part 2 did what no one else's
did.
