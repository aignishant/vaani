## One-line answer

Rewrite your real reply on purpose so that most sentences are about you: *The same happened to me! When I
…*. Then count the sentences about you and about them, and ask a stand-in reader *whose problem is this
reply about?* A reply full of your own story doesn't read as sympathy. It reads as a change of subject.

## The story

Your friend writes that her father is in hospital and she's scared. You want her to know you understand,
so you write:

> Oh, I know exactly how you feel! When my father was in hospital in 2019, I was so scared too. I didn't
> sleep at all. I went every day, and the doctors never told us anything. My brother was useless. In
> the end my father was fine, thank God. Hang in there!

Seven sentences. One is about her. She replies *thanks*, and nothing more.

Your sister reads it later and says: *You told her the story of your father. She wanted to tell you about
hers.*

Sharing a similar story can help. But when it fills the reply, the friend's problem disappears from the
page.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the five parts**: thank, understand, suggest, offer, close, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-thank-understand-suggest-offer-close.md).
- **stand-in reader**: a person who reads your writing in place of the real reader, from
  [part 2.2](2.2-reply-to-a-real-problem.md).
- **I-sentence**: a sentence whose subject is you, *I*, *me*, *my*.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A reply to a problem is **for** the other person. You can see on the page who it's about by counting
subjects:

| Sentences about … | What the reader feels |
| --- | --- |
| mostly them | understood |
| half and half, with one short story of yours | understood, and less alone |
| mostly you | not heard, the subject has changed |

One sentence of your own story, *My father was in hospital once too, and I remember how scary it was*,
can help. After that, come back to them.

**Step four: the whose-problem test.** Give copy M to one stand-in reader and your real reply to another.
Tell each the friend's problem in one sentence, and ask:

> Whose problem is this reply mostly about?
> If you were my friend, how would you feel reading it?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when your real reply has more sentences about you
than about them, or its stand-in says it's mostly about you. It is **GREEN** when your real reply is mostly
about them, its stand-in says so, and copy M's stand-in says it's about you.

**Step five: the repair.** If your real reply failed, cut your own story to one sentence, and put the
space you save into part 2, with another of their details.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a copy M with no real story**. Use a true, similar
experience of yours. That's exactly what makes it tempting in real life.

The second is **counting *I* in the offer**. *I'll call you on Sunday* is about them, even though it starts
with *I*. Count what the sentence is about, not just the first word.

The third, in real writing, is **the story used to prove you understand**. *I know exactly how you feel,
because when I …* The intention is kind. Say the first half, and stop.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Most people do this sometimes, and most friends forgive it. But a friend in real trouble remembers who
wrote back about *them*.

Under pressure, a scary or sad message, your own memories come up fast, and it's natural to write them
down. Write them, then cut them to one line before you send.

A reader can't hear your kind voice in writing. They can only count what you wrote about.
