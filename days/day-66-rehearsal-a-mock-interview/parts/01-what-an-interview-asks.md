# What an interview asks that a conversation doesn't

`CF-14` · Day 66

## The one-line answer

An interview is not harder English than a conversation — it is the same English with somebody
sitting beside you making a judgement, and the one thing that genuinely changes is that you now
have to choose your answer before you start saying it.

## The model

Think about the difference between driving somewhere and taking a driving test. The road is the
same road. The car is the same car. Every single thing you do in the test you did last week on
the way to the shops. What has changed is that somebody is in the passenger seat with a clipboard,
they will ask for specific manoeuvres in an order you do not control, and they are watching how
you do them rather than whether you arrive.

That is the whole of it. Nobody in an interview is testing your English. They are testing whether
you can produce, on request, a piece of thinking with a shape. Your English is the car. You have
been driving it for sixty-five days.

Four things a test asks that ordinary driving does not.

**The manoeuvre on demand.** *Tell me about a time when something went wrong.* You do not get to
pick the topic and you cannot wait for it to come round naturally — you have to produce a whole
shaped thing, now, on a subject somebody else chose. Day 61 gave you the shape an answer has. What
it could not give you is the *choosing*, and the choosing is where the long pause actually goes.
Most people think they are searching for words in that pause. They are not. They are searching for
which of their own stories to tell.

**Commentary.** In a test you have to say what you are doing while you are doing it, because the
person beside you cannot see inside your head. Interview answers are the same. *I'll start with
what the problem was, then what I actually did.* You have signposting from Day 57. An interview is
the one place where signposting inside a single answer is normal rather than heavy-handed.

**No help from the other seat.** This is the one that catches people, and it is worth sitting with.
In an ordinary conversation the other person props you up constantly — they finish your sentence,
they ask the follow-up that rescues you, they nod you along. An interviewer deliberately does none
of that. They ask, and then they wait. The silence after their question belongs to you, and it is
longer than any silence you have practised in. It is also completely neutral. It is not
disapproval; it is the passenger seat.

**The manoeuvre you dread.** Everyone has one. The gap in your history, the thing that did not
work out, the question about money, the question about why you left. You know which one yours is,
and you have known since long before today. That one gets answered in this take, on purpose, and
not last.

**What an interview is not.** It is not faster, and it is not more formal than your English
actually is. Going formal is the standard overcorrection and it makes you worse in a way you can
hear: the contractions go, the chunks you own go with them, and every sentence gets longer and
stiffer while your speed drops. You end up sounding like a document being read aloud by somebody
who is not sure of the words. Day 51 gave you the register dial — an interview sits about one
notch up from a chat with a colleague you like. One notch. Not five.

**The answer has an end, and you say the end out loud.** This is the addition today makes to the
shape you already have. Point, one example, land it — and then hand it back. The single most
audible interview fault is answers that do not end. They fade. The speaker runs out of point,
keeps talking at a lower energy, adds a bit that restates the first bit, and eventually stops
because the sentence ran out rather than because the answer did. An interviewer hearing that has
no idea whether you have finished, so they wait, and the silence you get is one you built yourself.

> Does that answer what you were asking?

That line ends an answer cleanly and it puts the next move in their hands, which is where it
belongs.

**Before the take: build a stock, not a script.** Do not write answers. Rehearsed answers sound
rehearsed, and they sound worst of all when they are aimed at a question nobody asked. Instead,
before the recorder goes on, pick five or six real episodes from your own life and say each one
out loud in a sentence to fix it in your mouth. One thing that went badly. One thing you fixed.
One time you disagreed with somebody and it went alright. One thing you learned much later than
you should have. One thing you are quietly proud of. One thing you are still not good at.

That is your stock. Now any question is a matter of choosing which one to point at it, and the
same episode can serve three different questions depending on which end you tell it from. This is
what an interview actually tests, and it is the reason a stock beats a script every time.

**Today's phrases** — the lines that let an answer start, get its shape, and end where you decided
it ends. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Where I'd want to start is … | Choosing your own opening instead of taking theirs |
| There's a before and an after to that one. | Announcing an answer's shape before you start saying it |
| If you only take one thing from that — | Pointing at the part of a story worth their attention |
| That one didn't go well, and here's what I changed. | Answering the failure question without hiding in it |
| I don't have that yet — what I do have is … | The gap question, answered without apologising |
| Where I'd like to get to is … | Answering about the future without inventing a plan |
| Just so I'm answering the right question — do you mean …? | Checking before you spend a whole answer on the wrong one |
| Does that answer what you were asking? | Handing it back at the end of an answer, cleanly |

## Say it now

Pick one real episode from your stock and use the same one for all eight lines. Say each line
twice: once at the speed you would use with a colleague, once one notch up. Only one notch.

1. *Where I'd want to start is {{the beginning of your episode}}.*
2. *There's a before and an after to that one — {{the before}}.*
3. *If you only take one thing from that — {{the part that was actually hard}}.*
4. *That one didn't go well, and here's what I changed — {{what you changed}}.*
5. *I don't have that yet — what I do have is {{the nearest real thing you have}}.*
6. *Where I'd like to get to is {{the direction, not the plan}}.*
7. *Just so I'm answering the right question — do you mean {{one reading of it}}, or {{the other}}?*
8. *Does that answer what you were asking?*

Then run lines 1, 3 and 8 as one continuous answer, twice, and stop dead on line 8. Nothing after
it. That three-line run is the whole shape of an interview answer in miniature, and the stopping is
the half you have never rehearsed.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d66-free.m4a`.**

> Four questions, drawn one at a time without looking, answered cold. One answer each, and every
> answer ends on a handback. Write eight ordinary interview questions on slips beforehand — the
> obvious ones, including the one you dread — shuffle them face down, and draw as you go.

You will notice the pause between drawing a slip and starting to speak. Leave it in. That pause is
the choosing, it is the honest cost of not having a script, and it shrinks with the size of your
stock rather than with effort.

- **Solo form:** as written. Read each slip out loud in a flat voice before answering it, so the
  question is on the tape and you can hear the gap between the question and your first word.
- **With a partner:** they ask, then they say nothing at all — no nodding along, no *mm*, no
  rescue. Tell them that beforehand, because being unhelpful on purpose is harder than it sounds.
  They ask one follow-up per answer, chosen to be slightly awkward.

## The deliberate failure

Take one question and answer it in the most formal English you can produce. No contractions, no
chunks, longest available word every time. Try to sound like the version of you that you think is
being asked for.

**What breaking sounds like:** you get slower, and then you get worse. Listen for two specific
things on playback. First, every phrase you own — the ones that have been arriving on their own for
weeks — disappears, and what replaces them is longer and less certain. Second, your pauses move.
They stop landing between ideas and start landing inside sentences, in the middle of a clause,
because you are choosing vocabulary mid-flight instead of speaking.

That is the uncomfortable finding: the formal version is not a better version of you being
careful. It is a less fluent speaker. The chunks were not casualness — they were the evidence that
you can actually speak the language. Take them out and you take out the thing that was working.

Now answer the same question again at one notch up from a chat with a colleague. Play both. The
second one is more competent and it is not close.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are in a real interview, going well, and a question arrives that you have no episode for. So
you start talking in order to find out what you think, which in your first language you can just
about get away with. Here you cannot. Two sentences in you can hear it going nowhere, you speed up
to get to a point that has not formed, and the answer ends in a place that has no relation to the
question. Then they ask the next one and you are still recovering from the last.

**The repair:**

> I don't have that yet — what I do have is the thing next to it, if that's any use.

Naming that you have not done something is a complete answer and it takes one sentence. What loses
the room is the long stretch of manufacturing people do instead, because it is audibly
manufactured and everybody in the room can hear it.
