# The take, and the three things you count

`CF-14` · Day 66

## The one-line answer

This is the twelfth piece in your portfolio and the last rehearsal before the gate, and the only
things you judge tonight are countable: how many answers ended where you meant them to, how many
started with the point, and how many episodes you used twice.

## The model

Portfolio recording 12. A mock interview — eight questions, somebody else asking, no notes, and
the whole thing on one file from the first question to the last answer.

This is the test, not the lesson beside the test. Everything you need is already in the car. Set
it up like this.

**Eight questions, and you do not see them first.** Whoever is asking picks them, or they come off
your own slips face down. Include the one you dread; if you are running it alone, put two you
dread in there. The point of eight is that you cannot carry eight scripts in your head, so
somewhere around the fifth one you stop performing and start speaking. That is the part of the
tape that is actually worth having.

**They ask, then they wait.** No help from the passenger seat. If you are doing this solo, read the
slip aloud and then leave a real gap before you begin — the same gap a person would give you.

**No notes, and the stock stays in your head.** You built five or six episodes in part 01 and said
them out loud once. That is the whole preparation. Do not re-read them between questions.

**Keep the first take.** Same rule as every rehearsal since Day 6, and the same reason: a second
take is a performance of the first one, and you already know what a performance sounds like from
the deliberate failure in part 01.

**Stalls stay in.** Somewhere in eight questions you will get one you have nothing for. Do not stop
the recorder. You have three cheap moves for exactly this, and they all do the same job — they keep
the car moving while you find the manoeuvre.

> Let me pick the right example.
> Sorry — I've gone off the question.
> And that's the example.

*Let me pick the right example* is thinking time said out loud, and it is the most useful line in
this document. It is true, it is short, and it converts a silence that sounds like a freeze into a
silence that sounds like judgement. Nobody has ever minded waiting for somebody to pick a good
example.

*Sorry — I've gone off the question* is for the middle of an answer that has wandered. You will
hear it happen live: you started on their question, took a turning, and are now three sentences
into a different subject. Saying so and turning back is a strong move, not a weak one. Following
the drift in silence and hoping they did not notice is the weak one, and they always notice,
because they are holding the question.

*And that's the example* is a landing. It is for the answer that has said its point and has nothing
more, but which your mouth wants to keep decorating. Say it, and stop. It works because it is true
— the example was the answer — and because it puts a full stop where you would otherwise have put
another three sentences.

**Then the playback, once, with a job.** The job tonight is three counts and nothing else. Not
whether you sounded clever. Not whether you would hire yourself.

**Count one — clean endings.** Out of eight answers, how many ended on a handback or a landing,
followed by silence? How many faded? A fade is an answer whose last sentence is quieter, longer
and less relevant than the one before it. You will hear the difference immediately once you are
listening for it.

**Count two — point-first openings.** Out of eight, how many had the point inside the first
sentence? An opening that spends its first sentence on *so, I suppose, it depends really, I mean
there's a few ways of looking at that* is an opening that was still choosing. Count them honestly.

**Count three — repeats.** How many separate episodes did you actually use across eight answers?
If the number is three, your stock is too small and that is tonight's finding, not a shameful one.
If it is seven, you have plenty and the work is elsewhere.

Three numbers. They go into `docs/RECORDINGS.md` tonight beside the date, and they are the row —
not an adjective about how it felt.

**Today's phrases** — appended tonight with the ones from part 01.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Let me pick the right example. | Taking thinking time out loud instead of freezing into it |
| Sorry — I've gone off the question. | Catching your own drift mid-answer and turning back |
| And that's the example. | Landing an answer before your mouth starts decorating it |
| Shall I keep going, or is that enough? | Checking whether they want the rest, instead of guessing |

## Say it now

Before the recorder goes on, say each of these three times, flat and unhurried, so they arrive
without a run-up when an answer goes wrong live.

1. *Let me pick the right example. {{the episode you land on}}.*
2. *Sorry — I've gone off the question. {{the question, said back}}.*
3. *And that's the example.*
4. *Shall I keep going, or is that enough?*

Then say lines 1 and 3 together as a pair, twice — a stall at the front, a landing at the back, and
one sentence of actual content in between. That pair is the smallest complete answer you can make,
and being able to produce it under any question at all is what stops question five from being a
problem. These four lines are the only thing you rehearse today. Everything else on this recording
is live.

> **A mock interview.** Eight questions you have not seen, asked one at a time, answered cold. Point
> first, one example, land it, hand it back. The question you dread is in there and it is not last.

Rules:

- No notes, and no plan beyond the stock of episodes you said out loud in part 01.
- **Keep the first take.** You may do a second afterwards; the first one is not deleted and it is
  the one that goes in the portfolio row.
- Stalls, wanderings and the one bad answer all stay on the file. Do not stop the recorder to start
  a question again.

- **Solo form:** eight slips face down. Draw, read the question aloud in a flat voice, leave a real
  gap, then answer. Harder than the partner version, not lesser — nobody is going to let you off a
  question, because nobody is there to.
- **With a partner:** they pick the eight, they ask, and they wait in silence. Two instructions for
  them: one follow-up per answer, chosen to be slightly awkward, and absolutely no encouragement
  while you are speaking. The silence is the exercise.

## The deliberate failure

After the take is finished, record one more answer on the end. Take a question you have no episode
for at all, and answer it anyway by inventing as you go — commit to it, do not stop, and do not
admit at any point that you are making it up.

**What breaking sounds like:** the tense goes soft. Listen for it. Real episodes come out in the
plain past — *I did, we tried, it didn't work.* An invented one drifts into what you would
generally tend to do, what one usually does, what you would probably say in that situation. You
will hear your own answer slide from a thing that happened into a description of a kind of person,
and you will hear the moment it happens.

Then answer the same question with *I don't have that yet — what I do have is …* and one real
episode pointed sideways at it. Play both. The short honest one is stronger, and the reason is
audible rather than moral: it is in the past tense and it has a specific thing in it.

## When it breaks in the wild

Question six of a real interview and you have used your best episode already, on question two.
Everything left in your head feels thin. So you tell the good one again with different framing, and
halfway through you realise they were in the room for the first telling and they can hear it is the
same story.

**The repair:**

> There's a before and an after to that one — and the before is the example I already used, from
> the other end.

Reusing an episode deliberately, and saying so, is completely normal — an interview is short and a
life is not made of eight separate stories. What damages you is reusing it while pretending it is
new, because that is the one thing on the tape they will remember.
