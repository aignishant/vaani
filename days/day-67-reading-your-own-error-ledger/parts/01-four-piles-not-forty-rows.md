# Four piles, not forty rows

`CF-15` · Day 67

## The one-line answer

Your error ledger is not a list of every mistake you have made — it is a small number of mistakes
written down a lot of times, and today you sort it into four piles and say their names out loud.

## The model

Tip the basket out on the floor before you sort anything.

That is the first move and it is the one people skip. Open `docs/ERRORS.md` and read every row out
loud, top to bottom, in one pass. Do not fix anything. Do not stop on a row and start practising
it. Do not skip the embarrassing ones — those are usually the ones with the most rows behind them.
Just read the whole pile out, once, so it is all in front of you and out of your mouth rather than
sitting quietly in a file you have been adding to without ever re-reading.

You will notice something during that pass, and it is the entire reason this day exists. The
ledger looks long. It is not long. It is short and repetitive, and you have been experiencing it
as long only because you were adding one row at a time on separate evenings.

**Now sort by kind, not by item.** You do not sort washing by garment. You sort it by what happens
to it next — the things that get the same treatment go in the same pile. Your rows work the same
way. Two rows about two different words that both went wrong in the same way belong together,
because tomorrow they get the same drill.

The `Type` column is your first cut, and it is deliberately coarse: `sound`, `stress`, `grammar`,
`word`, `freeze`, `filler`, `speed`. Go through and count the rows in each type. Say the counts out
loud. It is plain honest arithmetic and it will already have told you something — usually that two
types have almost everything in them and three types have one row each.

But type alone is too coarse to act on. *Sound* is not a pile; it is a shelf with several piles on
it. Within each type, group the rows by what actually goes wrong: the same sound, the same tense,
the same word arriving in place of another, the same kind of moment where you stop dead. That
second cut is where the real piles appear, and you make it by ear — read two rows out loud one
after the other and ask whether they are the same fault wearing different clothes.

**What makes a pile a pile.** Three tests, and a pile has to pass all three.

**It repeats.** Three or more rows of the same shape. Two is a coincidence you are welcome to keep
an eye on; it is not yet a pattern.

**It spreads across days.** The rows come from at least two different dates. Five rows of the same
fault all logged on one evening is not a pattern — that is one bad session, and one bad session
usually means you were tired, or the topic was new, or you had just learned something and were
overusing it. Check the `Day` column before you promote anything.

**It costs somebody something.** Did it ever make a listener pause, ask again, or answer the wrong
question? A fault that has actually cost you a repeat outranks a bigger pile that nobody ever
noticed. This test breaks ties, and it should break them decisively — the count is evidence, not
the verdict.

**A one-off goes back in the drawer.** Some rows will not join anything. A word you got wrong once
and have never got wrong since. A freeze on a day when you were talking about something you had
never talked about before. Those rows stay in the ledger for ever, because the ledger is
append-only and nothing is ever deleted from it, but they do not become work. Chasing a one-off is
the most reliable way to spend the last days of this course on nothing.

**Now pick four.** Biggest first, then apply the cost test, then stop at four. Four is not a
tradition — it is the number of things you can actually hold in your head while speaking, and
tomorrow you build one drill for each of them. Five drills is a filing system. Four is a session.

**Then name them, in your own words, out loud.** This is the output of the day and it is the part
that has to be spoken rather than written.

A name is a sentence you could say to a person who is about to listen to you. It has to be short
enough for one breath and specific enough to act on. *My grammar is bad* is not a name. *My
pronunciation needs work* is not a name. Those are shrugs with words in them. A name says what you
do, and when.

The shape is: **what happens, and when it shows up.**

> The one I keep making is {{the thing that happens}} — I hear it most when I'm {{the condition}}.

The *when* half is not decoration. Almost every pile in almost every ledger has a condition
attached: when you speed up, when you are on the phone, when the sentence is long, when you are
telling a story rather than answering a question, when you are the third person to speak. Go back
through the rows in the pile and look at what they have in common besides the fault. Whatever that
is, it goes in the name — because tomorrow's drill has to reproduce it, and a drill that does not
reproduce the condition will not touch the fault.

**If your ledger is thin.** Perfectly possible, and it is not a failure of the ledger. If you have
fewer than four piles, you have fewer than four, and you take what is genuinely there. Then, to
fill the rest: open your last three recordings, put the ledger beside you, and listen with a task
— play, stop the moment you hear something, write the row. Actual sentences you said, in your own
words, exactly as on Day 1. Add rows the ordinary way, dated today.

What you do not do, under any circumstances, is guess what your errors probably are. A pile you
invented is worse than no pile at all, because tomorrow you will spend a real drill on it and get
a real feeling of progress out of something that was never happening.

**Today's phrases** — the language for naming your own faults out loud, to yourself and to
somebody who is about to listen to you. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| The one I keep making is … | Naming your repeat offender in one breath |
| I hear it most when I'm … | Attaching the condition an error shows up under |
| That's the same one again. | Catching a repeat while you're listening back |
| It only happened the once. | Ruling a row out of the four, out loud |
| It costs me when … | Saying why a smaller pile outranks a bigger one |
| The four I'm working on are … | Opening the list for somebody who asked |
| If you hear me do that, stop me. | Handing one listener one thing to listen for |
| I know exactly what I did there. | Catching yourself in the act without stopping to fix it |

## Say it now

Use your own four piles. If you have fewer than four, run the lines you have and leave the rest
until the ledger fills. Say each line twice — once slowly, once at ordinary speaking speed.

1. *The one I keep making is {{pile one, in your own words}}.*
2. *I hear it most when I'm {{the condition on pile one}}.*
3. *That's the same one again — {{pile two}}.*
4. *It only happened the once, so {{the row you are leaving out}} isn't on the list.*
5. *It costs me when {{the moment pile three actually cost you something}}.*
6. *The four I'm working on are {{one}}, {{two}}, {{three}} and {{four}}.*
7. *If you hear me do that, stop me — {{the one you most want catching}}.*
8. *I know exactly what I did there.*

Then run lines 1, 2 and 6 as one continuous run, twice, with no gaps. Say them at the speed you
would use telling somebody something ordinary, not the speed of reading a list. These four names
have to be sayable to a person without a preamble, and a name you have to slow down for is a name
that is still too long.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d67-free.m4a`.**

> Brief somebody who is going to listen to you speak tomorrow. Take each of your four in turn: what
> it is, when it shows up, and what it has actually cost you — with a real example from the ledger,
> the sentence you said and the sentence you meant. Then say which one you would fix first, and
> why that one.

- **Solo form:** as written, said to the recorder as though to a person. Keep the ledger open in
  front of you but do not read the rows out — say each pile from memory, and only glance down for
  the example sentences.
- **With a partner:** brief them for real, then hand them the job. They listen to you talk about
  anything for a while afterwards and stop you the moment they hear one of the four. Ask which one
  they heard most. Their answer and your ranking will not match, and the mismatch goes in the
  ledger tonight.

## The deliberate failure

Take one of your four and name it the way you would have named it before today — vaguely. *My
grammar is bad.* *My pronunciation isn't good.* *I make a lot of mistakes when I speak fast.* Say
it out loud, and then, without stopping, try to say what you would do about it tomorrow.

**What breaking sounds like:** you cannot get to a second sentence. Listen for the stall — the vague
name comes out fluently, because you have said versions of it for years, and then nothing follows
it. There is no action on the other side of it, so your voice goes up at the end and waits, or you
restate the same vague thing in different vague words.

Then say the specific version — what happens, and when — and immediately say what you would do
tomorrow. That one produces a next sentence on its own. The difference between the two takes is not
about honesty or attitude. It is that one of them names something you can hear and the other names
a mood.

This is also why the vague version survived so long. It is comfortable, it is unfalsifiable, and it
lets you feel you are working on your English without ever putting a fault where you could trip
over it.

## When it breaks in the wild

Somebody who speaks good English offers to correct you and asks what they should listen for. You
say *everything, honestly — just correct me whenever*. So they do it twice, both times on things
that were not really the problem, and then they stop, because correcting somebody constantly is
unpleasant and they can tell you are not enjoying it either. You have used up a rare and generous
offer and got nothing out of it.

**The repair:**

> Just the one thing, honestly — the first one on my list. Let the rest go.

One thing is a job somebody can actually do. Everything is not a job, it is a mood, and it puts the
work of choosing onto a person who has no way of knowing which of your faults matters to you.
