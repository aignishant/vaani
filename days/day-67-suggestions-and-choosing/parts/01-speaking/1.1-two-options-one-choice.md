## One-line answer

To choose something with another person, put two options on the table (*We could … or we could …*),
ask what they'd prefer, say your own preference with a reason (*I'd rather …, because …*), and close
it (*OK, let's go with …*). A choice needs somebody to say which one they want.

## The story

Saturday afternoon. You and your friend want to do something, and neither of you says what.

> What do you want to do?
> I don't know. What do you want to do?
> Anything's fine.
> Me too.

Twenty turns later, you're still on the sofa and the afternoon is gone.

The next Saturday you start differently:

> We could go to the lake, or we could see the new film. Which would you prefer?
> Hm. The lake's far.
> True. I'd rather go to the film, actually, because it's going to rain later.
> OK, let's go with the film.

Four turns. Two options, one preference, one reason, one close.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **suggest**: to offer an idea for the other person to agree with or not, from
  [day 26](../../../day-26-making-plans/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-ways-to-suggest.md).
- **option**: one of the things you could choose.
- **prefer**: to like one option more than another.
- **preference**: the option you prefer.
- **close**: here, the line that makes the decision final.

On day 26 you learned *let's*, *shall we* and *how about* for one suggestion. Today there are two
options, and somebody has to choose. Choosing has four moves:

| Move | Phrase | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. options | *We could … or we could …* | *We could go to the lake, or we could see a film.* |
| 2. ask | *Which would you prefer?* · *What do you think?* | *Which would you prefer?* |
| 3. prefer | *I'd rather … because …* · *I'd prefer …* | *I'd rather see the film, because it might rain.* |
| 4. close | *OK, let's go with …* · *Let's do that, then.* | *OK, let's go with the film.* |

Moves 3 and 4 are the ones people skip. Without them there's no decision.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every plan you make with other people needs a choice at some point: where to eat, which day, which
route. The phase 4 gate is an opinion with reasons, and *I'd rather …, because …* is a small opinion with
a reason. In phase 5 you'll decide things in meetings the same way.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Suggestions and obligations](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/suggestions-obligations)
says: "We also use could to make positive suggestions", with:

> We could meet at the weekend.

[rather](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/rather), which carries audio, gives the sense "Used to specify a
choice or preference", and notes that "When expressing preference, the expression is usually *would
rather* or *had rather*, usually contracted to *'d rather*". Its examples include:

> Tomorrow's impossible. We'd rather you came next weekend.

**Move 1: give exactly two options.** One is a suggestion, and three is a menu. Two is a choice.

**Move 3: *I'd rather* + base form, then *because*.** Say it even if you only mildly prefer one:

> I'd rather stay in, because I'm tired.
> I'd rather not go too far, because the bus is slow.

Push the option word: *I'd rather see the FILM.*

**Move 4: somebody says the close.** If the other person agreed, you can close: *OK, let's go with the
film.* The close is what turns talk into a plan.

**If they prefer the other one**, and you don't mind much: *OK, fine, the lake then.* That's a close too.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the polite loop**:

> I don't mind. — Me neither. — Whatever you want. — No, you choose.

Both people are being kind, and nothing gets decided.

The second is **the option with no reason**: *I'd rather the film.* It's a choice, but the other person
can't weigh it. Add *because*.

The third is **no close**. You both seem to agree, and then nobody books anything, because nobody said
*let's go with …*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers usually make small decisions in four to six turns. The person who offers two options and
says *I'd rather* is often thanked, because they saved everybody the loop.

Under pressure, with someone senior or someone you want to please, *I don't mind* feels safest. A soft
preference is still kind: *I'd slightly rather the film, but I'm happy either way.*

In some families and cultures, saying what you want first can feel rude. In English, a small, clear
preference with a reason usually sounds helpful, not selfish.
