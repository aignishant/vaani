## One-line answer

Make three real small decisions with real people this week using the four moves: two options, ask,
prefer with a reason, close. Afterwards, count the turns it took and write down what was decided. A
decision reached, in a few turns, with both of you happy, is the goal.

## The story

Your family needs to decide where to eat for your mother's birthday. Last year it took three days of
messages.

This time, at breakfast, you say:

> We could go to the South Indian place near the temple, or we could order in and eat at home. Which
> would Amma prefer?

Your father says *she likes going out*. You say:

> Then I'd rather the South Indian place, because she loves their dosa, and she won't have to cook.

Your sister says *fine, let's go with that*. It takes six turns. You book a table the same morning.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the four moves**: options, ask, prefer, close, from [part 1.1](1.1-two-options-one-choice.md).
- **turn**: one person's part of a conversation before the other person speaks, from
  [day 8](../../../day-08-greetings-and-goodbyes/parts/01-speaking/1.1-hello-and-how-are-you.md).
- **decided**: chosen and agreed, so somebody can act on it.

A decision is only finished when somebody could act on it: book the table, buy the ticket, tell the
others. That's how you'll know whether each of your three decisions really happened.

## Why Vaani needs it

Deciding things with people in English is daily life: at home, with friends, at work. This part makes
you do it three times for real, and count, so you can hear whether your moves 3 and 4 are there.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **hiding your preference to be polite**. You offer two options, they say
*I don't mind*, and you say *me neither*. Say your preference. You can still add *but I'm happy either
way*.

The second is **options you don't really like**. If you'd hate option B, don't offer it.

The third is **the close that nobody acts on**: *Yes, let's go with that*, and nobody books. The close
can include the action: *Let's go with the South Indian place. I'll call them now.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Most small decisions are made by whoever says the first clear preference. That person is usually seen
as helpful, not bossy, as long as they asked first.

Under pressure, in a group with lots of opinions, offer two options, not five, and ask the person the
decision matters most to. The group will usually follow.

A native listener hears *I'd rather …, because …* as a normal, polite way to choose. It isn't pushy.
