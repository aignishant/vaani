## One-line answer

With a willing friend, make the same small decision twice. The first time, you both say *I don't mind*
on purpose until one of you gives up. The second time, use the four moves. Count the turns and ask them
how it felt. *Anything is fine* doesn't sound easy-going. It hands the whole job of deciding to the other
person.

## The story

Your colleague asks you where to go for the team lunch. You don't want to seem difficult, so you say:

> Anything's fine with me.

She asks two other people. They say the same. In the end she chooses, and half the team doesn't like
the place. Later she says, a little tired: *Next time, somebody else decides.*

Everyone was being polite, and she did all the work, and took all the blame.

If you'd said *We could try the new canteen or the dhaba by the gate. I'd rather the dhaba, because it's
quicker*, she'd have had something to choose from.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the four moves**: options, ask, prefer, close, from [part 1.1](1.1-two-options-one-choice.md).
- **the polite loop**: two people saying *I don't mind* back and forth until nothing is decided, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-two-options-one-choice.md).
- **burden**: a heavy job that somebody has to carry.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

*I don't mind* feels generous. What the other person actually hears depends on what comes after it:

| What you say | What they hear |
| --- | --- |
| *I don't mind.* (and nothing else) | *you decide, and it's your fault if it's wrong* |
| *I don't mind, but I'd slightly rather the dhaba.* | *here's some help* |
| *We could do A or B. I'd rather A, because …* | *let's decide together* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Decisions in meetings and families in phase 5 go the same way. Hearing the loop on purpose, once, and
hearing how long it takes, is how you notice yourself starting it.

"We also use could to make positive suggestions".
[rather](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/rather): "Used to specify a choice or preference".

**Step one: find a willing friend** and a real small decision you both need to make: what to eat, when
to meet, what to watch.

**Step two: version L, the deliberate loop.** Record it with their permission. Both of you say *I don't
mind*, *you choose*, *anything's fine* until one of you makes a choice, or until you both give up.

**Step three: version M, the four moves.** Same decision, same friend. Record it.

**Step four: count and ask.** Count the turns in each from the recording, and ask your friend:

> In which one did you feel you had to do the work?
> In which one did we decide something we both like?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a loop that breaks too fast**, because one of you gets
bored and chooses. That's fine. Count the turns anyway. Even three turns of *I don't mind* is a lot.

The second is **an M where you only suggest**: two options, a question, and then *I don't mind*. That's
the loop with a nicer start. Move 3 is required.

The third, in real speech, is ***I don't mind* when you do mind.** You say it, then you're unhappy with
the choice. Your friend can't know. A soft preference protects both of you.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

The person who always says *I don't mind* is often, over time, the person who is never asked. The person
who says *I'd rather …, but I'm happy either way* is asked, and listened to.

Under pressure, in a new job or with in-laws, the loop feels safest. A tiny preference, *I'd slightly
rather …*, is safe too, and helps.

A native listener hears one *I don't mind* as easy-going. They hear a second one as unhelpful.
