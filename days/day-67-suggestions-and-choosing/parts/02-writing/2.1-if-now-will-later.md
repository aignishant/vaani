## One-line answer

For a possible future, write *if* + present simple for the condition and *will* + base form for the
result: *If it rains, we'll stay in.* The *if* half never takes *will*. When the *if* half comes first,
put a comma after it.

## The story

Your family is planning a picnic by the river on Sunday. Your uncle writes on the family group:

> If it will rain we go to Auntie's house.

Half the family reads it twice. Your cousin replies: *So if it rains, we're going to Auntie's? Or are
we going anyway?*

You rewrite it for everyone:

> If it rains, we'll go to Auntie's house instead. If it's sunny, we'll meet at the bridge at ten.

Two sentences, and nobody asks again. The condition was in the *if* half, the result was in the *will*
half, and the comma showed where one ended.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- ***will***: the future form for decisions, promises and predictions, from
  [day 61](../../../day-61-giving-opinion/parts/02-writing/2.1-decided-before-decided-now.md).
- **clause**: a part of a sentence with its own subject and verb, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-four-joining-words.md).
- **condition**: the thing that has to happen first: *if it rains*.
- **result**: what will happen because of it: *we'll stay in*.
- **first conditional**: an *if* sentence about a real, possible future.

A first-conditional sentence has two clauses:

| Clause | Verb | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the *if* clause: the condition | present simple | *If it rains,* |
| the main clause: the result | *will* + base form | *we'll stay in.* |

The *if* clause talks about the future, but its verb is in the present. That's the part that feels
strange, and it's the part that goes wrong.

## Why Vaani needs it

Plans in real life come with *ifs*: if the train is late, if it rains, if you get the job. The next
writing day is a plan written step by step, and the good ones have a backup line in the first
conditional. In phase 5 you'll write it in emails at work.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Conditionals: zero, first and second](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/conditionals-zero-first-second).
It gives the first conditional as "if + present simple >> will + infinitive", used for "future
situations we believe are real or possible", with:

> If it doesn't rain tomorrow, we'll go to the beach.

It says "The order of the clauses does not change the meaning", and it does not use *will* in the *if*
clause.

**The two orders.**

```text
   If it rains,  we'll stay in.        ← if clause first: comma after it
   We'll stay in  if it rains.         ← if clause second: no comma
```

This is the same comma rule you used for time clauses on
[day 56](../../../day-56-explaining-why/parts/02-writing/2.1-four-words-that-put-events-in-order.md):
when the joining clause comes first, a comma follows it.

**The negative.** *Doesn't* or *don't* in the *if* clause, *won't* in the main clause:

> If the bus doesn't come, we'll take an auto.
> If you're late, I won't wait.

**He, she, it in the *if* clause take the *-s*,** because it's the present simple: *If she calls*, *If
it rains*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***will* in the *if* clause**:

> If it will rain, we'll stay in.

It looks wrong to a careful reader. None of the reference page's examples put *will* after *if*.

The second is **no *will* in the main clause**:

> If it rains, we stay in.

Readers understand it, but it reads like a rule that always happens, not a plan for Sunday.

The third is **the missing comma**:

> If it rains we'll go to Auntie's house instead.

In a long sentence, the reader loses where the condition ends.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Family messages, school notices and work emails use the first conditional constantly: *If you can't
come, please let me know. If the office is closed, we'll meet online.*

Under pressure, typing fast, people put *will* in both halves, because the whole sentence is about the
future. Check the *if* half first. It should look like the present.

A formal reader marks *if it will* every time. A friend just reads more slowly.
