## One-line answer

Write a real message about a plan for a group of real people, with the main plan and at least three
*if* lines for what could change: weather, lateness, somebody who can't come. Then ask a reader *what
will you do if …?* for each case. If they answer without asking you anything, every *if* was clear.

## The story

Your neighbours are organising a cleaning day for the building's garden. You offer to write the message
for the group:

> Hello everyone! Garden cleaning is on Sunday at 8 am. Please bring gloves if you have them.
> If it rains, we'll move it to next Sunday.
> If you can't come, please tell me by Friday, and I'll find someone else for your job.
> If we finish early, Mrs Rao will make tea for everyone!

On Saturday night it rains. Nobody calls you. On Sunday morning nobody comes, and on the next Sunday
twelve people come, with gloves.

Every *if* had its answer already in the message. That's why nobody needed to ask.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **first conditional**, **condition**, **result**: *if* + present, *will* + base form, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-if-now-will-later.md).
- **backup plan**: what you'll do if the main plan doesn't work.
- **case**: one possible situation, like *if it rains*.

A plan for a group always has a main line and some backup lines. The main line says what's happening.
The *if* lines answer the questions people would otherwise phone you about.

## Why Vaani needs it

The next writing day is a plan written step by step, and a plan that says what happens if something goes
wrong is a plan people trust. The same *if* lines turn up in phase 5 in every email that arranges
something.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Conditionals: zero, first and second](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/conditionals-zero-first-second):
"if + present simple >> will + infinitive" for "future situations we believe are real or possible", and
"The order of the clauses does not change the meaning".

**Step one: choose a real plan** for a real group: a family visit, a meeting, a trip, an event at work,
a celebration.

**Step three: write the message.** A greeting, the main plan with the day and time, then one line for

first, one with it second.

**Step four: the *if* check.** Underline the verb in every *if* clause. Present simple? No *will*? Then
circle the verb in every main clause. *Will* or *'ll*, and the base form?

**Step five: the comma check.** Every *if* clause that comes first has a comma after it.

**Step six: the what-if test.** Give the message to a reader. For each case, ask:

> What will you do if …?

If they can answer from the message alone, the line works. If they ask you, rewrite it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the main plan with no *if*s**. The plan is clear, and then it rains,
and your phone rings all evening.

The second is **the *if* with no result**: *If it rains, let's see.* The reader still doesn't know what
to do.

The third is **too many *if*s**: eight backup lines for a family lunch. Choose the three or four cases
people will really ask about.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Organisers who write the *if* lines in advance get far fewer questions. People trust a plan that has
thought about what could go wrong.

Under pressure, a message sent in a hurry the night before, the *if* lines are the first thing left out.
One *if* line for the most likely problem is better than none.

A reader who answers *what will you do if …?* straight from your message has everything they need.
