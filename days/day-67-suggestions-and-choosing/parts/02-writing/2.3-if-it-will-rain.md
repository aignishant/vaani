## One-line answer

Plant four errors in a copy of your real plan message, including *when* in place of *if* on something
that might not happen. Ask a reader *which of these things will definitely happen?* Most *if* errors only
look wrong. *When* in place of *if* tells the reader a possibility is certain.

## The story

Your friend is applying for a job abroad. She writes to her family group:

> When I get the job, I'll move in March. If I will move, you will visit me?

Her aunt starts telling everyone she's leaving in March. Her mother cries. Her father asks about the
flight.

She hasn't had the interview yet.

*When I get the job* says: *I'll get it, the only question is the date.* She meant *if*. And *If I will
move, you will visit me?* is two errors in one line. It should be *If I move, will you visit me?*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **plant**: to put an error into a text on purpose, from
  [day 46](../../../day-46-describing-a-trip/parts/02-writing/2.3-there-was-two-beds.md).
- **first conditional**, **condition**: from [part 2.1](2.1-if-now-will-later.md).
- **certain**: sure to happen.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

*If* and *when* look like they do the same job. On the page they make different promises:

| Word | What it says about the condition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *if* | it might happen, or might not | *If I get the job, I'll move.* |
| *when* | it will happen; only the time is open | *When I finish work, I'll call you.* |

The four common errors:

| Error | Example | What the reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *when* for something uncertain | *When I get the job, I'll move.* | the wrong fact: it's certain |
| *will* in the *if* clause | *If it will rain, we'll stay in.* | an error, meaning clear |
| no *will* in the result | *If it rains, we stay in.* | reads like a general rule, not a plan |
| no comma after a first *if* clause | *If it rains we'll stay in.* | a harder read |

## Why Vaani needs it

Plans, job applications and messages about the future all mix *if* and *when*. A *when* that should be
an *if* makes a reader plan around something that may never happen. This part shows you once what a
reader believes.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **planting *when* on something certain**. *When the
meeting starts* is fine as *when*. The planted one must be something that might not happen.

The second is **a reader who knows your plans** and knows the job isn't certain. Use someone who doesn't.

The third, in real writing, is ***when* used out of hope**: *When I pass the exam …* Writing *if* can feel
unlucky. On the page, readers take *when* at its word.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Families, managers and landlords plan around your *when*s. *When I move out in March* on a message to a
landlord may start a search for a new tenant.

Under pressure, excited or hopeful, *when* comes out more easily than *if*. Check every *when* about
something that isn't fixed yet.

A formal reader marks *if it will* as an error. A family reader just believes your *when*.
