# One fix per fault

`CF-16` · Day 68

## The one-line answer

Each of your four gets a drill built from the **type** of the fault rather than from the fault
itself — because you cannot oil a door that needs planing, and most practice fails for exactly that
reason.

## The model

Think about the faults in an old house. A hinge that squeaks wants oil. A door that sticks in the
frame wants a millimetre taken off the edge. A handle that rattles wants tightening. Three faults,
all of them in the same door, and each one has exactly one fix. Put oil on a sticking door and you
have made a mess and changed nothing — and worse, you will have felt like you did some maintenance.

That is what happens to error practice. People take every fault they have and apply the same fix to
all of them: say it slowly a few times, think hard about it, try to remember it next time. That is
oil. It works on one of the seven types below and does nothing to the other six.

So the fix comes from the type. You have your four named piles from yesterday, and each one has a
`Type` on it from the ledger. Find your type in the seven below, build the drill in that shape,
using your own material. Nobody can build these for you, because nobody but you knows what is in
your ledger.

**`sound` — widen out from the sound, never in.** Start with the pair: the word you say and the
word you meant, back to back, twice. Then the right word inside a short phrase you would actually
use. Then that phrase inside a longer sentence, said at ordinary speed. Then the sentence inside a
run where you are also thinking about something else. Four rings, each one wider. The rule is that
you never go back inwards — the moment you are getting it right in the sentence, the isolated pair
is finished work and drilling it again is comfort. A sound you can only make on its own is not a
sound you own.

**`stress` — get the shape without the words first.** Say the word or the sentence with the stress
in the wrong place on purpose, then in the right place, back to back, twice. You need both in your
ear because a stress fault is invisible from the inside — it is not a wrong sound, it is a right
sound in the wrong place, and it sounds fine to the person making it. Then drop the words
altogether and hum the shape: *da-DA-da*, *DA-da-da-DA*. Hum it four or five times until the rhythm
is running on its own, then put the words back on top of the hum without changing the rhythm.
Rhythm first, words second. Doing it the other way round is how the wrong stress got in.

**`grammar` — one carrier sentence, fifteen different things.** Do not go back to the rule. You
almost certainly know the rule, and knowing it has not helped, which is the evidence that this is
not a knowledge fault. Build one sentence you would genuinely say, containing the form, and then
say it about fifteen different things as fast as you can produce them, without pausing between
them. The form stays; everything around it changes. Then run the whole set again faster than is
comfortable, because a grammar fault is nearly always a speed fault wearing a grammar costume —
the form is fine when you have room and collapses when you do not. If it survives the fast pass, it
is going in.

**`word` — build the slot the word has to fill.** This is for the wrong word arriving, or the word
never arriving at all. Make three sentences where the right word is the only word that fits, and
say them. Then make one more where the near-miss word actually belongs, and say that too, so the
two stop living in the same place in your head. Then add the escape route from Day 52 and run it
deliberately: say a sentence, refuse to use the word, and get round it. Both halves matter. One
teaches the word, the other means the sentence survives on a day when the word does not come.

**`freeze` — drill the restart, not the sentence.** The instinct is to rehearse the sentence you
froze on. That is the wrong repair, because the next freeze will happen on a different sentence.
What repeats is not the sentence, it is the moment. So walk into a freeze on purpose: start a
sentence about something you have not thought about, take it into a place you have no ending for,
and then get out — with a restart line, not with silence. You have them from Day 17. Do it four or
five times on four or five different subjects. What you are training is the reflex that fires
between the stop and the next word, and the countable version is: how many freezes restarted within
one breath?

**`filler` — replace one filler with silence, and only one.** Not all of them. Pick the single
filler that has the most rows behind it, and work on that one alone; going after your whole
inventory at once produces speech that is careful and dead. The drill: talk about something
ordinary, and every single time that filler wants to come out, close your mouth instead and let the
gap happen. It will feel enormous. It is not. Count the filler on the tape before and after — this
is the one type where the pass condition is a plain number going down, and where the fix is
something you stop doing rather than something you start.

**`speed` — two speeds, then find the third.** Say the same short thing twice: once at your runaway
speed, once at a speed that feels ridiculous, almost insultingly slow. Then find the one in
between and say it there. Do this three times on three different pieces of material. Then the
important half: listen to the slow version, because a speed fault almost always has another fault
hiding underneath it. Speed is a blanket — you cannot hear the sounds, the endings or the stress
through it. Whatever surfaces in the slow take goes into `docs/ERRORS.md` as its own row tonight,
and it may well be a better candidate for your four than the speed was.

**Now build your four.** One drill per pile, in the shape its type calls for. Say each drill's shape
out loud before you run it — *this one's a sound, so it's the four rings* — because naming the
shape is what stops you defaulting to oil. Write nothing down beyond the lines you are about to
say. You are building reps, not a document.

And run each drill **in the condition you attached to it yesterday.** If the *when* was *when I
speed up*, the drill happens fast. If it was *on the phone*, do it into a voice note with the phone
at your ear. If it was *when I'm telling a story*, wrap the drill in a story. A drill that skips the
condition trains the fault in a room where it never appears.

**How you know it died.** Three stages, and only the third one counts.

**One — right in the drill.** This is worth almost nothing. Of course it is right in the drill; you
are staring at it.

**Two — right in a sentence you made up on the spot, about something else entirely.** Better. Still
not it, because you knew what you were listening for.

**Three — right in a free rep where you were thinking about something else.** That is the death
test, and it is the only one that counts. It has to be a recording, and it has to be a recording
you made for another reason.

And it dies by its count going down, not by vanishing. A pile that was in every recording and now
turns up once is dead enough. Expect it back when you are tired or going fast — that is tomorrow's
day, and it is not a relapse, it is the fault telling you it is not finished.

**Today's phrases** — the language of working on a fault out loud, in front of somebody, without
turning it into a performance. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I'll say that one again slowly. | Giving a word a second, cleaner pass mid-conversation |
| One more time, at speed. | Ordering the fast pass on yourself |
| Wrong on purpose, then right. | Setting up the two-pass rep so a listener knows what's coming |
| That's the one I'm killing this week. | Naming your target when somebody catches you at it |
| Did that one land? | Asking a listener for a yes or no on a single sound |
| Say it back to me? | Getting the word returned so you can hear the two side by side |
| I've got that on my list. | Taking a correction without making a speech of it |
| It's gone everywhere except when I'm tired. | Reporting honest progress instead of a claim |

## Say it now

Use your own four. Say each line twice — once at ordinary speed, once faster than is comfortable.

1. *That's the one I'm killing this week — {{pile one}}.*
2. *Wrong on purpose, then right: {{the fault version}} … {{the fixed version}}.*
3. *I'll say that one again slowly. {{the phrase from pile two}}.*
4. *One more time, at speed. {{the same phrase}}.*
5. *Did that one land? {{the word from pile three}}.*
6. *Say it back to me? {{the word you want returned}}.*
7. *I've got that on my list — {{pile four}}.*
8. *It's gone everywhere except when I'm tired.*

Then run lines 2, 3 and 4 as one unbroken run on a single phrase, three times over: wrong, right,
slow, fast, no gaps anywhere. That run is the whole day compressed, and running it without stopping
is what stops each pass being a separate careful performance.

## The deliberate failure

Run the wrong drill on purpose. Take one of your four and treat it as a different type: drill a
stress fault as if it were a sound, ring by ring. Or drill a freeze by carefully rehearsing the
sentence you froze on. Or drill a grammar fault by saying the rule out loud several times. Give it
a real go — do not sabotage it.

**What breaking sounds like:** it is comfortable. That is the finding, and it is an unwelcome one.
Nothing feels wrong. You will sound clean and controlled on the drilled line, you will get to the
end feeling like you did some work, and then — this is the part to listen for — the fault will be
completely untouched the moment you say anything you did not drill. Rehearse the frozen sentence
and you will say that sentence beautifully and freeze on the next one instead.

Now run the right drill on the same fault and notice the second thing: it is awkward. It feels
clumsy and slightly stupid, especially the humming and the deliberately wrong pass. That awkwardness
is the whole signal. The right fix works on the part that is actually broken, and the part that is
actually broken has never been used, so of course it feels bad. A drill that feels good is usually
oil on a sticking door.

## When it breaks in the wild

You are mid-conversation and you hear your own fault land. So you stop and fix it. Then you hear
another one and stop and fix that. By the fourth repair the other person has stopped listening to
what you are saying and started watching your mouth, and you can see it happening, which makes the
next one worse. You end the conversation having said almost nothing, but very accurately.

**The repair:**

> Ignore that, I'll get it in a second.

Then carry on and do not get it. Pick one fault to catch in live speech — one, for a week — and let
the other three go completely until you are at the recorder. The drill is for the drill. A
conversation is for the conversation, and a person you have corrected yourself at four times will
not offer you a fifth.
