## One-line answer

To talk about what might happen, choose a step on the certainty ladder: *will definitely*, *will
probably*, *might*, *probably won't*, *definitely won't*. Then push that word, so the listener hears how
sure you are. *I MIGHT be late* is a different message from *I'll be late*.

## The story

Your friend is organising dinner for eight people on Friday. She asks who's coming. You say:

> I'll come. Maybe late.

She books a table for eight at seven. You arrive at nine, because the *maybe* was really *probably*, and
you knew your manager wanted a meeting. She's annoyed. She'd have kept a seat for you, but she'd also have
ordered without waiting.

Next time, you say:

> I'll probably be late, to be honest. There's a meeting that usually runs over. I might not make it
> before eight.

She says *OK, we'll order without you and keep you a plate.* Nobody waits, and nobody's annoyed.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **certain**: sure to happen.
- **possible**: it could happen, or could not.
- **likely**: it will probably happen.
- **the certainty ladder**: the steps from *definitely* to *definitely not*.
- **push**: to make one word the loudest in the sentence, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/01-speaking/1.1-push-the-word-you-mean.md).

On day 63 you learned to say how *fixed* a plan is. Today is about something wider: anything in the
future, a plan or not. The rain, the bus, the meeting, your mood on Friday.

The ladder, from top to bottom:

| Step | Words | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| certain yes | *will definitely* · *will* | *I'll definitely be there.* |
| likely | *will probably* | *I'll probably be late.* |
| possible | *might* · *may* · *could* | *I might not make it.* |
| unlikely | *probably won't* | *I probably won't eat much.* |
| certain no | *definitely won't* · *won't* | *I definitely won't miss it.* |

Notice where *probably* sits: after *will*, but before *won't*. *I'll probably come.* · *I probably
won't come.*

## Why Vaani needs it

Every plan with other people needs this: who's coming, who might be late, what might go wrong. In the
phase 4 gate you'll answer the other side of an argument, and *that might happen, but …* is how you
answer a worry. In phase 5 you'll give updates at work where *might* and *will* mean very different
things to a manager.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[The future: degrees of certainty](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/future-degrees-certainty)
arranges the future in levels, "Very Sure", "Sure", "Almost Sure" and "Not Sure", with examples:

> We'll probably finish the project by tomorrow.
> He probably won't have enough time.
> I might go to the party, but I'm not sure yet.

[might](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/might), with a US audio file, gives the sense "Used to indicate
conditional or possible actions; would perhaps/maybe", and the example:

> Well, I might go to the party on Sunday, but I haven't decided yet.

**Listen to *might* first**, then say it with the recorder on, then play both back. It rhymes with
*night*, and the *t* at the end is short, but it must be there.

**Push the ladder word.** It carries the message, so it's the loudest word:

> I MIGHT be late.
> I'll PROBably be late.
> I DEFinitely won't be late.

**Add a reason for anything below *definitely*.** The listener wants to know *why* it's uncertain:

> I might be late, because the meeting often runs over.

**A soft word for bad news.** *To be honest* before *probably won't* makes it kinder:

> To be honest, I probably won't make it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the *maybe* that hides a *probably***:

> Maybe I'll come.

You know it's unlikely. The listener hears fifty-fifty and plans for you.

The second is **the swallowed *might***: *I might be late* said so fast that it sounds like *I'll be
late* or *I be late*. The listener gets the wrong step on the ladder.

The third is ***probably* in the wrong place**: *I will not probably come.* It sounds odd, and the
listener pauses to work it out.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use the ladder constantly and precisely, and listeners plan exactly by it. *Probably*
gets a plate kept. *Might* gets *let us know*. *Definitely* gets a seat.

Under pressure, when you don't want to disappoint somebody, the step moves up: *probably not* becomes
*maybe*. It feels kinder, and it causes more trouble later.

A native listener hears *might* and *will* as very different promises, even said quickly. Make sure
yours can be heard.
