## One-line answer

Record six real sentences on different steps of the ladder, twice: once with the ladder word deliberately
swallowed, quick and quiet, and once pushed. Play them mixed up to a listener, who writes down how sure
you sounded. A swallowed *might* doesn't sound casual. It disappears, and the listener hears a promise.

## The story

On the phone to your landlord about the water heater, you say quickly:

> I might be home on Tuesday afternoon.

It comes out fast: *I'mi' be home Tuesday.* He hears *I'll be home Tuesday*, and sends the plumber. You're
not there. The plumber charges a visit fee, and your landlord is annoyed with you.

You said the right word. He just never heard it.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the certainty ladder**: the steps from *will definitely* to *definitely won't*, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-the-certainty-ladder.md).
- **swallow a word**: to say it so short and quiet that the listener never hears it, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/01-speaking/1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md).
- **push**: to make one word the loudest in the sentence.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The ladder words are small: *might*, *probably*, *won't*. When they're swallowed, the sentence still
sounds complete, but it has moved on the ladder:

| Said | Heard |
| --- | --- |
| *I MIGHT be home.* | possible |
| *I'mi' be home.* (swallowed) | *I'll be home*: certain |
| *I'll PROBably come.* | likely |
| *I'll prob' come.* (swallowed) | *I'll come*: certain |

## Why Vaani needs it

On the phone to a landlord, an office or a manager, there's no face to help. A swallowed ladder word turns
*maybe* into a promise, and you're held to it. Hearing that happen once, on purpose, is how you learn to
push the word.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[might](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/might): "Used to indicate conditional or possible actions; would
perhaps/maybe", with a US audio file.
[The future: degrees of certainty](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/future-degrees-certainty):
"I might go to the party, but I'm not sure yet."

**Step one: choose six real sentences** about your coming week, on at least four different steps of the
ladder.

**Step two: record each twice**: **S**, with the ladder word deliberately swallowed, fast and quiet; and
**P**, with it pushed. That makes twelve. Mix them up and keep a hidden list.

**Step three: the ladder test.** Play the twelve lines to a listener. After each, ask them to write down:

> How sure was I: definitely, probably, maybe, probably not, or definitely not?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when any P line is heard on the wrong step. Then
your pushed version still isn't clear. It is **GREEN** when every P line is heard on the right step, and
at least half the S lines are heard higher up the ladder than you meant.

**Step five: the repair drill.** For every P line heard wrongly, say the ladder word alone three times,
then the sentence with a tiny pause before the ladder word: *I … MIGHT be home.* Record it and try it on
a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an S that's still clear**. Say it as fast as you would in
a real hurry on the phone.

The second is **a listener who can see your face**. On the phone there's no face, so turn away, or play
the recording from another room.

The third, in real speech, is **the swallowed *not***: *I probably won't* heard as *I probably will*.
*Won't* needs its own push too: *I probably WON'T.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Phone calls about times, visits and deliveries are where swallowed ladder words cause the most trouble.
The other person writes down what they heard, not what you meant.

Under pressure, in a hurry or nervous, the small words go first. A tiny pause before the ladder word is
the simplest protection.

A native listener rarely asks *did you say might or will?* They choose one and act on it.
