## One-line answer

A written plan starts with one line saying the goal, then lists numbered steps in order. Each step has one
action, one person who does it, and a date or day it's done by. It ends with one *if* line for the most
likely problem. A reader should be able to point to any day and say what happens and who does it.

## The story

Your parents are moving to a smaller flat at the end of the month. Four of you are helping: you, your
brother, your sister and your uncle. After a week of phone calls, nobody knows who's doing what.

You write it down and send it to the family group:

> **Goal: Amma and Appa in the new flat by Sunday the 28th.**
>
> 1. Book the tempo for Saturday the 27th. — Ravi, by the 15th.
> 2. Pack the kitchen. — Meena, on the weekend of the 20th.
> 3. Pack the books and clothes. — Me, on the weekend of the 20th.
> 4. Get the new flat cleaned. — Uncle, by Friday the 26th.
> 5. Move everything. — All of us, Saturday the 27th, from 8 am.
>
> If the tempo can't come on the 27th, we'll move on Sunday instead.

That evening your brother books the tempo, and for the first time in a week nobody phones anybody.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **goal**: the thing the whole plan is for.
- **step**: one instruction in a set of directions, from
  [day 23](../../../day-23-asking-directions/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-instruction-per-step.md). Here,
  one action in a plan.
- **who**: the one person responsible for a step.
- **deadline**: the day or time by which you need something, from
  [day 31](../../../day-31-asking-for-help/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-softened-question.md).
- **imperative**: a sentence telling somebody to do something, with no subject written, from
  [day 32](../../../day-32-giving-instructions/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-imperative-on-paper.md).
- **first conditional**: an *if* sentence about a real, possible future, from
  [day 67](../../../day-67-suggestions-and-choosing/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-now-will-later.md).

On day 32 you wrote instructions: steps that tell a reader *how* to do something. A plan is different. It
says *who* will do *what*, and *by when*, to reach a goal. Instructions can be followed by anybody. A plan
is for particular people.

| Part | What it says | Grammar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| goal line | what the plan is for, and by when | a short line, often in bold |
| each step | one action, one person, one date | imperative, then *who*, *when* |
| backup line | what happens if the likeliest problem comes | first conditional |

## Why Vaani needs it

Plans for families, events and work are some of the most useful things you'll ever write in English. In
phase 5, meeting notes and status updates are plans that other people act on. And the next writing day,
explaining a process, uses the same numbered steps.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Action plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_plan). It opens:

> An action plan is a detailed plan outlining actions needed to reach one or more goals.

Among the things it says a plan should have: "Action steps should be clear and actionable versus vague
ideas or thoughts", "Responsibility: each action step needs to have one person responsible", and a
"Completion date".

**The shape on paper.**

```text
   Goal: <what> by <when>

   1. <Action>. — <who>, <by when>.
   2. <Action>. — <who>, <by when>.
   3. ...

   If <likeliest problem>, <what we'll do>.
```

**One action per step.** *Book the tempo* is a step. *Book the tempo and pack the kitchen* is two steps.

**One name per step.** *All of us* is fine for the day everyone works together. Otherwise, one name. When
two people own a step, each thinks the other is doing it.

**A real date, not a feeling.** *By the 15th*, *on Saturday the 27th*, not *soon* or *next week
sometime*.

**Numbered in time order.** The step that has to happen first is number 1.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the plan with no names**:

> 1. Book the tempo. 2. Pack the kitchen. 3. Clean the new flat.

Everyone reads it, and everyone thinks someone else will do it.

The second is **the plan with no dates**: every step has a name, and nothing says when. The tempo is
booked the day before, and it's full.

The third is **the step that's a wish**: *Make sure everything goes smoothly.* That's not an action
anyone can do. Break it into things people can do.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Family events, office projects and community work all run on plans like this. Often they're typed on a
phone into a group. The good ones fit on one screen.

Under pressure, a busy week with lots of people, the names and dates are what get left out, because
everyone *knows*. They don't, and that's why the phone rings.

A reader who can point to any day on your plan and say what happens and who does it has a plan they can
use. That's the test in the next part.

## Check yourself

- **Write:** `TODO(me)` Think of a real thing that needs several people or several days: a move, a
  celebration, a repair, a trip, a job at work. Write the goal line with a date. Write four to six numbered
  steps, each with one action, one name and one real date. Write one *if* line for the likeliest problem.
  Then check every step: one action, one name, one date.
- **Say out loud:** Read your goal line and your first step. Say who does step 1 and by when.
