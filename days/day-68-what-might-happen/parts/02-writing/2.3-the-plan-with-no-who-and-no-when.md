## One-line answer

Make a copy of your real plan with the names and dates taken out, and one step turned into a wish. Then
give it to a reader with a calendar and ask *what happens on each day, and who does it?* A plan with no
owners and no dates still looks like a plan. The reader just can't use it.

## The story

Your office is moving to a new floor. Your manager sends this:

> Plan for the move:
> 1. Pack all files.
> 2. Label the boxes.
> 3. Book the lift.
> 4. Move the computers.
> 5. Make sure everything goes smoothly.

It looks organised. Numbered, in order, clear actions. On moving day, nobody has booked the lift,
because everyone thought someone else would. The files are packed and nobody labelled the boxes.
Step 5 happened to nobody, because nobody can *do* it.

A colleague says what everyone's thinking: *Who was meant to book the lift? And by when?*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **goal**, **step**, **owner**, **deadline**: from [part 2.1](2.1-goal-steps-who-when.md) and
  [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-plan.md).
- **wish step**: a step that sounds like an action but that nobody could actually do: *make sure it
  goes well*.
- **calendar test**: asking a reader to put each step of a plan on a real day, with a name.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A plan without owners and dates reads well and works badly:

| What's missing | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| the owner | everybody thinks somebody else is doing it |
| the date | it's done too late, or the night before |
| a real action (a wish step) | nothing happens, because nothing can |

## Why Vaani needs it

Plans you write at work in phase 5, meeting notes and status updates, will be judged by whether people
could act on them. A reader can tell you, from the page alone, which steps they could act on. This part
shows you what it looks like when they can't.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Action plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_plan): "Action steps should be clear and actionable
versus vague ideas or thoughts"; "Responsibility: each action step needs to have one person
responsible"; and a "Completion date".

**Step one: take your real plan** from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-plan.md). Keep it safe.

**Step two: make copy E**, deliberately empty: remove every name and every date, and replace one real
step with a wish step. Label it *deliberate*.

**Step three: the calendar test.** Give copy E to one reader and your real plan to a different reader.
Give each a blank week on paper. Ask:

> Could you write each step on the day it happens, with the name of the person who does it?

**Step four: fill the table.**

| Copy | Steps placed on a day | Steps with a name | Steps they couldn't place |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E (deliberate) | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| my real plan | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader of your real plan can't place
any step on a day with a name. Then your real plan has a gap. It is **GREEN** when every step of your
real plan is placed with a name, and copy E's reader can place almost none.

**Step five: repair.** Fix every step the reader couldn't place in your real plan. If they asked a
question, the answer goes into the plan.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who knows the plan**. They'll fill in names from
memory. Use someone who isn't in it.

The second is **leaving one date in copy E by accident**. Check it before you hand it over.

The third, in real writing, is **the *we* step**: *We'll book the lift.* Who is *we*? Unless everyone is
really doing it together, *we* means nobody.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Most failed plans at work and at home fail at the owner and the date, not at the idea. People are good at
listing what to do and bad at saying who and when, because it feels bossy.

Under pressure, a plan written in a hurry, the wish step appears: *make sure it all works*. Replace it
with the real action behind it: *Test the lift on Friday. — Ravi.*

A reader who fills a blank week from your plan without asking you anything has been given a plan they can
act on.
