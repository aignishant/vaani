## One-line answer

To describe a process out loud, say first what it makes and how many stages there are (*There are four
stages*), then walk through them with a signpost at the start of each (*First … The second stage … After
that … Finally …*), and check the listener is with you halfway. The count is a map. The signposts show
where you are on it.

## The story

A visitor from another city asks your uncle how the jaggery in your village is made. He starts talking:

> So they cut the cane and then it goes to the crusher and the juice comes and they boil it, a long time,
> in the big pans, and they take the dirt off the top, and when it's thick it goes in the moulds …

The visitor nods, but after a while her eyes go blank. She can't tell how many steps there are, or which
one he's on.

Your cousin tries:

> It's made in four stages. First, the cane is cut and crushed for juice. Second, the juice is boiled in
> big iron pans. Third, while it boils, they take the dirt off the top. And finally, when it's thick, it's
> poured into moulds to set. Does that make sense so far?

The visitor says it back: *Cut and crush, boil, clean, set. Four stages.* She could explain it to someone
else.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **process**: a set of stages that happen in order to make something or to get something done.
- **stage**: one part of a process.
- **signpost**: a word that tells a listener where they are in a sequence, from
  [day 44](../../../day-44-time-words-aloud/parts/01-speaking/1.1-first-then-after-that-in-the-end.md).
  Here: *first*, *the second stage*, *finally*, at the start of each stage.
- **check-in**: a short question to see if the listener is still following.

On day 32 you gave instructions: *Boil the water. Add the tea.* Instructions tell a listener what to do.
A process description is different. It tells a listener **how something happens or is done**, often by
other people, somewhere else: how jaggery is made, how a passport is issued, how your office handles an
order.

| Instructions | A process |
| --- | --- |
| tells *you* what to do | tells you *how it's done* |
| *Boil the juice.* | *The juice is boiled.* · *They boil the juice.* |
| the listener will do it | the listener wants to understand it |

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Tell a story or personal anecdote](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/tell-story-or-personal-anecdote).
Its tips for speaking at length include:

> Use sequencing words: first of all, then, after that, later on, finally, in the end...
> Say what happened step by step.

The same words mark the stages of a process.

**Step one: say what it makes, and how many stages.**

> Jaggery is made in four stages.
> Getting a new ration card takes three steps.

**Step two: a signpost at the start of every stage.** Mix them so they don't sound like a list:

| Stage | Signposts |
| --- | --- |
| first | *First,* · *First of all,* · *The first stage is …* |
| middle | *Then* · *After that,* · *The second stage is …* · *Next,* |
| last | *Finally,* · *In the end,* · *The last stage is …* |

**Step three: one short stage per breath.** Pause after each stage, as you would at a full stop.

**Step four: a check-in halfway.**

> Does that make sense so far? · With me so far?

**Step five: say the result again at the end.**

> … and that's how you get jaggery.

**Who does it?** For a process, *they* or *you* is fine: *They boil the juice.* *You fill in the form.*
Here *you* means *anyone*, not the listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the stream**: one long *and then … and then …* with no count and no
signposts. The listener loses count at the third *and*.

The second is **the missing result**. You describe every stage and never say what it all makes. The
listener doesn't know what the stages are for.

The third is **the count that doesn't match**: *There are three stages* and then five. The listener stops
trusting the map.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

People who explain processes well, like trainers, guides and good managers, often give the count
first. It's how a listener knows how long to concentrate.

Under pressure, explaining something you know very well, people skip the count because the stages feel
obvious. To a listener who doesn't know them, they're not.

A native listener who says the stages back in order, *cut, boil, clean, set*, has understood. That's the
check you'll use in the next part.
