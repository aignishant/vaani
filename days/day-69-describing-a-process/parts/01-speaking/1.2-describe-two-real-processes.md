## One-line answer

Describe two real processes you know well to two people who don't know them, with the count, the
signposts, a check-in and the result. Then ask each listener to name the stages back in order. If they
get the count and the order right, your map worked.

## The story

A new colleague asks how an order goes through your shop, from the phone call to the delivery. You've
done it a thousand times. You write the stages on the back of a receipt first: *call, write the slip,
pack, check, deliver.* Five.

Then you tell her:

> It's done in five stages. First, the customer calls and we take the order. Second, we write it on a
> slip, in duplicate. After that, the packer packs it. Then the owner checks the box against the slip.
> That's the important one. With me so far? Finally, the boy delivers it and brings back the signed
> slip.

She says it back: *Call, slip, pack, check, deliver. And the check is the owner.* On her third day she
catches a missing item at stage four.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **process**, **stage**: a set of stages that happen in order, and one of them, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-count-the-stages-then-walk-them.md).
- **signpost**, **check-in**: from [part 1.1](1.1-count-the-stages-then-walk-them.md).
- **stage list**: the stages written as one or two words each, before you speak.
- **name back**: when a listener lists the stages you described, in order.

Writing the stage list first is what makes the count true. If you count as you speak, you'll get it
wrong.

## Why Vaani needs it

Describing a real process to someone who needs it is the practice for tomorrow's day and for work in phase
5. The name-back test is how a trainer checks a new person understood.

**Step three: record each description** with the count, a signpost on every stage, a check-in, and the
result at the end.

**Step four: listen back** and count the signposts. One per stage?

**Step five: tell each to a real person** who doesn't know it. Afterwards, ask:

> Could you name the stages back, in order?

Write their answer down and compare it with your stage list.

**Step six: for any stage they missed or put out of order**, record that stage again with a clearer
signpost and a pause before it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **too much detail in one stage**: stage three becomes a story of its own.
The listener forgets the map. Keep each stage short, and add detail only if they ask.

The second is **a stage the listener needs and you skipped**, because it's so obvious to you. If they ask
*and then what?* between two stages, you've found it.

The third is **the same signpost every time**: *then … then … then*. It's better than nothing, but the
listener can't hear which number you're on. Mix in *the second stage* and *finally*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Training a new colleague, helping a relative with an official form, showing a visitor how something in
your town works: all of these are process descriptions, and the listener nearly always wants the map first.

Under pressure, a queue behind you or a busy shop, the count and the signposts are what let you go fast
without losing the listener.

A native listener who names your stages back in order can now do something with them.
