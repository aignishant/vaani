## One-line answer

Record one real process twice: once deliberately as a stream, with no count, no signposts and *and then*
everywhere, and once with the map. Play them to two listeners and ask each to name the stages. A process
with no map doesn't sound relaxed. It sounds like one long stage, and the listener can't count it.

## The story

At the post office, the clerk explains how to send a parcel abroad:

> So you fill this and then you take it to that counter and they weigh it and then you pay and then
> the customs form, no wait, the customs form first, and then they give you a receipt and you keep it.

You go to the counter. They send you back for the customs form. You pay at the wrong window. It takes
three trips.

A second clerk, the next week, says:

> There are four steps. First, the customs form. Second, weighing at counter two. Third, pay at
> counter three. Last, keep the receipt with the tracking number.

One trip.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **stage**, **signpost**: from [part 1.1](1.1-count-the-stages-then-walk-them.md).
- **name back**: when a listener lists the stages you described, in order, from
  [part 1.2](1.2-describe-two-real-processes.md).
- **stream**: a description with no count and no signposts, all joined with *and then*.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The words in the stream and the map can be almost the same. What changes is whether the listener can see
the edges:

| Version | What the listener can do afterwards |
| --- | --- |
| stream: *and then … and then … and then* | remember two or three things, not in order |
| map: *four stages. First … Second … Third … Last …* | name all four, in order |

## Why Vaani needs it

Tomorrow you explain how something works to a beginner, and in phase 5 you'll explain processes at work.
Hearing, on purpose, how little a listener keeps from a stream is how you learn to give the map every time.

happened step by step."

**Step one: choose one real process** with four to six stages that your listeners don't know.

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **S**, the deliberate stream: no count, no signposts except *and then*, no pauses between stages, no
   result at the end.
2. **M**, the map: the count, a signpost on every stage, a pause after each, a check-in and the result.

**Step three: the name-back test.** Play S to one listener and M to a different listener. Ask each:

> How many stages were there? Could you name them, in order?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a stream that's secretly a map**. Your *and then*s fall
exactly on the stage edges, with pauses. Run the stages together the way the first clerk did.

The second is **a listener who knows the process**. They'll name it from memory. Choose someone who
doesn't.

The third, in real speech, is **the correction mid-stream**: *no wait, the form first.* Fixing the order
out loud confuses more than it helps. Write the stage list before you speak.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Clerks, trainers and relatives explain processes as streams all the time, because they know them too
well to see the stages. Listeners go along with it, then get something wrong later.

Under pressure, explaining something at a counter with a queue, the map feels slow. It isn't. It saves the
second and third trip.

A native listener given a map often repeats the count: *four steps, OK.* That's a listener getting ready
to follow you.
