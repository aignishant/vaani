## One-line answer

Write a real label, sign or short notice of about eighty words about something made, grown or built near
you, with at least five passive sentences in the present and the past. Then ask a reader *what is this
about?* If they name the thing, not a person, your passives put the right thing at the front.

## The story

The small temple at the end of your street is two hundred years old, and visitors keep asking about it.
The committee asks you to write a board for the gate. You write:

> This temple was built in 1820 by the weavers of the village. The stone was brought from the river by
> bullock cart. The roof was repaired in 1962, after a storm. Today, the temple is looked after by the
> families of the street, and the lamp is lit every evening at six. Visitors are welcome. Shoes are left
> at the gate.

A visitor reads it and says to her son: *So the weavers built it, and the stone came from the river.* She
got the temple, the stone, and the one doer who mattered.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **passive**, **active**, **doer**: from [part 2.1](2.1-be-plus-the-third-form.md).
- **label**: a small piece of writing on a product, saying what it is.
- **notice**: a short piece of writing put up where people will read it, like a sign.

Labels, signs and notices are about things. That's why they're the natural home of the passive: the
thing goes first, and the doer is named only if it matters.

**Step three: write the piece**, about eighty words, with at least five passives: at least two present
and two past. Keep one or two active sentences where a person really matters, like *Visitors are welcome*
or *We deliver on Sundays*.

**Step four: the *be* check.** Underline every passive. Is there a *be*? Does it match the subject: *is*
or *are*, *was* or *were*? Is the verb after it a past participle?

**Step five: the *by* check.** Circle every *by* + doer. Does the reader need it? If not, cut it.

**Step six: the what-is-it-about test.** Give it to a reader and ask:

> What is this about, in three words?

If they name the thing, it worked. If they name a person, the actives are too strong.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **every sentence passive**. *Visitors are welcomed by us. Shoes are asked
to be left.* It becomes stiff. Keep the passive for the thing, and use the active when a person is the
point.

The second is ***by* on everything**: *The lamp is lit by someone every evening.* If the doer is *someone*
or *people*, leave it out.

The third is **the wrong tense of *be***: *The temple is built in 1820.* The date is past, so *was*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Heritage boards, food labels, product cards and notices at offices are where most people read passives
every day. The best ones are short, and every sentence starts with what the reader is looking at.

Under pressure, a sign written quickly, people use the active with *we*: *We close on Sundays.* That's
fine for a shop. For a thing, a place or a product, the passive usually reads better.

A reader who says what the piece is about in three words, and names the thing, has read it the way you
wanted.
