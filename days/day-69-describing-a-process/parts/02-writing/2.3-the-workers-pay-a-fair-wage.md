## One-line answer

Plant four passive errors in a copy of your real piece, including one where the *be* is dropped so that
the sentence turns active and says the opposite (*The workers pay a fair wage* for *The workers are paid a
fair wage*). Ask a reader *who does what?* for each sentence. A missing *be* doesn't always look like a
mistake. Sometimes it looks like a different fact.

## The story

A small cooperative in your town makes cotton bags, and you help write the card that goes with each one.
You mean to write:

> The workers are paid a fair wage.

In a hurry, you write:

> The workers pay a fair wage.

A customer reads it and asks the shopkeeper: *The workers pay? Pay whom?* The shopkeeper laughs and
explains. But another customer, who didn't ask, now thinks the workers pay to work there.

Two letters, *are*, were missing. The sentence was still perfect English. It just said something else.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **plant**: to put an error into a text on purpose, from
  [day 46](../../../day-46-describing-a-trip/parts/02-writing/2.3-there-was-two-beds.md).
- **passive**, **active**, **doer**: from [part 2.1](2.1-be-plus-the-third-form.md).
- **who does what**: which person or thing does the action, and which receives it.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Some passive errors only look wrong. Others make the sentence mean something else:

| Error | Example | What the reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *be* dropped, and the verb looks active | *The workers pay a fair wage.* | the wrong fact: the workers pay |
| *be* dropped, verb clearly a participle | *The temple built in 1820.* | an error, meaning mostly clear |
| base form after *be* | *The bags are make by hand.* | an error, meaning clear |
| wrong tense of *be* | *The roof is repaired in 1962.* | an error: a past date with a present verb |

The first one is the dangerous one, because nothing on the page looks broken.

## Why Vaani needs it

In reports, notices and formal letters in phase 5, the passive carries facts about who did what. A dropped
*be* that turns a sentence active can change a fact in a way no spell-check will catch. Seeing a reader get
it wrong, once, is how you learn to check.

participle"; "Only the form of be changes to make the tense. The past participle stays the same."

**Step one: take your real piece** from [part 2.2](2.2-write-how-it-is-made.md). Keep a clean copy.

**Step two: plant four errors** in a new copy, one of each kind in the table, with a hidden list. For the
first, find a passive whose verb has the same past and participle form, like *paid*, *made* or *sold*,
and whose subject could also be a doer. Drop the *be*, and change the verb to the present if you need to,
so it reads as active.

**Step three: the who-does-what test.** Give the planted copy to a reader who hasn't seen the clean one.
For each sentence, ask:

> Who does what here?

Then ask them to circle anything that looks wrong.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a first error that still looks passive**: *The workers
paid a fair wage* can still be read either way. Make it clearly active, with a present verb if needed.

The second is **a reader who knows the thing**. They'll give the doer from knowledge. Choose someone who
doesn't.

The third, in real writing, is ***be* lost at a line break or in a list**: *All bags checked by hand* on a
card. Fine on a label, but in a sentence the reader has to guess.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Customers, visitors and officials read short pieces quickly and take the facts at face value. A missing
*be* that flips the doer isn't noticed. It's believed.

Under pressure, a card or notice written fast, *be* is the smallest word in the sentence and the first to
go. Read every passive once, looking only for *be*.

A careful reader who asks *who does what?* and gets the right answer every time is reading exactly what you
meant.
