# The route you can walk in the dark

`FL-10` · Day 69

## The one-line answer

On a bad day your English does not disappear — it narrows to the part you own best, and today you
record that narrow version on purpose so you find out how high your floor actually is.

## The model

Walking home on a route you know, with a torch that is nearly out. You do not stop. You do not
suddenly forget where you live. What happens is that the beam gets small, so you stop lighting up
the whole street and start lighting up the next few steps — and you get home, slower, on a route
your feet already know.

That is what tired English is. Not a collapse. A narrowing. And it is worth knowing exactly how
narrow, because right now you are guessing, and people always guess low.

Three conditions degrade speech, and they do not degrade it in the same way.

**Tired.** Retrieval slows down first. The word is there, it just arrives late, and while you are
waiting for it the sentence you had started keeps going without a plan. What you hear on a tired
recording is long sentences that lose their shape somewhere in the middle and land somewhere that
does not match the beginning. The newest phrases — the ones from the last few weeks — go first,
because they are the least worn in. And your first language leaks: a rhythm, a filler, sometimes a
whole small word.

**A bad line.** You lose the face. Everything you have been doing without noticing — reading
whether they followed you, catching the small frown, taking the half-nod as permission to carry on
— is gone, and you are speaking into nothing. Then the overlap goes: the sound arrives late, so you
both start, both stop, both start. Day 46 gave you the phone version of this. Today it is a
condition rather than a subject.

**Pressure.** Breath goes shallow and high in the chest, speed goes up, and the pauses vanish
first — which is unfortunate, because the pauses were doing the work of finding the next thing.
Fast and unbreathing is the sound of pressure, and it is the one degradation that makes you *harder*
to follow rather than slower.

**About the nervous part — read this properly.** Do not make yourself anxious for this recording.
Do not go looking for something to dread, do not think your way into a state, and do not use a real
thing you are genuinely worried about as material. That is not practice, it is just a bad evening,
and nothing in this curriculum is worth it.

The honest version of pressure is physical and small. Record standing up rather than sitting.
Record straight after going up and down the stairs a few times, or after a brisk walk, so your
breath is genuinely short and high. Record with the ordinary noise of a room around you rather than
in silence. Any of those gives you the real thing — raised heart rate, short breath, a body that is
not settled — without asking you to feel bad. That is the whole of it, and it is enough. If you are
already tired today, you have most of the condition for free.

**What holds, and what goes.** This is the useful finding and it is consistent.

What holds: short sentences. The chunks you have said hundreds of times. One idea per breath.
Anything your mouth can produce without your attention.

What goes: long sentences with clauses hanging off them, the phrases you learned recently, precise
vocabulary, and anything that needed planning.

So the strategy is not to fight for the wide beam. It is to **narrow it deliberately, before it
narrows on its own.** Shorter units. One idea, stop, next idea. Say the important word twice — once
early, once at the end — so it survives the line even if the middle does not. Let the pauses be
long; on a bad line a long pause reads as a bad line, not as a freeze. And say out loud that
conditions are poor, rather than quietly working around them, because a listener who knows you are
half asleep hears slow speech as tiredness, and a listener who does not hears it as bad English.

**Today's phrases** — the lines that keep a conversation going when you have nothing spare. All of
these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Let me do this in short bits. | Announcing the narrow beam so the gaps read as choice |
| I'm half asleep, so this'll be slow. | Naming your state instead of speeding up to cover it |
| Can I say that again, shorter? | Trading a sentence that collapsed for one that won't |
| You're breaking up — just the last bit again. | Asking for the part you lost, not the whole thing |
| I'll keep it to the main bit. | Capping what you take on when you have nothing spare |
| The important word is {{…}} — the rest you can guess. | Getting one word through a bad line intact |
| Did that get through? | Checking the line without dropping the thought |
| I'll be better on this tomorrow, honestly. | Closing without apologising for the whole of your English |

## Say it now

Run these once in ordinary conditions, then once in the degraded ones — standing, breath short,
room noisy — and listen for what changes. Same lines, same order, twice through.

1. *Let me do this in short bits. {{first bit}}. {{second bit}}.*
2. *I'm half asleep, so this'll be slow — {{what you're going to say anyway}}.*
3. *Can I say that again, shorter? {{the short version}}.*
4. *You're breaking up — just the last bit again.*
5. *I'll keep it to the main bit — {{the main bit}}.*
6. *The important word is {{the word}} — the rest you can guess.*
7. *Did that get through?*
8. *I'll be better on this tomorrow, honestly.*

Then run lines 1, 5 and 7 as one continuous stretch, twice, with a full long pause after each unit
— longer than feels right, long enough to be uncomfortable. You are training the pause to stay
available under load, because the pause is the first thing pressure takes and the thing you most
need back.

## The deliberate failure

While you are in the degraded state, take one long complicated sentence — three clauses, at least
one *which* or *although* — and try to land it whole. Then do it again with a different one.

**What breaking sounds like:** listen for where it goes wrong, because it is not where you expect.
You will not run out of words. You knew every word in that sentence. What goes is the
**architecture** — about two thirds of the way through you lose track of how the sentence was
going to end, and you land somewhere that does not agree with how it started. Or you restart the
same clause, twice, in slightly different words, hunting for the shape you had already lost.

That is the whole finding of this day. Tiredness does not take your English away, it takes your
ability to hold a long structure in the air while you are also speaking it. And the fix follows
directly: stop building long structures when the torch is low. Say the same content in three short
sentences and it all arrives, in the same state, with no effort at all.

Say it in three short ones now, straight after, on the same tape. The short version is more
intelligible and it will surprise you how much.

## When it breaks in the wild

Late call at the end of a long day, on a line that keeps dropping syllables. They say *sorry,
what?* So you say it again, louder and faster, because loud and fast is what everybody does. It
does not work — volume was never the problem — so they ask again, and now you are embarrassed as
well as tired, and by the third attempt you give up and say *never mind, it doesn't matter*, about
something that did matter.

**The repair:**

> The important word is *Thursday* — the rest you can guess.

One word, said clearly and on its own, gets through a bad line when a whole sentence will not. Say
the load-bearing word by itself, then rebuild the sentence around it. *Never mind* is the expensive
option, and it is the one everybody reaches for.
