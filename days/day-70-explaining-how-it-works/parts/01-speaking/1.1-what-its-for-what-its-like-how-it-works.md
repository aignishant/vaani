## One-line answer

To explain how something works to someone who doesn't know, start with what it's **for**, then say what
it's **like**, comparing it to something they already know, and only then say **how it works** in two or
three stages. End by asking them to say it back. A beginner needs the purpose before the parts.

## The story

Your grandmother asks how paying by phone works. Your nephew, who knows everything about phones, starts:

> So the app connects to your bank account through a code, and it sends a request to the server, and
> then there's a PIN, and it's linked to your number …

Your grandmother nods politely and understands nothing.

You try:

> It's a way to pay a shop without cash. It's like writing a cheque, but on the phone. You type how much,
> the shop's name comes up, and you type your secret number, like signing the cheque. Then the money goes
> from your bank to theirs. Can you tell me back how you'd pay the vegetable man?

She says: *I type the money, check the name, put my number, and it goes from my bank.* She pays the
vegetable man herself the next morning.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **beginner**: somebody who is new to something and knows nothing about it yet.
- **purpose**: what something is for.
- **comparison**: saying one thing is like another thing: *it's like a cheque*.
- **stage**: one part of a process, from
  [day 69](../../../day-69-describing-a-process/parts/01-speaking/1.1-count-the-stages-then-walk-them.md).
- **say back**: when a listener tells you in their own words what they understood, from
  [day 59](../../../day-59-mistake-you-made/parts/01-speaking/1.2-tell-two-real-mistakes.md).

Yesterday you described a process with a map of stages. Today's listener is different: they don't know
what the thing is for, and they don't have the words for its parts. So the stages come later.

Four moves, in this order:

| Move | What you say | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. for | what it does for them | *It's a way to pay a shop without cash.* |
| 2. like | a comparison with something they know | *It's like writing a cheque, but on the phone.* |
| 3. how | two or three stages, in their words | *You type the amount, check the name, type your number.* |
| 4. back | ask them to say it back | *Can you tell me how you'd pay the vegetable man?* |

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Analogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogy). It opens:

> Analogy is a comparison or correspondence between two things (or two groups of things) because of a
> third element that they are considered to share.

In move 2, the *third element* is the thing both share: a cheque and a phone payment both move money
from your bank when you sign or type your secret number.

**Move 1 is one sentence.** *It's a way to …* · *It's what …* · *It's for …*

**Move 2: choose a comparison from the listener's life**, not yours. For a grandmother, a cheque. For a
child, a piggy bank. For a farmer, a water tank.

> It's like a water tank for money.

**Move 3: two or three stages, with no new words.** If you must use a new word, explain it straight away:
*the PIN, that's your secret number*.

**Move 4: ask for a say-back about their own life.** Not *do you understand?* People usually say yes to
that. Ask them to tell you how *they* would use it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **starting inside**: the parts first, the purpose never. The beginner has
no idea why any part matters.

The second is **the comparison from your world**: *It's like an API call.* It helps nobody who doesn't
already know. Choose something from theirs.

The third is ***do you understand?*** instead of a say-back. The listener says *yes*, to be polite, and you
learn nothing.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Good explainers, like a patient teacher or a pharmacist explaining a new medicine, often start with *this
is for …* and use one comparison. Listeners often remember the comparison long after they forget the details.

Under pressure, a queue at the counter or an impatient relative, people jump straight to the parts
because it feels faster. It isn't. The purpose and the comparison take two sentences and save ten.

A native listener who says your explanation back in their own words, about their own life, has really
understood. That's the only check that counts.
