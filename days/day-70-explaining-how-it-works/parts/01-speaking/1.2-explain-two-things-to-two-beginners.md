## One-line answer

Explain two real things to two real beginners with the four moves: *for*, *like*, *how*, *back*. Then
write down their say-back. If they can say how they'd use the thing in their own life, the explanation
worked, even if they can't name a single part.

## The story

Your neighbour's son has just started work, and he asks you how the provident fund at your office works.
He's heard the words and understood none of them.

You plan it first: *for* — money saved for later; *like* — a piggy bank that your company also puts
money into; *how* — some of your salary goes in every month, the company adds the same, and you get it
back later with interest. Then you tell him, and ask: *So if you left in five years, what would you
have?*

He says: *What I put in, what they put in, and a bit extra.* He's never used the words *employer
contribution*, and he doesn't need to.

That evening your mother asks how the new gas meter works, and you do it again, with a different
comparison.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have heard you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the four moves**: *for*, *like*, *how*, *back*, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-its-for-what-its-like-how-it-works.md).
- **beginner**: somebody who is new to something, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-its-for-what-its-like-how-it-works.md).
- **plan card**: four lines on paper, one for each move, written before you explain.
- **jargon**: words used inside one field or workplace, from
  [day 33](../../../day-33-your-job-or-studies/parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-answer-nobody-could-follow.md).

A beginner's say-back is in *their* words, not yours. That's the point. If they repeat your words exactly,
they may only have remembered them. If they say it in their own words, about their own life, they've
understood.

## Why Vaani needs it

Explaining something to a real person who needs it is the practice for phase 5, where you'll explain work
things to new colleagues and customers. The say-back is how you know it worked.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **choosing someone who already half-knows**. Their say-back will be good
whatever you said. Find a real beginner.

The second is **a comparison that needs its own explanation**. If you have to explain the comparison, pick
a simpler one.

The third is **correcting their say-back with your words**. If their words are right, even if they're not
the proper words, leave them.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

Explaining things to parents, children, new colleagues and customers is one of the most useful things
you'll do in any language. People remember who explained something to them well.

Under pressure, explaining your own job, jargon comes back first, because those are the words you use
every day. The jargon hunt before you speak catches most of it.

A beginner who goes and uses the thing afterwards, like the grandmother paying the vegetable man, is the
strongest evidence there is.
