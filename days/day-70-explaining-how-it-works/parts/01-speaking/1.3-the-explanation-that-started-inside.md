## One-line answer

Record one real explanation twice: once deliberately starting inside, with the parts first and no purpose
or comparison, and once with the four moves. Stop each recording after three sentences and ask a beginner
*what is this thing for?* An explanation that starts inside doesn't sound clever. The beginner can't even
say what it's for.

## The story

At the bank, a clerk explains a fixed deposit to a woman opening her first account:

> So the principal is locked for the tenure, and the interest is compounded quarterly, and on maturity
> you get the principal plus accrued interest, unless there's premature withdrawal, which has a penalty.

The woman says *OK* and signs nothing. She leaves to ask her son.

The next clerk says:

> It's a way to save money for a fixed time and get more back. It's like lending the bank your money for
> a year, and they pay you for it. You put money in, you leave it for the year, and at the end you get it
> back with extra. If you take it out early, you get a bit less. So if you put in ten thousand, when would
> you want it back?

She says *after Diwali next year*, and opens the deposit.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the four moves**: *for*, *like*, *how*, *back*, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-its-for-what-its-like-how-it-works.md).
- **start inside**: begin an explanation with the parts and how they connect, before saying what the
  thing is for.
- **three-sentence stop**: stopping an explanation after three sentences to check what the listener has so
  far.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The first three sentences decide whether a beginner keeps listening:

| First three sentences | What the beginner can say after them |
| --- | --- |
| parts, words, connections | *I don't know what it is* |
| what it's for, what it's like, the first stage | *It's for saving money, like lending it to the bank* |

## Why Vaani needs it

In phase 5 you'll explain technical things at work and to customers, often to people who will decide
something because of what you said. A beginner who can't say what the thing is for won't decide. They'll
go and ask someone else.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Analogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogy): "a comparison or correspondence between two things …
because of a third element that they are considered to share".
[Technical writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_writing) makes the same point for any
explanation: "To be effective, the expert and 'layman' reader must be equally capable of understanding
the same procedures." A *layman* is someone who isn't an expert.

**Step one: choose one real thing** from [part 1.2](1.2-explain-two-things-to-two-beginners.md), or a new
one, that you know well.

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **I**, deliberately starting inside: the parts and their proper names first, how they connect, no
   purpose, no comparison.
2. **O**, the four moves: *for*, *like*, *how*, *back*.

**Step three: the three-sentence stop.** Play the first three sentences only of I to one beginner, and of O
to a different beginner. Stop and ask:

> What is this thing for? And what's it like?

Then play the rest, and ask them to say back how they'd use it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an I that sneaks the purpose in** at sentence two. Keep
the purpose out of the first three sentences completely.

The second is **a listener who knows the thing**. Their answer tells you nothing. Choose a real beginner.

The third, in real speech, is **the proper word used as if it explained itself**: *it's a fixed deposit*.
To a beginner, the name is one more thing to explain.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Banks, hospitals, offices and phone shops are full of explanations that start inside. People nod, go home,
and ask their children. The person who starts with *it's for …* is the one they come back to.

Under pressure, explaining something you do every day, you start inside because that's how you think
about it. Before you speak, say move 1 to yourself.

A beginner who can say what a thing is for after three sentences will usually stay with you for the rest.
