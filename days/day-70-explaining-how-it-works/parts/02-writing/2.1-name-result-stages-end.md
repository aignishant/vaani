## One-line answer

A written explanation of a process has a title that names it, a first sentence saying what the process is
for and what you have at the end, the stages in order with a signpost word or a number each, and a last
line saying how you know it's finished. Short sentences, plain words, and the passive where the doer
doesn't matter.

## The story

A new colleague joins your office and asks how to take a day off. Everybody tells her something
different. So you write it down for her, and for the next new person:

> **How leave is approved**
>
> This is how you get a day off approved, so that your pay isn't cut and your work is covered.
>
> 1. First, a leave form is filled in. You can get it from the front desk.
> 2. Next, the form is signed by your team leader, who checks that someone can cover your work.
> 3. Then it's handed to the office manager, at least three days before the leave.
> 4. Finally, the manager enters it in the leave book and gives you back a copy.
>
> When you have the signed copy, your leave is approved. Keep it until your next pay slip.

She takes her first day off without asking anyone. The next new person gets the same page.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **process**: a set of stages that happen in order to make something or get something done.
- **result**: here, what you have at the end of the process.
- **end state**: how things are when the process is finished, so you know it's done.
- **passive**: *be* + past participle, for when the thing matters more than the doer, from
  [day 69](../../../day-69-describing-a-process/parts/02-writing/2.1-be-plus-the-third-form.md).

On [day 32](../../../day-32-giving-instructions/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-instructions-somebody-can-follow.md)
you wrote instructions: commands for somebody to follow. An explanation of a process is wider.
It tells a reader how something works or is done, including stages other people do, so they understand
the whole thing.

| Part | What it says | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| title | names the process | *How leave is approved* |
| first sentence | what it's for, and the result | *This is how you get a day off approved, so …* |
| stages | in order, one per line or short paragraph | *First, a leave form is filled in.* |
| end | how you know it's finished | *When you have the signed copy, your leave is approved.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the writing version of what phase 4 has been building: explaining clearly to somebody who wasn't
there. In phase 5 you'll write instructions, rules and technical explanations at work. They all follow
this shape.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Technical writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_writing). It says that technical writing is
"used by industrial and scientific organizations to clearly and accurately convey complex information",
and that:

> To be effective, the expert and 'layman' reader must be equally capable of understanding the same
> procedures.
> Modern procedural technical writing relies on simple terms and short sentences rather than detailed
> explanations with unnecessary information.

A *layman* is someone who isn't an expert: your new colleague.

**The title names the process.** *How leave is approved.* · *How the water bill is shared.* The passive
often fits a title, because the process matters more than the people.

**The first sentence says what it's for and what you get.** This is the sentence people skip, and it's the
one a newcomer needs most.

**One stage per line, in order**, with a number or a signpost word: *First*, *Next*, *Then*, *Finally*.

**Passive for the stages where the doer doesn't matter; *you* for the stages the reader does.** *The form
is signed by your team leader.* · *You can get it from the front desk.*

**The end tells them it's finished.** How will the reader know? A copy in their hand, a message, a
stamp.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the stages with no first sentence**: the reader starts at step 1 with no
idea what the process is for.

The second is **the long sentence stage**: one stage that runs to four lines with three *and*s. The reader
can't tell where it ends. One stage, one or two short sentences.

The third is **no end**: the steps stop, and the reader doesn't know whether they're done or whether
something else is coming.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Notices about how things work in a building, pages for new staff, and explanations on official websites
are all written this way. The good ones fit on one page and can be followed by someone on their first day.

Under pressure, writing for a newcomer who's waiting, people write the stages and skip the first sentence
and the end. Those two sentences are what turn a list into an explanation.

A newcomer who follows your page without asking anyone has been given a real explanation. That's the test
in the next part.
