## One-line answer

Write a real one-page explanation of a process that newcomers find confusing, for a real newcomer. Use a
title, a first sentence with the purpose and result, numbered stages and an end line. Then give it to
someone who doesn't know the process and ask three questions: *what's it for, what's stage two, and how do
you know it's finished?* Three right answers mean the page works.

## The story

New families keep moving into your building, and every one of them asks how the shared water bill works.
You write a page for the notice board:

> **How the water bill is shared**
>
> The building gets one water bill every two months. This is how it's divided, so every flat pays its
> fair share and the supply isn't cut.
>
> 1. The bill is received by the secretary and put up on this board.
> 2. The total is divided by the number of people living in each flat, not by flat size.
> 3. Each flat's share is written on the list below the bill.
> 4. You pay your share to the treasurer, in cash or by phone, by the 10th.
> 5. Your name is ticked on the list when your payment is received.
>
> When your name is ticked, you've paid. If it isn't ticked by the 12th, please speak to the treasurer.

A new family reads it. The next day, the father says to you: *So it's per person, we pay by the 10th, and
we check for our tick.* All three questions answered, and nobody told him anything.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **title**, **first sentence**, **stages**, **end line**: the four parts of a written process, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-name-result-stages-end.md).
- **newcomer**: somebody who has just arrived, who doesn't yet know how things work.
- **three-question test**: asking a reader what the process is for, what one stage is, and how they know
  it's finished.

The three questions test the three places an explanation usually fails: the purpose, the stages, and the
end.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Technical writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_writing): "the expert and 'layman' reader must
be equally capable of understanding the same procedures", and "Modern procedural technical writing relies
on simple terms and short sentences rather than detailed explanations with unnecessary information."

**Step one: choose a real process** that confuses newcomers where you live or work: a bill, a booking, a
form, a routine at work, how something is shared. Choose a real reader: a new neighbour, a new colleague, a
relative who has just moved.

**Step three: write the page**, one page, about one hundred and twenty to one hundred and fifty words.
Numbered stages, one or two short sentences each.

**Step four: the plain-words check.** Circle every word a newcomer might not know. Replace it or explain it
in the same sentence. Split every sentence longer than about twenty words.

**Step five: the passive check.** For each stage, ask: does the reader need to know who does it? If not,
the passive. If the reader does it, *you*.

**Step six: the three-question test.** Give it to someone who doesn't know the process. Ask:

> What is this for? What happens at stage two? How do you know it's finished?

Write down their answers.

**Step seven: fix anything they got wrong**, then put it up, send it, or give it to the newcomer it's for.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **writing for yourself**: the page uses your words for things, *the
treasurer's list*, *the per-head method*. The newcomer doesn't know them yet.

The second is **the missing stage you do without thinking**. You never wrote *Your name is ticked*, so
nobody knows how to check. The three-question test finds it.

The third is **the page that's two pages**. Newcomers don't read long notices. Cut detail they don't need
to do the process.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Notice boards, pages for new staff and messages to new tenants are all process explanations. The ones that
work are short, numbered, and tested on someone new before they go up.

Under pressure, a newcomer asking you in the corridor, people explain out loud instead. A page means you
explain once, and it keeps working without you.

A newcomer who answers the three questions from your page, without asking you anything, is the proof.
