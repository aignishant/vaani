## One-line answer

Make a copy of your real page with the first sentence and the end line removed, so it's only a title and
stages. Give it and your real page to two readers and ask *what is this for, and how do you know when
you've finished?* A process with no purpose and no end still looks complete. The reader just can't tell
why they're doing it or when to stop.

## The story

At a new job, you're given a printed page:

> **Stock check**
>
> 1. Take the red folder from the manager's desk.
> 2. Count the boxes on shelves A to F.
> 3. Write the numbers in the folder.
> 4. Compare with last week's page.

You do it all. Then you stand there with the folder, not sure what to do. Is it finished? Do you give it
back? Why did you compare with last week?

Your supervisor walks past and says: *Oh, if anything's down by more than five, tell me, so I can order
it. Then put the folder back.* That was the purpose and the end. Neither was on the page.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are not
ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **title**, **first sentence**, **stages**, **end line**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-name-result-stages-end.md).
- **three-question test**: what's it for, what's stage two, how do you know it's finished, from
  [part 2.2](2.2-explain-a-real-process-for-a-newcomer.md).
- **stranded**: left somewhere with no idea what to do next.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Stages without a purpose and an end are a list of actions. The reader can do them and still not know:

| Missing | What the reader can't do |
| --- | --- |
| the first sentence: what it's for | judge anything: *is five boxes down a problem?* |
| the end line: how you know it's finished | stop, hand over, or check it worked |

## Why Vaani needs it

In phase 5 you'll write explanations and instructions that people follow at work without you. A page with
no purpose and no end leaves them stranded, and they'll come and ask you, which is what the page was
meant to prevent.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Technical writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_writing): its aim is "to clearly and
accurately convey complex information", and "the expert and 'layman' reader must be equally capable of
understanding the same procedures".

**Step one: take your real page** from [part 2.2](2.2-explain-a-real-process-for-a-newcomer.md). Keep it
safe.

**Step two: make copy L**, deliberately a list: keep the title and the stages, and remove the first
sentence and the end line. Label it *deliberate*.

**Step three: the purpose-and-end test.** Give copy L to one reader and your real page to a different
reader. Neither should know the process. Ask each:

> What is this for?
> How do you know when you've finished?

**Step four: fill the table.**

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the reader of your real page can't say what
it's for or how they'd know it's finished. Then your first sentence or end line isn't doing its job. It is
**GREEN** when your real page's reader answers both, and copy L's reader has to guess at least one.

**Step five: the repair.** If your real page failed, rewrite the first sentence to say *so that …* and the
end line to name something the reader can see or hold. Test it on a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a title that gives the purpose away**: *How to get your
pay on time*. Then copy L still has its purpose. Use a plain title for both copies, like *Stock check*.

The second is **a reader who knows the process**. They'll answer from memory. Choose someone who doesn't.

The third, in real writing, is **the end line that isn't an end**: *Then you're done.* Done how? Name the
sign: *when you get the copy*, *when your name is ticked*, *when the folder is back on the desk*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows in
it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Printed procedures at work, notices in buildings and pages on official websites often have the stages and
nothing else. People follow them and then ask somebody. Two extra sentences would have saved the question.

Under pressure, writing a quick page for a new colleague, the stages feel like the whole job. The purpose
and the end are what make it an explanation.

A reader who can say what the page is for and how they'll know they're finished can use it alone, which
is what a written explanation is for.
