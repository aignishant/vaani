# Cutting back, not replanting

`CF-17` · Day 70

## The one-line answer

What survives after Day 72 is only ever the thing small enough that you would still do it in your
worst week — so today you cut the routine down until it fits that, and then say it out loud so it
exists somewhere other than in your intentions.

## The model

A garden that has been worked on for a season does not need planting again. It needs somebody to
come out every so often and cut things back before they go over. That is a completely different
job, it is much smaller, and it is the one people get wrong — they either do nothing until the
whole thing has gone to seed, or they decide to redesign the garden, which is enormous, so they
never start.

Both of those are the standard ways this ends after Day 72.

**The second curriculum.** You finish, you feel the momentum, and you sit down and design the next
seventy-two days. It is a good plan. It is bigger than the one you just finished, because you are
better now and you are ambitious about it. You will get a few weeks in and stop, and stopping this
time will be worse than never starting, because the first thing you will conclude is that you have
lost it.

**The slow one.** You do nothing at all, and nothing appears to happen. That is the trap: the decay
is far too slow to notice. Nothing goes wrong on the first weekend, or the second. Then a couple of
months on there is a call that goes badly and you cannot work out why, and the honest answer is
that you have not spoken English out loud since the gate.

So: cutting back. Four things need keeping, and each has a full version and a bad-week version. You
decide both today, out loud, and the bad-week version is the one that actually matters.

**One — the recorder.** One recorded free rep, unscripted, kept. This is the whole routine and the
other three are optional beside it. Without a recording you cannot hear drift, and drift is the
only thing you are guarding against. Full version: a real free rep on a real subject, played back
once with a task. Bad-week version: talk into the recorder about nothing much, keep it, do not play
it back. The unlistened recording still counts, because it keeps the habit alive and you can listen
to it later.

**Two — the ledger.** `docs/ERRORS.md` stays open and stays append-only. When you do play a
recording back, you listen with a task and add rows. And every so often — a season, not a week —
you re-run Day 67 on it: read the whole thing out, sort it into piles, pick four, name them out
loud. That is why Day 67 is a procedure and not a list. The list expires; the procedure does not.

**Three — the mouth.** On the days you are not recording, something out loud, cheap, with no setup.
Shadow a clip. Run one of your old **Say it now** sets. Say the phrases from one old day while you
are doing something else. Bad-week version: one set of lines, once, out loud, wherever you happen
to be. The point is to keep your mouth in the shapes, not to learn anything.

**Four — a person.** One live conversation in English with an actual human being, at whatever
cadence you can hold. This is the one that decays fastest and the one you cannot fake with a
recorder, because nothing you do alone reproduces somebody talking back before you were ready. It
is also the hardest to keep, because it needs somebody else to say yes. So make it small enough
that saying yes is easy.

**The bad-week test.** Take each of the four and picture the worst week you have had this year —
the one where everything was late and you were ill and something went wrong at home. Would you have
done it? If the answer is no, it is too big, and you cut it until the answer is yes. Then that
smaller thing is your routine, and the fuller version is what you do when you happen to have room.

This feels like giving up on the good version. It is the opposite. A routine you keep in bad weeks
is a routine, and a routine you keep only in good weeks is a hobby that goes over in the autumn.

**Hang it on something that already happens.** Not a day of the week — a thing. After the shopping.
On the walk back from somewhere you always walk back from. The evening the bins go out. A day of
the week is a decision you have to remember to make; a thing that already happens carries the
routine along with it and needs no memory at all.

**And when it lapses.** Nothing happens. Exactly as in the course — no streaks, no counter, and no
starting again from the beginning. You missed some weeks and the next recording is still the next
recording. The only failure mode worth naming is the one where a lapse turns into a story about
yourself, because that story is much harder to get out of than the lapse was.

**Today's phrases** — the lines for keeping this going in front of other people, which is where it
actually gets kept. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I'm keeping it up, just less of it. | Answering somebody who asks whether you're still at it |
| Once a week, that's all it is. | Describing the routine without overselling it |
| Can I practise on you for a bit? | Asking a person for the live conversation |
| Nothing formal, just a chat in English. | Setting the bar low enough that people say yes |
| Give me a topic, any topic. | Getting a prompt off somebody so you don't pick a safe one |
| I've let it slide a bit — starting again this week. | Coming back after a lapse without a speech |
| I still do the recordings, yeah. | Saying the habit out loud so it stays real |
| Same again next week? | Booking the next one before this one has ended |

## Say it now

Use your own routine — the four things, as you have just cut them. Say each line twice, once as you
would to a friend and once as you would to somebody you do not know well.

1. *I'm keeping it up, just less of it — {{the one thing you're definitely keeping}}.*
2. *Once a week, that's all it is. {{when in the week it hangs off}}.*
3. *Can I practise on you for a bit? {{how often you'd want to}}.*
4. *Nothing formal, just a chat in English — {{what you'd talk about}}.*
5. *Give me a topic, any topic.*
6. *I've let it slide a bit — starting again this week.*
7. *I still do the recordings, yeah. {{what you did on the last one}}.*
8. *Same again next week?*

Then run lines 3, 4, 5 and 8 as one continuous run, twice, as though you were asking somebody
across a table. That run is the entire ask, start to finish, and it needs to be sayable without a
build-up — because the moment where you could ask somebody is always short, and a request that
needs a preamble does not get made.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d70-free.m4a`.**

> say the full version, the bad-week version, and the thing in your week it hangs off. Then say
> what you will do the first time you notice you have lapsed, and when the next one is.

- **Solo form:** as written, said to the recorder as if to a person who will remember what you told
  them. Say the dates and cadences out loud rather than thinking them — a routine you have only
  thought about has never once survived.
- **With a partner:** tell it to somebody real, and ask them for one thing only: to ask you about it
  next time you see them. That single question, from somebody who will actually ask it, is worth
  more than any plan you write down tonight.

## The deliberate failure

Now design the big version out loud, and commit to it. A recording every day. A new set of phrases
every day. Somebody to talk to every day. A ledger re-read every night. Say the whole thing as
though you meant it, with all the specifics.

Then, straight afterwards and on the same tape, answer one question: when in the last year did you
actually do something at that cadence, every day, without a course telling you to?

**What breaking sounds like:** the two halves do not sound like the same speaker. The plan comes out
fluent and fast and certain — ambition is easy to say, and you have said versions of it before.
Then the question lands and you slow right down, hedge, qualify, go quiet, or start explaining why
this time is different. That change of gear, on one tape, is the whole diagnosis, and it is not
about willpower. It is evidence, in your own voice, about what you actually sustain.

Then say the bad-week version again after it. It will sound almost embarrassingly small. Small is
the specification, not a compromise on it — and it is the version that will still be running when
the big plan has gone over.

## When it breaks in the wild

A couple of months after Day 72, somebody asks how the English is going. You realise you have not
recorded anything since the gate and you cannot remember the last full conversation you had in
English. The honest answer is uncomfortable, so you say *yeah, good, still at it* — and now you
have said it out loud, restarting means admitting you were not, so you quietly do not restart. The
lie is much more expensive than the lapse.

**The repair:**

> I've let it slide a bit — starting again this week.

Then record something that same day, whatever it sounds like. Restarting is one recording, not a
plan, and the recording does not have to be any good — it has to exist and be dated. That is the
only thing that ever gets the routine moving again, and a bad one does it just as well as a good
one.
