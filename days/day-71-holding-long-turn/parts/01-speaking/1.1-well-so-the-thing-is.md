## One-line answer

To hold a long turn, open with a small word like *well* or *so*, bring in your main point with *the
thing is*, and keep your voice from dropping to the bottom until your very last sentence. A voice
that falls all the way tells the listener *I've finished*, and they'll start talking.

## The story

At a family lunch, your aunt asks why you changed jobs. You answer:

> It was far. ↘ (silence)

Your cousin jumps straight in with a story about his own office. You never get to say the real
reason.

Next Sunday somebody asks again. This time you say:

> Well … it's a long story. → So, first, the travel was two buses each way. → And the thing is, →
> my mother wasn't well, and I needed to be home by six. ↘

Nobody jumps in. The small words and a voice that stays up tell the table *there's more coming*. The
last sentence falls, and your aunt knows it's her turn.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **turn**: your time to speak in a conversation, from
  [day 8](../../../day-08-greetings-and-goodbyes/parts/01-speaking/1.1-hello-and-how-are-you.md).
- **long turn**: a turn of several sentences, where you keep speaking and the listener waits.
- **hold the turn**: keep speaking without the other person taking over.
- **falling**: the voice going down at the end, from
  [day 24](../../../day-24-voice-up-and-down/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md).
  There you learned that a fall at the end means *I have finished*.
- **fall-rise**: the voice going down and then a little up again, from
  [day 65](../../../day-65-polite-intonation/parts/01-speaking/1.1-start-high-let-it-fall.md).
- **filler**: a sound like *um* or *er* that fills a silence and means nothing, from
  [day 57](../../../day-57-pausing-right-place/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-lengths-of-silence.md).
- **marker**: a small word or phrase that tells the listener where you are in your turn. It doesn't
  change the facts in your sentence.

A listener is always asking one question: *has this person finished?* They listen for the answer in
two places, the small words and the tune of your voice.

| What you do | What the listener hears |
| --- | --- |
| *Well …* or *So …* at the start | *they're starting something longer* |
| a sentence ending that stays level or dips and comes back up | *more is coming* |
| *the thing is,* | *here's the main point, or the problem* |
| a sentence ending that falls to the bottom | *they've finished; my turn* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 4 gate is a recorded opinion with three reasons and one counter-argument answered. On a

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Taking turns](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/taking-turns) gives
"So …" and "Well …" as ways of "Avoiding silence and gaining thinking time", and "What I mean is …"
and "So, in other words …" for "Making your turn longer". It also says: "In English-speaking cultures
we avoid long silences or pauses."
[Discourse marker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_marker) defines one as "a word or a phrase
that plays a role in managing the flow and structure of discourse", which "usually do not change the
truth conditional meaning of the sentence". It says speakers use them "to claim conversational
turns", with *so* as its example.
[the thing is](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/the_thing_is): "Used to shift focus to an overriding
point or impediment", with "I'd like to come, but the thing is, I just can't afford it."
[Intonation and Tones](https://usefulenglish.ru/phonetics/intonation-and-tones): "The falling tone
expresses finality, completeness, confidence", and "The fall-rise expresses nonfinality, signals
continuation of the utterance."

**Three markers, three jobs.**

| Marker | Where | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Well …* | the very start | *the answer isn't short or simple, and I'm starting now* |
| *So …* | the start, or moving to the next piece | *here comes the next part* |
| *the thing is,* | before the main point | *this is the part that matters* |

**The tune inside a long turn.** Every sentence except the last ends level, or dips and comes back up
a little: the fall-rise. Only the last sentence falls all the way down.

```text
   Well …      it's a long story. →     So, the travel was two buses. ↘↗
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   And the thing is, →   I needed to be home by six. ↘
                                                     (all the way down: finished)
```

**Say the markers lightly.** They're small words. Say them quickly and quietly, then leave a very short
silence after *well* and after *the thing is,*. The comma in the dictionary example is that silence.

**Silence, not *um*.** If you need to think in the middle, say a marker and then stay silent for a
moment. *So … (silence) the second thing was …* sounds like choosing. A string of *um, um, er* sounds
lost, and a lost speaker gets interrupted.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **every sentence falling to the bottom**:

> It was far. ↘ The bus was slow. ↘ My mother was ill. ↘

Each fall tells the listener *finished*. After the first one, somebody takes the turn.

The second is **the marker used as a filler**: *so so so, well, so, basically*. One marker at each
new piece is a signpost. Five in a row is noise.

The third is **the thing is with no thing after it**: *the thing is … the thing is …* and then a
fall. Say the main point straight after it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers do this all the time without thinking. *Well* at the start of an answer often
warns you that the answer isn't a simple yes. *The thing is* often comes just before the reason they
can't do something.

Under pressure, in a busy room or with a fast speaker, your voice drops at every full stop, because
that's what a nervous voice does. That's when you lose the turn.

What a native listener notices is not the markers. It's the tune. A voice that stays up says *wait*,
and most people wait.
