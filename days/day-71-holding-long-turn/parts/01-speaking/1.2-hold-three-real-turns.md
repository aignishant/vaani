## One-line answer

Answer three real questions from your own life in turns of four to six sentences. Say each one to a
real person who has been told to jump in the moment they think you've finished. A turn is held when
they never find a gap before your last sentence.

## The story

After dinner at your uncle's house, the family is sitting together. Your grandmother asks:

> So, how's the new flat?

You've got a lot to say. There's the landlord, the water problem, the lovely neighbour. But every
time you stop for breath, your brother says *oh, that reminds me* and he's off.

Your sister does something different when it's her turn. She says *Well, it's been a mixed week*, and
then *so, first* and *and the thing is,* with her voice staying up. Your brother opens his mouth
twice and shuts it twice. She finishes, her voice falls, and he starts.

She wasn't louder than you, and she wasn't faster. Her voice just never said *finished* until she had.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **turn**, **long turn**, **hold the turn**, **marker**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-well-so-the-thing-is.md).
- **jumper**: in this part, the person you ask to jump in the moment they hear a gap.
- **gap**: a place in your turn where the listener thinks you've finished.
- **turn map**: four short lines on paper showing the pieces of your turn and the marker for each.

This part is practice with a real person trying to take your turn. A listener who is being polite
will wait whatever your voice does. A jumper won't, so a jumper tells you where your gaps are.

| Piece of the turn | Marker | Voice at the end of it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the opening | *Well …* | level |
| the first piece | *So, first …* | level or fall-rise |
| the main point | *And the thing is,* | level or fall-rise |
| the last sentence | none, or *so* | falls all the way down |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 4 gate asks for three reasons and a counter-argument in one recorded turn. The recording
won't interrupt you, but the habit you build today is what stops a real person interrupting you.
Every longer turn in this phase, and every interview answer in phase 5, needs a voice that doesn't
hand over too early.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Taking turns](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/taking-turns). Among

"gaining thinking time", "So …" and "Well …".

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **reading the map**. Your eyes go to the paper, your voice goes flat,
and flat voices fall at every full stop. Look at the map, look up, then speak.

The second is **a jumper who is too kind**. They wait whatever you do, so your table says *no gaps*
when there were gaps. Ask them again to really jump in.

The third is **speeding up to beat the jumper**. You rush, and the listener stops understanding.
Holding a turn is about the tune, not speed.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

In a family or among friends, several people want the turn, and nobody waits for a long silence.
The person who keeps it is usually the one whose voice doesn't fall until they've finished.

Under pressure, when somebody jumps in anyway, fluent speakers often say *just a sec, and the thing
is …* and carry on. That's a small, friendly way to hold on.

A native listener rarely notices that you're holding your turn well. They notice when you aren't:
they think you've finished and are surprised when you start again.
