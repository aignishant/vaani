## One-line answer

Record one real turn twice. In the first, deliberately let every sentence fall to the bottom, with no
markers. In the second, use the markers and hold your voice up. Play both to two listeners and ask
each one to say *stop* the moment they think you've finished. The dropped version gets stopped
early. The held version should reach the end.

## The story

At a cousin's engagement, an older relative asks why you're not getting married yet. You have a real
answer, in three parts. You start:

> I'm studying. ↘

Silence. He says *studying is fine, but at your age …* and talks for the rest of the evening. Your
three parts stay in your head.

Later, driving home, you say the whole answer to the windscreen:

> Well … there are three things. → So, first, I'm studying till next June. → And the thing is, → I
> want a job before anything else. ↘

It's the same answer. It just has markers and a voice that stays up. This time nobody could have
jumped in.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **dropped version**: here, a turn where every sentence falls to the bottom and there are no
  markers.
- **held version**: the same turn with markers and the voice kept up until the last sentence.
- **stop test**: a listener says *stop* the moment they think you've finished.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The words in both versions are almost the same. Only two things change: the small markers, and
whether the voice falls to the bottom before the end.

| Version | Markers | Voice before the last sentence | What the stop test should show |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D, dropped (deliberate) | none | falls every time | stopped after sentence 1 or 2 |
| H, held | *well*, *so*, *the thing is* | level or fall-rise | reaches the end |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the check that proves the rule is real for your voice. In phase 5 you'll answer interview
questions and speak in meetings. People there won't wait politely for a fourth sentence if your
voice said *finished* after the first.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Intonation and Tones](https://usefulenglish.ru/phonetics/intonation-and-tones): "The falling tone
expresses finality, completeness, confidence", and "The fall-rise expresses nonfinality, signals
continuation of the utterance."

**Step one: choose one real turn** of four or five sentences, from
[part 1.2](1.2-hold-three-real-turns.md) or a new question.

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **D**, deliberately dropped: no markers, and every sentence falls all the way down.
2. **H**, held: *well* or *so* to open, *the thing is,* before the main point, and the voice falling
   fully only on the last sentence.

**Step three: the stop test.** Play D to one listener and H to a different one. Tell each: *Say
"stop" the moment you think I've finished.* Write down after which sentence they said it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a D that isn't dropped enough**. You know it's the
wrong version, so you keep your voice up by habit. Let each sentence really fall, like the end of a
conversation.

The second is **using the same listener for both**. After D, they know how long the turn is and
they wait for H. Use two listeners.

The third, in real speech, is **the held voice that turns into a question**. The voice goes so high
that every sentence sounds like *right?* and the listener starts answering. Level is enough. You
don't need to rise.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Many people lose turns they'd have kept, not because they said less but because their voice said
*done* too early. The listener isn't being rude. They're answering the tune they heard.

Under pressure, speaking to someone older or more important, your voice drops after every sentence
to sound respectful. That can hand them the turn before you've given your reason. Hold the voice
until the reason is out, then let it fall.

A listener who waits for your last sentence without being asked has heard a speaker in control of
their turn.
