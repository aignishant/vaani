## One-line answer

To write about something that isn't true now, or probably won't happen, use *if* + a past verb, then
*would* + the base form: *If I had a car, I would drive to work.* The past verb here doesn't mean
the past. It means *not real*. For advice, the fixed form is *If I were you, I would …*

## The story

Your neighbour slips a note under your door:

> The family upstairs play loud music every night. I don't want a fight. What would you do?

You write back:

> If I were you, I'd knock on their door on a quiet afternoon, not at night. If I went at midnight, I'd be too angry to be polite.

Your neighbour reads it and knows two things at once. You're not them, and you haven't done it.
You're imagining. The past verbs, *were* and *went*, told them that without a single extra word.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **condition**: the thing that has to happen first, the *if* part, from
  [day 67](../../../day-67-suggestions-and-choosing/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-now-will-later.md).
- **first conditional**: an *if* sentence about a real, possible future: *If it rains, we'll stay
  in*, from [day 67](../../../day-67-suggestions-and-choosing/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-now-will-later.md).
- **past simple**: the past form of a verb, such as *walked*, *went*, *had*, from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/02-writing/2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **base form**: the plain verb with nothing added: *go*, *buy*, *be*, from
  [day 42](../../../day-42-three-sounds-of-ed/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-verbs-that-refuse-ed.md).
- **imaginary**: not real, only in your head.
- ***would***: a helper word that goes before a base form to show an imagined result: *I would go*.
  Short form *'d*: *I'd go*.
- **second conditional**: an *if* sentence about something imaginary now, or unlikely in the future.

Compare the two conditionals. They look alike, and they tell the reader different things.

| Sentence | Type | What the reader understands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *If I get the job, I'll move.* | first | *it might really happen* |
| *If I got the job, I'd move.* | second | *it probably won't happen, or I'm only imagining* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Tomorrow's writing is a wish paragraph, which lives in imagined things. Advice on paper, *if I were
you*, is one of the most common written sentences in replies to friends. In phase 5 you'll soften
formal requests with it: *I would be grateful if you could …*

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Conditionals: zero, first and second](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/conditionals-zero-first-second).
It says: "The second conditional is used to imagine present or future situations that are impossible
or unlikely in reality", and "The structure is usually: if + past simple >> + would + infinitive."
Its examples include:

> If we had a garden, we could have a cat.
> I wouldn't worry if I were you.

On *were*, it says: "When if is followed by the verb be, it is grammatically correct to say if I
were, if he were, if she were and if it were. However, it is also common to hear these structures
with was, especially in the he/she form." In your writing, use *were*. It's always correct.

**The shape.**

```text
   If  +  past simple ,     would + base form
   If  I had more time,     I would learn to swim.
   I would learn to swim    if I had more time.      ← no comma
```

The comma rule is the same one you used for the first conditional on day 67: a comma after the *if*
clause when it comes first, and no comma when it comes second.

**Irregular verbs keep their past form.** *If I knew*, *If she had*, *If we went*. They're the same
past forms you learned on day 42.

**The advice form.** *If I were you, I'd …* is fixed. It always uses *were*, even with *I*.

**Negatives.** *didn't* in the *if* clause, *wouldn't* in the result:

> If I didn't live so far away, I'd visit more often.

**Could in place of would** means *would be able to*: *If we had a garden, we could have a cat.*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***would* in both halves**:

> If I would have a car, I would drive to work.

The *if* clause takes the past simple, not *would*. Fix: *If I had a car, …*

The second is **a first-conditional half mixed with a second-conditional half**:

> If I won the lottery, I will buy a house.

A reader can't tell whether you think it's likely. Choose one: *If I win, I'll* (possible) or *If I
won, I'd* (imaginary).

The third is ***If I was you*** in writing. You'll hear it in speech. The page shows *were* as the
correct form, so in writing, *were*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

In friendly writing, messages and notes, people use the short form *I'd* almost every time. In formal
writing, *I would* stays in full.

Under pressure, writing quickly, people put *would* in the *if* clause because it feels like the
right word for imagining. A reader can usually still understand, but it looks wrong on the page, and
on a formal page it's noticed.

A careful reader notices one thing above all: whether your verbs match your meaning. *If I win* and
*If I won* tell them how hopeful you are.
