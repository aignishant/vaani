## One-line answer

Write a short reply to a real problem someone has told you about. Give two pieces of advice with *If I
were you, I'd …*, and one imagined result with *if* + past + *would*. Then check every *if* clause for
a past verb and every result for *would*.

## The story

The neighbour two doors down stops you at the gate. Her son wants to leave college and work in a
shop. She doesn't know what to say to him. That evening she sends you a message:

> You know him. What should I do?

You write:

> If I were you, I'd ask him what he wants in five years, not now. If he had a plan, I'd listen to it. And if I were him, I'd want my mother to ask before she said no.

She reads it twice and forwards it to her husband. It doesn't tell her what to do. It shows her what
you'd do in her place, and that's easier to accept.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **second conditional**, ***would***, **imaginary**: from [part 2.1](2.1-if-past-would.md).
- **advice**: what you think somebody should do, from
  [day 65](../../../day-65-polite-intonation/parts/02-writing/2.1-advice-rule-no-choice-no-need.md).
- **reply**: a message you write back.
- **in their place**: imagining you were them.

*If I were you, I'd …* is gentler than *You should …* on paper. It doesn't tell the reader what to do.
It tells them what you would do, and leaves the choice with them.

| Written advice | How it reads |
| --- | --- |
| *You should talk to him.* | direct: *do this* |
| *If I were you, I'd talk to him.* | softer: *this is what I'd do* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Replying to a real person with a real problem is where this grammar is used most. Tomorrow's wish
paragraph and later pieces in this phase ask you to imagine on paper, and the gate asks you to argue
one side and then the other. Imagining clearly on the page starts here.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **advice that isn't advice**:

> If I were you, I'd be happy.

It has the right grammar and tells the reader nothing to do. Advice names an action: *I'd ask him*,
*I'd wait a week*.

The second is **every sentence starting *If I were you***. Three in a row sounds like a form letter.
Use it once or twice, then vary: *I'd also …*, *If he had …*.

The third is **a present verb creeping into the *if* clause**: *If I am you*. That's not a sentence
English uses. *Were*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Most real replies to a friend's problem mix one *If I were you* with plain sentences. Fluent writers
use the second conditional for the one line they want to be gentle, not for every line.

Under pressure, replying fast to someone upset, people write *You should* three times. It can read
like an order. One *If I were you* softens the whole message.

What the reader notices is whether your advice respects that the choice is theirs. The second
conditional says it for you.
