## One-line answer

Make a copy of your reply with three deliberate conditional mistakes: *would* in the *if* clause, a
first-conditional result on a second-conditional *if*, and *If I am you*. Give both copies to two
readers and ask how likely each thing is and whether it sounds right. A reader stops at the broken
copy, and your fixed copy reads cleanly.

## The story

A neighbour in your building asks you to help her write a note to the building secretary about the
broken lift. Her draft says:

> If the lift would work, my mother will come to visit. If I am the secretary, I would fix it this week.

The secretary writes back: *Is your mother coming or not?* The note was meant to say *she can't come
because the lift is broken*. It said something closer to *she will come, perhaps*.

You rewrite it:

> If the lift worked, my mother would visit. If I were the secretary, I'd fix it this week.

Now the imagined things read as imagined, and the secretary understands the problem.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly write. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **second conditional**, ***would***: from [part 2.1](2.1-if-past-would.md).
- **broken copy**: a copy of your page with mistakes put in on purpose, labelled *deliberate*.
- **likelihood question**: asking a reader *does the writer think this will really happen?*
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The three deliberate mistakes each change what a reader thinks, or make them stop:

| Deliberate mistake | Example | What a reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *would* in the *if* clause | *If the lift would work, …* | a sentence that looks wrong; they stop |
| mixed conditional | *If I won, I will …* | unsure whether you think it will happen |
| present *be* in the advice form | *If I am you, …* | a sentence English doesn't use |

## Why Vaani needs it

A reader can't ask you what you meant. The phase 4 gate asks for an argument on the page, and phase
5 asks for formal letters where *if* sentences carry requests and conditions. If your conditionals
read wrong, the reader decides what you meant for you.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who is too polite**. They say *it's fine*
to both. Ask them to point to the sentence they'd change if they had to change one.

The second is **fixing copy B in your head** as you make it. Write it wrong exactly, or the test
means nothing.

The third, in real writing, is ***would have*** where a simple past belongs: *If I would have time, I
would help.* Many learners write this because *would* feels like the imagining word. On the page, the
imagining is done by the past verb: *If I had time*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Most readers forgive one slip in a friendly message. They're less forgiving in a letter to an office,
where *If you would send* sounds like you're unsure what you're asking.

Under pressure, writing a complaint when you're angry, conditionals go wrong first, because you're
thinking about the problem and not the verbs. Read your *if* sentences once more before you send.

A reader who never stops at your *if* sentences has been given the right verbs. They only notice
conditionals when they're wrong.
