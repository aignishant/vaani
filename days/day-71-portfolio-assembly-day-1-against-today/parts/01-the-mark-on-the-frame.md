# The mark on the frame

`CF-18` · Day 71

## The one-line answer

You record the Day 1 prompt again — the same words, cold, before you listen to anything — and then
you play the two takes back to back and say what is different in counted words, because a feeling
about your own progress is the one measurement you cannot trust.

## The model

A child's height gets marked in pencil on a doorframe, and the reason it goes on the frame rather
than in somebody's memory is that nobody in the house can see it happening. They see the child
every day. The change arrives at exactly the speed they get used to it.

Your English is the same, and worse than the same, because you get used to your own faults as well.
Today sounds like normal. Last week sounded like normal too. That is why Day 1 told you to keep the
baseline and never delete it, and why Day 18 stood you against it for the first time — a mark on
the frame is worth nothing on its own and everything beside an older one.

**The order is the method, and getting it wrong wastes the day.** Record first. Then listen to Day
1. Then listen to what you just recorded. Not any other way round.

If you play the baseline first you will not be able to help yourself — you will speak against it.
You will slow down, choose better words, tidy your sentences, and what you will have recorded is
your care rather than your English. Careful speech is slower and less fluent than ordinary speech,
so the tape would show you going backwards on the exact thing this course trains. Record cold, in
the dark, and the comparison stays honest.

**The same prompt, and the same conditions as the first time.** One take. No notes, no plan,
nothing written down, no run at it beforehand. Whatever place arrives when you hear the prompt is
the place — the same one as Day 1 or a completely different one, it makes no difference. The prompt
is the constant here, not the subject.

**Then the listening, and there are four things to count.** You count all four on both tapes, so
you finish with eight numbers.

**One — freezes.** The Day 18 definition and no other: a silence where you had stopped speaking and
nothing was holding the turn. Not a pause you put there on purpose — Day 45 spent a whole day
separating those two, and a deliberate pause is not a freeze no matter how long it runs.

**Two — the longest run.** How many sentences go past in a row without you abandoning one and
starting it again. Count them on your fingers, take the longest run on each tape, write both
numbers down. This is usually the biggest difference between the two tapes, and it is the one
nobody can hear from the inside.

**Three — chunks that arrived on their own.** Phrases out of your own `docs/CHUNKS.md` that turned
up in the take without you deciding to use them. Count them on both tapes. On the baseline the
number is probably nought, because on Day 1 you did not have any — that is precisely why the count
is worth taking.

**Four — endings.** Sentences that landed and stopped, against sentences that trailed off and faded
into the next one. Day 66 taught you to hear a fade. Same ear, both tapes.

Eight numbers. They go on paper, and they go into the `PROGRESS.md` row tonight, and no adjective
is allowed anywhere near them.

**Two warnings, and they are both about the flinch.** The baseline will be worse than you remember
— nearly everybody's is, and somewhere in it there will be a moment where you want to stop the
playback. Don't. And today's take will be worse than you hoped, because you are listening with an
ear you did not have in week one and you can now hear faults that were always there and always
inaudible to you. Both flinches point the same way and they cancel out. The gap between the tapes
is the measurement. Neither tape on its own is.

**And one thing that has not changed, on purpose.** Your voice. Your accent. Day 65 settled that
and it stays settled: you were never trying to arrive as a different-sounding person, and if you go
hunting for one on today's tape you will hear a failure that is not there. Something being the same
across both tapes is evidence that you measured the right thing.

**Then the assembly, which is bookkeeping and needs no talent at all.** Fourteen recordings exist by
tonight. Walk `docs/RECORDINGS.md` from row 01 downwards, confirm each file is where its row says
it is, and fill in the blank cells. Where a file is missing, the note says it is missing and names
the day to re-run — an index that quietly steps over a hole is worth less than one with the hole
written into it. Then row 14, tonight, with the counts.

That is the artifact. Not a feeling about improvement and not a certificate: a shelf of dated
recordings with a true index, opening on somebody who could not do this and closing tomorrow on
somebody who can.

**Today's phrases** — the lines for talking about your own English out loud, which you do more often
than you think and have been doing badly. All of these go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Have a listen to this one first. | Playing somebody the baseline before the new take |
| That's the same prompt, both times. | Saying what makes the two comparable, in one line |
| It's the pauses that changed, more than the words. | Naming what actually improved, precisely |
| I'd have stopped dead there a few months ago. | Pointing at one specific gain instead of a general one |
| I still do that — I just hear it now. | Owning an error that is still there, without apologising |
| No, that one stays in. It's the honest one. | Somebody suggests deleting a bad recording |
| It has, actually — thanks. | Taking a compliment about your English instead of deflecting it |
| I'm not finished. I'm just further on than that. | Saying where you are, with no boast and no apology |

## Say it now

Run this drill **after** both playbacks, with the two files and your eight numbers in front of you.
Say each line twice — once as you would to a friend, once as you would to somebody who does not
know you have been working on this.

1. *Have a listen to this one first — that's me at the start.*
2. *That's the same prompt, both times. {{the prompt, in your own words}}.*
3. *It's the pauses that changed, more than the words. {{your two freeze counts}}.*
4. *I'd have stopped dead there a few months ago.*
5. *I still do that — I just hear it now. {{the error that is on both tapes}}.*
6. *No, that one stays in. It's the honest one.*
7. *It has, actually — thanks.*
8. *I'm not finished. I'm just further on than that.*

Then run lines 1, 2, 3 and 8 as one continuous run, twice, as though somebody were sitting beside
you with both tapes queued up. That run is the whole of what you would say while they listen, and
it has to come out without a build-up — the moment when somebody is genuinely curious about this is
short, and an answer with a preamble on the front gets abandoned halfway through.

## The deliberate failure

Both playbacks done, both sets of numbers written. Now record a third take of the same prompt on
the end of the same file — and this time, try to beat what you have just heard. Aim at it properly.
Reach for the better words. Steer around the errors you have just caught yourself making.

**What breaking sounds like:** you can hear the watching. The third take comes out slower, with
gaps in front of nouns while the good word loads, and with sentences abandoned in the middle and
restarted in a tidier form. Your own chunks mostly vanish — they arrive when nobody is monitoring,
and you have just posted a monitor. It will be more correct than the second take and less fluent
than either, and that combination is the sound of somebody listening to themselves while they
speak.

Keep it. The third take is not your level; it is a performance of the second one. It is also the
best argument you will ever have for why the gate tomorrow has to be cold, and why one take is a
rule rather than a hardship.

## When it breaks in the wild

Somebody who has known you a long time says *your English has really come on, hasn't it.* And out
comes the habit: *no, no, it's still bad.* You mean it kindly. Agreeing feels like boasting.

But you have just told them, out loud, that a whole shelf of dated evidence does not count — and
having said it, you now have to keep believing it. Worse, they will not say it again, and an
outside ear is the only measurement in this whole project that you did not make yourself.

**The repair:**

> It has, actually — thanks. I've got a recording from the first day if you ever want to hear the
> difference.

Then stop, and let it sit there. No account of how you did it, no list of what is still wrong. The
long modest explanation is the same deflection with more words on it, and it is the version that
sounds least like somebody who has done the work.
