## One-line answer

A dream said out loud has three pieces: the dream with *If I could …, I'd …*, the reason why, and one
detail that makes it yours. Say *I'd*, with a clear *d*, not *I'll*. *I'd* tells the listener it's a
dream. *I'll* tells them it's a plan.

## The story

Late at night, at a tea stall near the station, a friend asks:

> If you could do anything, what would you do?

The first friend says *travel*, and the talk moves on. The second says:

> If I could, I'd open a small bakery. My grandmother baked every Sunday, and I miss that smell. I'd
> call it after her.

Everybody wants to know more: where, what kind, what name. A one-word dream closes the talk. A dream
with a reason and a detail opens it.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **dream**: here, something you'd love to do or have, but probably can't, or not yet.
- **wish**: wanting something to be different from how it is.
- **reason**: why something is so, from
  [day 56](../../../day-56-explaining-why/parts/01-speaking/1.1-reason-and-result.md). Here, why you
  want it.
- **contraction**: two words said as one short word, such as *I'm* or *don't*, from
  [day 35](../../../day-35-contractions-aloud/parts/01-speaking/1.1-two-words-one-beat.md).
- ***I'd***: the contraction of *I would*. It sounds like *eyed*.
- ***I'll***: the contraction of *I will*. It sounds like *aisle*.
- **homophone**: a word that sounds exactly like another word but means something different.

The three pieces of a dream said out loud:

| Piece | What it does | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the dream | says what you'd do | *If I could, I'd …* · *I'd love to …* |
| the reason | says why it matters to you | *because …* · *My grandmother …* |
| the detail | makes it yours, not anybody's | a name, a place, a colour, a person |

## Why Vaani needs it

Talking about dreams is one of the commonest friendly conversations, and one of the commonest
interview questions: *Where do you see yourself …?* It's also practice for the phase 4 gate, where a
view needs a reason and a real example. Today the view is a dream.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Conditionals: zero, first and second](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/conditionals-zero-first-second).
It says the second conditional "is used to imagine present or future situations that are impossible
or unlikely in reality", with examples including:

> If I won a lot of money, I'd buy a big house in the country.
> If we had a garden, we could have a cat.

**Open with the dream.** Three ways to start, from softest to biggest:

> I'd love to …
> If I could, I'd …
> If I had the money, I'd …

**Say *I'd* with a real *d*.** Two dictionary pages, both opened on 2026-09-23, carry a sound button
for each word. Listen, then say it with the recorder on, then play both back.
[I'd](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/I%27d) gives "/aɪd/" and says it is a homophone of "eyed": it
sounds exactly like *eyed*. [I'll](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/I%27ll) gives "/aɪ̯l/" and lists
*aisle* among the words that can sound the same. So *I'd* ends like *eyed*, and *I'll* ends like
*aisle*. The difference is one small sound at the end, and it carries the whole meaning.

| You say | The listener hears |
| --- | --- |
| *I'd open a bakery.* | *a dream* |
| *I'll open a bakery.* | *a plan: when?* |
| *I open a bakery.* | *something you do already* |

**Then the reason.** One sentence, about your life, not about bakeries in general.

**Then one detail.** It's the detail people remember and ask about.

**Stop there, and hand back.** A question back keeps the talk going:

> What about you? What would you do?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the one-word dream**:

> Travel.

It's true, and it's empty. The listener has nothing to ask about.

The second is ***I'd* said so fast the *d* disappears**:

> I open a bakery.

The listener hears the present, and asks where your bakery is.

The third is **a dream with only reasons against it**: *I'd like to, but it's not possible, and
there's no money, and …* The listener hears a complaint, not a dream. Say the dream and the reason
first. The *but* can come later, once.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often start with *Oh, I'd love to …* and a laugh, because a dream said out loud feels
a little shy. The laugh is fine. The detail after it is what makes it a real answer.

Under pressure, in an interview, people give the safe dream, *a good job*, with no detail. It sounds
like nobody's dream in particular.

A native listener hears the *d* in *I'd* without thinking about it. If it's missing, they're not sure
whether you're dreaming or telling them news.
