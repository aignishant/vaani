## One-line answer

Record one real dream twice: once deliberately with *I'll* and *if I win*, which sounds like a plan,
and once with *I'd* and *if I won*, which sounds like a dream. Play each to a different listener and
ask *is this a plan or a dream?* The *I'll* version is heard as a plan. The *I'd* version should be
heard as a dream.

## The story

At the tea stall, your friend's cousin says:

> If I get money, I'll go to Canada.

His mother, standing nearby, hears it. By the next morning half the family knows he's leaving. They
ask when his visa comes. He has no visa. He has no money. He was dreaming, but he said it like a
plan.

Said this way, it would stay at the tea stall:

> If I had the money, I'd go to Canada.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- ***I'd***, ***I'll***: from [part 1.1](1.1-if-i-could-and-why.md).
- **plan**: something you've decided to do, from
  [day 63](../../../day-63-weekend-and-year-plans/parts/01-speaking/1.1-fixed-meant-hoped.md).
- **plan-or-dream test**: asking a listener *is this a plan or a dream?*
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Two small changes turn a dream into what sounds like a plan:

| Version | *If* part | Result part | Heard as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P (deliberate) | *If I get …* | *I'll …* | *a plan: it might really happen* |
| D | *If I got …* / *If I had …* | *I'd …* | *a dream: probably not* |

## Why Vaani needs it

A listener acts on what they hear. A dream heard as a plan gets passed on, and people start asking
you about it. In phase 5, at work, *I'll do it* is a promise. *I'd do it if …* isn't. Mixing them up
can promise things you never meant to.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Conditionals: zero, first and second](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/conditionals-zero-first-second).
It gives the first conditional for "future situations we believe are real or possible", and the
second for situations that are "impossible or unlikely in reality".

**Step one: choose one real dream** from [part 1.2](1.2-three-dreams-three-listeners.md), preferably
the big one.

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **P**, deliberately plan-shaped: *If I get …, I'll …*, said plainly, with no reason and no smile.
2. **D**, dream-shaped: *If I had … / If I could …, I'd …*, with the reason and the detail.

**Step three: the plan-or-dream test.** Play P to one listener and D to a different one. Ask each:

> Is this something I'm planning, or something I'm dreaming about?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a P that's secretly D**: you say *I'll* but smile
and laugh, and the listener hears a dream anyway. Say P flat, like news.

The second is **a listener who already knows your dream**. Their answer tells you what they know, not
what they heard.

The third, in real speech, is ***if I get … I'd***: half plan, half dream. Listeners usually follow
the second half, and still aren't sure. Keep both halves the same kind.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Families, neighbours and offices pass on plans quickly. A dream said as a plan can travel a long way
before it comes back to you as a question.

Under pressure, speaking fast, *I'd* loses its *d* first, because it's a small sound at the end of a
small word. In a sentence that matters, slow down just enough to hear it.

A native listener decides *plan or dream* in the first few words. If the *if* part is in the past
and the *d* is there, they relax and enjoy it.
