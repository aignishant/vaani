## One-line answer

After *I wish*, the verb moves one step into the past, even though you mean now: *I wish I lived
nearer.* *I wish I could* is for something you can't do. A wish paragraph has five sentences: the
wish, what's true now, why it matters, what would change, and what you can really do.

## The story

At New Year, your family sticks a sheet of paper on the fridge. Everyone writes one wish. Your
younger brother writes:

> I wish I have a bike.

Your mother reads it and says, *You have a bike*. He meant a new one. Your father writes:

> I wish I lived nearer to my work. I take two buses every morning, and I never see the children before school. If I lived nearer, I'd have breakfast with them. For now, I'm going to leave earlier on Fridays and walk them to the gate.

Everyone reads your father's wish twice. It says what isn't true, why it hurts, what would change,
and what he's doing about it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **wish**: wanting something to be different from how it is now.
- **past simple**: the past form of a verb, from
  [day 48](../../../day-48-problem-story/parts/02-writing/2.1-was-cooking-when.md).
- **second conditional**: *if* + past, *would* + base form, for imagined things, from
  [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-past-would.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, which says what it's about, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **past participle**: the third form of a verb, such as *worked* or *spent*, from
  [day 63](../../../day-63-weekend-and-year-plans/parts/02-writing/2.1-have-or-has-and-the-third-form.md).
- **regret**: being sorry about something you did or didn't do.

A wish is about something that isn't true. English shows *not true* by moving the verb back, just as
it did in the second conditional.

| True now | The wish | What moved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *I live far away.* | *I wish I lived nearer.* | *live* → *lived* |
| *I can't swim.* | *I wish I could swim.* | *can* → *could* |
| *I'm not tall.* | *I wish I were taller.* | *am* → *were* |
| *I spent all my money.* | *I wish I hadn't spent it.* | *spent* → *hadn't spent* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Wishes: 'wish' and 'if only'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/wishes-wish-if-only)
says: "We can use wish/if only + a past form to talk about a present situation we would like to be
different", with "I wish you didn't live so far away" and "He wishes he could afford a holiday". It
says "We can use wish/if only + a past perfect form to talk about something we would like to change
about the past", with "They wish they hadn't eaten so much chocolate."
[Wishes and hypotheses](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/wishes-hypotheses)
adds "I wish I lived somewhere more interesting", "I wish I could get a better job", and "I wish I
was taller", showing *was* in use. In writing, *were* is the safer choice, as it was for *If I were
you* on day 71.

**The four wish shapes.**

| You want … | Write | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a different now | *I wish* + past simple | *I wish I had more time.* |
| an ability you haven't got | *I wish I could* + base form | *I wish I could drive.* |
| to be different | *I wish I were* | *I wish I were braver.* |
| a different past | *I wish I had / hadn't* + past participle | *I wish I hadn't left school.* |

**The wish paragraph: five sentences, five jobs.**

| Sentence | Job | Often starts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | the wish: your topic sentence | *I wish …* |
| 2 | what's true now | *At the moment, …* · *Now, …* |
| 3 | why it matters to you | *This matters because …* |
| 4 | what would change | *If I …, I'd …* |
| 5 | what you can really do | *For now, …* · *I hope …* |

**Hope for the real part.** Sentence 5 is about something possible, so it doesn't use *wish*. In
the discussion under the *Wishes and hypotheses* page, *hope* is explained as suggesting "a realistic
possibility", with "I hope my English becomes better with practice". After *hope*, the verb stays in
the present.

**No comma after *I wish*.** *I wish I could*, not *I wish, I could*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a present verb after *wish***:

> I wish I have a bike.

A reader stops: do you have one or not? Fix: *I wish I had a new bike.*

The second is ***wish* + *will***:

> I wish I will get the job.

That's a hope about the future. Fix: *I hope I get the job.*

The third is **the paragraph with only sentence 1**. *I wish I lived nearer.* It's a true wish, but
the reader doesn't know why it matters to you.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

*I wish* is common in cards, messages, diaries and letters to family. A wish paragraph in a letter
often ends with the real thing the writer is doing, because a reader who cares wants to know you're
all right.

Under pressure, writing a message fast, people write *I wish I can*, because *can* is what they
mean. It's understood among friends and noticed on any page that matters.

A careful reader notices the verb after *wish* first. Moved back, it reads as a wish. Left in the
present, it looks like a mistake.
