## One-line answer

Write one real wish paragraph of five sentences: the wish, what's true now, why it matters, what
would change, and what you can really do. Check the verb after *wish*, the *if* sentence and the
*hope* sentence. Then give it to one person who knows you and ask what you really want.

## The story

On the family sheet on the fridge, your aunt writes her wish in one long sentence that runs to the
edge of the paper. Nobody can tell what she wants. Is it the house, her knees, or her son?

Your grandmother writes five short sentences:

> I wish I could read English. My grandchildren send me messages I can't read. It makes me feel far away from them. If I could read, I'd answer them myself. I hope my granddaughter will teach me a few words every Sunday.

By the next Sunday, the granddaughter is sitting next to her with a notebook. The paragraph didn't
just say a wish. It said what to do about it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **wish**, **regret**: from [part 2.1](2.1-wish-plus-past.md).
- **the five jobs**: wish, now, why, what would change, what you can do, from
  [part 2.1](2.1-wish-plus-past.md).
- **draft**: a first version of a piece of writing, from
  [day 38](../../../day-38-hobbies-and-free-time/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-your-paragraph.md).
- **verb check**: reading only the verbs, one sentence at a time.

A wish paragraph has two kinds of verb in it, and the reader uses them to tell the imagined from the
real:

| Sentence | Real or imagined? | Verb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 wish | imagined | moved back: *could read* |
| 2 now | real | present: *send*, *can't read* |
| 3 why | real | present: *makes* |
| 4 would change | imagined | *if* + past, *'d* + base form |
| 5 can do | real, possible | *hope* + present or *will* |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a list of wishes**: *I wish I had a car. I wish I lived in the city.
I wish I were rich.* That's three topics, and none of them is explained. Choose one.

The second is **sentence 5 missing**. The paragraph ends on the wish, and the reader is left feeling
sorry for you with nothing to say.

The third is **sentence 4 with *will***: *If I could read, I will answer them.* Keep the imagined
sentence imagined: *I'd answer them*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Wishes appear in letters, cards, and messages to family far away. The ones people keep are specific
and end with something real.

Under pressure, writing about something you really want, people pour it out in one long sentence.
Five short sentences with a full stop each are easier to read and harder to misunderstand.

A reader who knows you will notice whether the wish is true. A reader who doesn't will notice whether
it's clear. This paragraph should pass both.
