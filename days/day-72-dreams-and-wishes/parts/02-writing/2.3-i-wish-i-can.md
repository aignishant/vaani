## One-line answer

Make a copy of your wish paragraph with the verbs deliberately left in the present: *I wish I can*,
*I wish I have*, *If I can, I will*. Give both copies to two readers and ask *what's real and what's
only a wish?* The broken copy blurs the line. Your paragraph should keep it sharp.

## The story

On the family sheet on the fridge, your cousin writes:

> I wish I have a job in the city. If I get it, I will move.

His father reads it and starts asking which company, and when. His mother wants to know if he's
already applied. He hasn't. It's a wish, and he wrote it like news.

His sister writes hers underneath:

> I wish I had a job in the city. If I got one, I'd move.

Nobody asks her when she's leaving. They ask her what kind of job she'd like.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly write. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **wish**, **the five jobs**: from [part 2.1](2.1-wish-plus-past.md).
- **broken copy**: a copy of your page with mistakes put in on purpose, labelled *deliberate*, from
  [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/02-writing/2.3-if-i-would-have.md).
- **real-or-wish test**: asking a reader to mark each sentence as real or only a wish.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

When the verbs don't move back, the reader loses the one signal that separates the wish from the
real:

| Sentence | Broken (deliberate) | Fixed | What a reader gets from the broken one |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| wish | *I wish I have …* | *I wish I had …* | *do they have it or not?* |
| ability | *I wish I can …* | *I wish I could …* | a sentence that looks wrong |
| would change | *If I get it, I will …* | *If I got it, I'd …* | *it sounds planned* |

## Why Vaani needs it

A reader decides what's real from your verbs. The phase 4 gate argument has to keep *what is* apart
from *what would happen if*, and so does every formal letter in phase 5. This is where you see that
line break on paper.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Wishes: 'wish' and 'if only'](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/wishes-wish-if-only).
Its patterns are *wish* + a past form for the present, *wish* + *would(n't)* for annoyance, and
*wish* + a past perfect form for the past. None of them puts a present verb after *wish*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who knows your real life**. They mark from
what they know, not from what they read. Use a reader who doesn't know this wish.

The second is **a copy B that's too broken to read**. Change only the three verbs. Everything else
stays.

The third, in real writing, is ***I wish*** **used for politeness where *I hope* belongs**: *I wish you
will have a good trip.* It's a common line in cards. The reader understands the kindness, but on a
careful page it reads wrong. *I hope you have a good trip.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Family members forgive a wrong verb because they know you. A stranger, an office or an employer
takes the verb at its word.

Under pressure, writing something you really want, people write it in the present because it feels
closer. On paper, closer reads as *real*.

A reader who marks every sentence right has been given verbs that tell the truth about what's real.
