# Ten minutes, and nobody helping

`CF-19` · Day 72

## The one-line answer

Ten minutes of interview on a subject you did not choose, with somebody who is asking rather than
helping, on one file — and you pass on four things you can count, none of which is whether it was
any good.

## The model

Day 66 was the mock. This is the test, and there is exactly one difference between them: nobody in
the room tonight wants it to go well for you. The route is the same, the manoeuvres are the same,
everything you need has been in the car since Phase 4 started. What is new is that the person in
the passenger seat is marking rather than coaching.

So the setup is the whole preparation. Get it right and the tape is a measurement; get it soft and
the tape is a pleasant conversation with somebody kind, and the shelf is already full of those.

**Somebody else asks, and it has to be a person.** As on Day 54, there is no solo form for a gate
in this phase — the thing being measured is what your English does with a human being in front of
it. The cheapest version is a voice call with anybody who will pick up. Better if they are not
somebody you speak to every day, because familiarity does half your work for you and it will not be
there when this matters.

**They get three instructions, and no others.**

One: ask, then wait. No help, no finishing your sentences, no supplying a word unless you ask out
loud for it, no kind change of subject when it goes quiet. Two: one follow-up per answer, and pick
the slightly awkward one — the *why* rather than the *what*. Three: somewhere in the middle, ask
something they are fairly sure you have nothing ready for.

**You do not see the questions.** They pick them. If you must supply the pool yourself, write more
than they will use, hand them over face down, and do not read them back.

**Ten minutes, one file, both voices, first take kept.** The same rule as every rehearsal since Day
6 and the same reason: a second take is a performance of the first. If it goes badly, it goes badly
on the tape, and that tape is worth more tonight than a smooth one you reached on the third
attempt.

**What you actually do is already decided.** Point first, one example, land it, hand it back —
Day 61 built the shape, Day 66 counted it. When an answer runs dry you say the stall out loud
rather than falling into it. When the question is one you have nothing for, you use the Day 59
shape and answer the question next to it, out loud, on purpose. Nothing here is new. That is what a
gate is.

**And one thing you do not do, all the way through: you do not apologise for your English.**

Not *sorry, my English isn't very good*, not *sorry for my English*, not a small laugh in front of
an answer that does the same job. This is not about pride. It has an audible consequence and you
will hear it on the tape: the moment the apology lands, the other person starts helping. They slow
down, they simplify, they fill your pauses, they stop asking the awkward follow-up. Everything
after that point is a conversation with somebody being careful with you, and it measures nothing.
One sentence, and you have turned your own gate off.

**No new phrases in this part.** A gate does not teach. `docs/CHUNKS.md` gets nothing from here
tonight — part 02 adds the last of them.

**The phrases the gate listens for.** All of these are already yours. This is a reminder, not a
lesson.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| Let me pick the right example. | Day 66 · thinking time said out loud instead of frozen into |
| Sorry — I've gone off the question. | Day 66 · you hear your own answer drifting and turn it back |
| And that's the example. | Day 66 · landing an answer before you start decorating it |
| Bear with me. | Day 9 · holding the turn while the thought arrives |

## Say it now

Before the recorder goes on, and this is the only rehearsal you get today. Say each line three
times, flat and unhurried, so it arrives without a run-up when an answer goes wrong live.

1. *Let me pick the right example. {{an episode you have ready}}.*
2. *I can answer half of that. The part I can speak to is {{the slice you actually hold}}.*
3. *And that's the example.*
4. *Bear with me.*

Then run lines 1 and 3 together as a pair, twice, with one sentence of real content between them.
That pair is the smallest complete answer you can make — a stall on the front, a landing on the
back — and being able to produce it under any question at all is the thing that stops question
seven from becoming a problem.

Nothing else is rehearsed today. Everything else on this file is live.

## Say it yours

**This is the gate. Recorder on. File: `audio/d72-gate.m4a`.**

> **A ten-minute interview on a subject you did not choose. They ask, you answer, they follow up
> once. One take. Both voices on the file. You do not stop it, you do not restart it, and you do
> not delete it.**

The take is the gate. A rough interview honestly recorded is worth more tonight than a smooth one
you reached on the second attempt, because only one of them tells you where you actually are.

- **Solo form:** there isn't one, and that is the point of Phase 4. What you can vary is who —
  somebody at home, somebody you work near, a neighbour, or anybody at all on a voice call.
- **With a partner:** the three instructions above, and nothing else. They ask, they wait, they
  follow up once with the awkward one, and somewhere in the middle they ask the thing you have
  nothing ready for. No encouragement while you are speaking. The silence is the exercise.

If you do stop the recording, that stop is tonight's answer. Keep the file anyway. The gate can be
re-run on a later day, and the re-run gets its own row in `docs/PROGRESS.md`.

## The deliberate failure

The gate is finished and the recorder is still running. Take the hardest question of the lot and
answer it once more — but this time open with the apology. *Sorry, my English isn't very good,
but…* and then carry on and answer it properly.

**What breaking sounds like:** the answer downgrades itself after the apology. You will hear your
own speed drop and your sentences get shorter and safer, as though the apology were a promise you
now have to keep. And if somebody is in the room, listen to what happens to them — the *mhm*s
arrive, they nod you along, they hand you a word you had not asked for. That is the whole
demonstration. The apology is not modesty; it is an instruction to the room, and the tape catches
the room obeying it.

Keep it on the file. It is the shortest possible proof of why the fourth pass condition is a
condition and not a preference.

## When it breaks in the wild

A real interview, or a first meeting with people who will decide something, and the opening
question is one you have nothing at all for. The silence goes on a beat too long, and out of the
silence comes the apology — because it fills the gap and it lowers what anybody can expect of you.

It works, in the sense that the room softens. It also costs you the rest of the room, because
everything you say afterwards is being graded against the sentence you volunteered about yourself
rather than against the answers you gave.

**The repair:**

> I can answer half of that — the part I can speak to is this.

Then take that half, answer it in the plain past tense with one thing that actually happened, and
land it. Half a question answered specifically beats an apology followed by a vague pass at the
whole of it, every time — and it lowers nothing about you in the room.

## Check yourself

Play back `audio/d72-gate.m4a` once, all the way, without stopping it. Pass if:

- [ ] **Ten minutes, one unbroken file, both voices on it** — and you can say how many questions
      you were asked. Say the number out loud and write it in `docs/PROGRESS.md`.
- [ ] **One freeze or fewer.** The Day 18 definition: a silence where you had stopped and nothing
      was holding the turn. A pause you put there on purpose is not one. Count on your fingers as
      you listen, and write the number down whether it passes or not.
- [ ] **Two answers in every three ended on a landing or a handback**, followed by silence rather
      than a fade. Count both numbers — answers given, answers that ended — and say them out loud.
- [ ] **Not one apology for your English on the whole file.** Not a sentence, not half a sentence,
      not a laugh doing the same job. This one is a zero or it is a fail, and the demonstration you
      recorded a moment ago is the reason.

Nothing about accuracy is on this list. Do not add it, tonight of all nights.

**If the gate does not pass**, this is what happens, and it is short.

Name two or three days between 55 and 71 and re-run them, chosen by what you actually heard. If the
freezes came from a missing word, re-run Day 52 and Day 44. If the answers faded rather than ended,
re-run Day 61 and Day 66. If it was the question you had nothing for that broke it, re-run Day 59
and Day 62. If the apology came out, re-run Day 65 and Day 71 — one of those is about what was
never supposed to change, and the other is a tape of you being better than you think. If the whole
thing came apart in the first minute and settled afterwards, that is nerves, and Day 69 is the
re-run. Each re-run gets its own dated row in `docs/PROGRESS.md`. Then you run the gate again on a
later day, with a new person or a new subject, and that take gets a row too.

That is all. **It is not a restart**, nothing resets, and part 02 happens tonight either way — the
retrospective is not a reward for passing, it is the record of what happened. A gate passed on the
second attempt is a better record than one passed on the first, because the second one has evidence
underneath it.
