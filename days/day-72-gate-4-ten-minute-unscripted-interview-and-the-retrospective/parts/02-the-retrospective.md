# The retrospective, with the evidence in it

`CF-19` · Day 72

## The one-line answer

The sixteenth recording is you saying what changed, what did not, what is still wrong and what
happens now — with a number, a quote or a date attached to every single claim, because a
retrospective made of adjectives is unlistenable in a year and this one is for a year from now.

## The model

This is the last thing you record, and it has to be last, because it is the only recording you
could not have made until everything else existed. Fifteen files are on the shelf. This one says
what they add up to.

Say it, do not write it. Two reasons, and neither is ceremony. This is a speaking course and it
ends in your voice — that was the contract from Day 1. And a written retrospective gets read once
and never again, whereas a recording is a thing you play, and playing it is what you will actually
do the evening you notice the routine has slipped.

**It has four parts and they go in this order.**

**One — what changed, with a number on it.** The eight counts off Day 71 are sitting there: freezes
on the baseline against freezes on the re-record, longest unbroken run against longest unbroken
run, chunks that arrived on their own, endings that landed. Add tonight's gate counts. Say the
numbers out loud, both ends of each one. *I froze this many times on the first tape and this many
tonight* is a sentence with a fact in it. *I'm much more fluent now* is not.

**Two — what did not change, and was never meant to.** Your voice. Your accent. Day 65 decided
that on purpose and Day 71 asked you to find something audibly identical across both tapes. Say
what it was. A retrospective that claims everything improved is a retrospective nobody will
believe, starting with you.

**Three — what is still wrong.** The four errors you named on Day 68 — say all four out loud, and
give each one a verdict: gone, half gone, or exactly where it was. Some will not have moved, and
the ones that have not moved are the most useful sentences on this recording, because they are what
the next stretch of work is made of. Check them against `docs/ERRORS.md` rather than against
memory; memory drops the error you make most, on the grounds that it sounds normal.

**Four — what happens now.** The Day 70 routine, said again into this file so it is on the same
tape as the evidence: the four things, the bad-week version of each, the thing in your week that
each one hangs off, and when the next recording is. Then one sentence directly to whoever plays
this — which is you, later, probably at exactly the point when it has all slipped. Tell them what
to do first. Make it one small thing.

**The rule that holds the whole thing up:** every claim about change gets a number, a quote off a
tape, or a row in a ledger attached to it, in the same breath. No exceptions. If a sentence has
none of those three in it, it is a feeling, and feelings are the thing this repository has spent
seventy-two days refusing to run on.

**The two versions to avoid**, and you will feel the pull of both. The thank-you speech, which is
warm and says nothing. And the manifesto — everything you have realised about language and about
yourself. Both are pleasant to record and neither survives one playback in a year, because there is
nothing in either of them you can check.

**Today's phrases** — the last ones. Six lines for saying what changed with the evidence still
attached. They go into `docs/CHUNKS.md` tonight with nothing from part 01.

| Phrase | You use it when |
| --- | --- |
| I used to {{…}}, and now I {{…}}. | Stating a change with both ends of it in one sentence |
| On the first tape I {{…}} — on this one I {{…}}. | Backing the claim with the recordings instead of a feeling |
| The one that's still there is {{…}}. | Naming an error that survived, without dressing it up |
| That one I've stopped chasing, on purpose. | Saying what you deliberately let go of, so it reads as a decision |
| If you're listening to this and it's slipped — {{…}} | Speaking to yourself later, at the moment it will matter |
| Next one's after {{…}}, and it's about {{…}}. | Naming the next recording by its trigger and its subject |

## Say it now

Say each line twice, out loud, with your own numbers already in the slots. Not approximate numbers
— the ones off the tapes, in front of you.

1. *I used to {{…}}, and now I {{…}}.*
2. *On the first tape I {{…}} — on this one I {{…}}.*
3. *The one that's still there is {{…}}.*
4. *That one I've stopped chasing, on purpose.*
5. *If you're listening to this and it's slipped — {{the one small thing to do first}}.*
6. *Next one's after {{the trigger}}, and it's about {{the subject}}.*

Then run lines 2, 3 and 6 as one continuous run, twice. Evidence, then the thing still wrong, then
the next recording. That run is the retrospective in miniature, and if you can say it in that order
without a build-up, the full one will come out in that order too.

## Say it yours

**Recorder on. File: `audio/d72-retrospective.m4a`.**

> **What changed, what didn't, what's still wrong, and what happens now.** Told to somebody who
> will play this a long way from tonight, and who has forgotten everything about these seventy-two
> days except that they happened.

Rules:

- All four parts, in order. Say the numbers, do not summarise them.
- One take. This one is a record, not a performance, and a second take will be tidier and emptier.
- Have `docs/ERRORS.md`, `docs/RECORDINGS.md` and your Day 71 counts open in front of you. Reading
  a number off a page is not cheating; inventing one from memory is.

- **Solo form:** as written, to the recorder, as if to a person sitting opposite who will hold you
  to it.
- **With a partner:** tell it to somebody who has heard you speak English across these weeks, and
  ask them for one thing at the end — one change they noticed that you did not mention. Record
  their answer on the same file. It is the only outside measurement in the whole portfolio.

## The deliberate failure

Before the real one, record the party version. Somebody at a table asks how the English thing went,
and you give the answer you would actually give: *yeah, really good, it's helped a lot, I feel much
more confident, I can hold a conversation now.* Warm, modest, easy. Let it run as long as it wants.

Then play it back and count two things: how many claims you made, and how many of them had a
number, a quote or a date attached. The second number is usually nought.

**What breaking sounds like:** it is fluent. That is the whole finding, and it is uncomfortable.
The empty version will be the smoothest recording you have made in seventy-two days — no pauses, no
searching, no repairs — because nothing in it is being checked against anything. Every sentence is
one you have said before in some form. The moment you have to attach a number to a claim, the
speech slows down, and that slowing is the sound of something true being assembled rather than
recited.

Keep both. The empty one is not shameful — it is the version you will genuinely need at a table
some day, and it is fine there. It is only a failure when it is the only version you have.

## When it breaks in the wild

A long time from now, somebody asks how you learned to speak English, and you find you are telling
a story rather than reporting anything. Each retelling has smoothed it a bit further: the numbers
went first, then the errors, then the bad weeks, until what is left is a shape with a happy ending
and nothing anybody could act on — least of all the person asking, who is at the start of the same
road and needs the specifics.

**The repair:**

> I recorded myself every day and kept all of it — I've still got the first one and the last one.

Then say one number and one thing that was still wrong at the end. Two facts stop a story from
turning into a legend, and they are the only part of this that is any use to the person listening.
