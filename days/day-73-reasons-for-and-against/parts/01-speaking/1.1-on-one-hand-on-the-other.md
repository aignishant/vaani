## One-line answer

To weigh a decision out loud, say the question, give the reasons for, stop, say *on the other
hand* and give the reasons against, then finish with *so, on balance, I think …*. The listener
should hear two sides and one decision, with the turn clearly marked in the middle.

## The story

At home one evening, your family is deciding whether to buy a second-hand scooter. Your brother
says:

> It's cheap, and it's old, and we need it, and the brakes are bad, and it's cheap …

Nobody knows whether he's for it or against it. Your mother asks, *So do we buy it or not?*

Your sister says:

> Well, there are two sides. One good thing is the price: it's half of a new one. Also, Dad could
> stop taking two buses. (stop) On the other hand, it's eight years old, and the brakes need work.
> So, on balance, I think we should buy it, but only after a mechanic checks it.

Your mother nods. She heard both sides, where one ended and the other began, and what your sister
thinks.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **reason**: why something is so, from
  [day 56](../../../day-56-explaining-why/parts/01-speaking/1.1-reason-and-result.md).
- **verdict**: your final choice after comparing, from
  [day 53](../../../day-53-comparing-two-things/parts/01-speaking/1.1-two-things-three-points-one-verdict.md).
- **the stop**: the long silence between parts, with a higher start after it, from
  [day 57](../../../day-57-pausing-right-place/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-lengths-of-silence.md).
- **marker**: a small word or phrase that tells the listener where you are in your turn, from
  [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/01-speaking/1.1-well-so-the-thing-is.md).
- **for**: in support of something. **against**: not in support of it.
- **the pivot**: the moment you turn from one side to the other.
- **on balance**: after thinking about both sides.

A for-and-against turn has four pieces. The listener needs to hear where each one starts.

| Piece | Marker | What it carries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the question | *Well, there are two sides.* | what's being decided |
| for | *One good thing is …* · *Also, …* | one or two reasons for |
| against | (stop) *On the other hand, …* | one or two reasons against |
| verdict | *So, on balance, I think …* | your choice, and maybe a condition |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 4 gate is a recorded opinion with three reasons and one counter-argument answered. A
counter-argument is a reason against. Today you practise saying the other side fairly and then
coming back to your own view. Without the pivot and the verdict, a listener can't follow which side
you're on.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[A for and against essay about the internet](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b2-writing/against-essay-about-internet)
gives "One advantage of X is …" and "Another advantage of X is …" for the reasons in favour, and
says you "can start this contrasting paragraph with On the other hand, …". For the end, it gives "To
sum up, …" and "Personally, …". The same phrases work out loud.
[Discussion](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/discussion) advises:
"Say what you really think about the topic and explain why you think that."

**Say the pivot with a real stop.** Before *On the other hand*, stop for a breath and a moment more,
and start the next sentence a little higher. That's the long stop from day 57, and it's the one
place in this turn that matters most.

```text
   One good thing is the price. ‖ Also, Dad could stop taking two buses. ‖‖
   ↑ On the other hand, │ it's eight years old. ‖
   So, on balance, │ I think we should buy it. ↘
```

**Keep each side short.** One or two reasons each. Three reasons against and one reason for makes
your listener forget the *for*.

**The verdict falls.** *So, on balance, I think …* is your last sentence, so your voice falls all
the way down there, and not before.

**A verdict can have a condition.** *I think we should buy it, but only if …* is still a verdict.
*I don't know* is not.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the mixed pile**:

> It's cheap and it's old and we need it and the brakes are bad.

The reasons are all there, and the listener can't sort them.

The second is **no verdict**: both sides, then *so … yeah*. The listener asks *so what do you
think?*, which means your turn didn't finish.

The third is **the pivot with no stop**: *also Dad could stop taking buses on the other hand it's
old*. Without the silence, *on the other hand* gets lost.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

In family decisions, the person who says both sides fairly is often the one who gets listened to,
because they've shown they heard the others.

Under pressure, when you feel strongly, you skip the other side, or you say it so fast it doesn't
count. Saying it fairly, and slowly, makes your verdict stronger.

A native listener waits for the verdict. If it never comes, they'll ask for it, and that question
means your turn wasn't finished.
