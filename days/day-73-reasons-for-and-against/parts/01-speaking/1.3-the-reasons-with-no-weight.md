## One-line answer

Record one real decision twice: once deliberately as a mixed pile of reasons with no pivot and no
verdict, and once with the four pieces. Ask two listeners to sort what they heard into *for*,
*against* and *verdict*. The pile can't be sorted. The four-piece version should be.

## The story

Your uncle asks at dinner whether he should retire early. Your father answers:

> Well, the money's less, and you'd have time, and your knees are bad, and the office needs you, and
> you'd get bored, and you could garden.

Your uncle says, *So you think I should?* Your father doesn't know. He said six true things, and none
of them added up to anything.

Your aunt says it again with the pivot and a verdict, and your uncle says, *Right. One more year,
then.*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the four pieces**: question, for, *on the other hand* + against, verdict, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-on-one-hand-on-the-other.md).
- **the pile**: here, reasons said one after another with no sides and no verdict.
- **sort test**: a listener sorts what they heard into for, against and verdict.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The same reasons can be a pile or a weighing. Only the markers, the stop and the verdict change.

| Version | Markers | Stop before the pivot | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| M, the pile (deliberate) | only *and* | none | none |
| W, weighed | *one good thing*, *on the other hand*, *on balance* | yes | yes |

## Why Vaani needs it

At the phase 4 gate, a stranger listens to your recorded opinion and has to hear three reasons and
one counter-argument answered. If your reasons come out as a pile, the stranger can't count them,
and the gate can't pass.

**Step one: choose one real decision** from [part 1.2](1.2-weigh-three-real-decisions.md), or a new

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **M**, deliberately the pile: every reason joined with *and*, sides mixed, no stop, no verdict.
2. **W**, weighed: the four pieces.

**Step three: the sort test.** Play M to one listener and W to a different listener. Give each a
paper with three columns: *for*, *against*, *verdict*. Ask them to write what they heard.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an M that's secretly sorted**: you say the fors
first and the againsts second, out of habit. Mix them on purpose.

The second is **a listener who helps too much**. They guess your verdict from your face. Play the
recording, don't say it live.

The third, in real speech, is **the verdict said first and then forgotten**. You say *I think he
should retire*, then pile up both sides, and end on a reason against. The listener remembers the
last thing. Put the verdict last, or say it first and again at the end.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Many real discussions sound like M, especially when people care. The person who can turn the pile
into sides and a verdict helps the whole room decide.

Under pressure, the pile comes out because every reason arrives in your head at once. Take a breath
and ask yourself *which side is this on?* before you say it.

A native listener who can repeat your verdict straight away, and argue with one of your reasons, has
heard a weighed decision.
