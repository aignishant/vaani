## One-line answer

For and against on paper is two paragraphs. The first opens with a topic sentence for the idea,
*One advantage of … is …*, and holds only reasons for. The second opens with *On the other hand, …*
and holds only reasons against. Each reason gets a real example. The reader should be able to cover
one paragraph and still know what the other one argues.

## The story

The residents' association in your building wants to lock the main gate at ten every night. Before
the meeting, they ask one person from each floor to write the arguments for and against, so
everyone can read them first.

The first page that arrives is one long block. Safety, late shifts, the watchman's pay, delivery
drivers and children's tuition are all mixed together. Nobody can tell which side any sentence is on.

The second page has two paragraphs:

> One advantage of locking the gate at ten is safety. Last month, two bicycles were stolen from the
> stairwell at night. Another advantage is …
>
> On the other hand, several residents work late shifts. The nurse on the third floor comes home at
> half past eleven …

At the meeting, people point at the paragraphs and argue about them one at a time. The page did its
job.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph**: a group of sentences about one idea, from
  [day 13](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-paragraph-is.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, which says what it's about, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **the other side**: what people who disagree with you think, from
  [day 62](../../../day-62-agreeing-disagreeing/parts/02-writing/2.1-view-reason-example-other-side-close.md).
- **advantage**: a good thing about something. **disadvantage**: a bad thing about it.
- **for and against**: a piece of writing that gives the reasons on both sides.
- **proposal**: an idea that people are deciding about, such as *lock the gate at ten*.

On day 62 you wrote one opinion paragraph, with the other side in one sentence near the end. For and
against is different. Each side gets a whole paragraph of its own.

| Paragraph | Topic sentence starts | Holds | Never holds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1: for | *One advantage of … is …* | reasons for, each with an example | a reason against |
| 2: against | *On the other hand, …* | reasons against, each with an example | a reason for |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 4 writing gate is exactly this: a two-paragraph argument, one for and one against, with a
topic sentence in each. Today you learn the shape. Day 80 asks for it on a topic of your choice,
checked by a stranger.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[A for and against essay about the internet](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b2-writing/against-essay-about-internet).
Its tips say: "In the second paragraph talk about the advantages or reasons in favour. Use
expressions like One advantage of X is … and Another advantage of X is …" and "In the third paragraph
give the disadvantages or reasons against. You can start this contrasting paragraph with On the other
hand, …". Its example paragraphs open:

> One advantage of the internet is that young people can do research for their schoolwork and
> homework.
> On the other hand, there are also negative consequences.

The full essay on that page has an opening paragraph and a closing one as well. Today's piece is only
the two middle paragraphs, because those are what the gate asks for.

**Paragraph 1, for.** The topic sentence names the proposal and the first advantage. Then an example.
Then *Another advantage is …* and its example.

**Paragraph 2, against.** *On the other hand,* then the first disadvantage. Then an example. Then
*Another problem is …* or *Also, …* and its example.

**Commas.** A comma after *On the other hand* and after *For example* at the start of a sentence.

**A new paragraph for the other side.** Leave a blank line or indent the first line. That break is
what lets a reader find the other side in a second.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the one block**: both sides in one paragraph, sentence by sentence.
The reader can't find either side.

The second is **the missing topic sentence**. Paragraph 2 starts *The nurse comes home late*. It's a
true example, but the reader doesn't yet know it's an argument against.

The third is ***On the other hand* at the start of the first paragraph**. It's the turn, so it
belongs at the start of the second paragraph only.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Notices, residents' letters, school newsletters and reports at work often lay out both sides this
way before a decision. A reader who disagrees with you trusts a page that shows their side fairly.

Under pressure, writing about something you care about, the against paragraph shrinks to one weak
sentence. A reader notices, and trusts the whole page less.

A careful reader reads the two topic sentences first. If those two lines tell them both sides, the
page is well built.
