## One-line answer

Write the two paragraphs for a real proposal: two reasons for with a real example each, and two
reasons against with a real example each. Then run the cover test: cover one paragraph and ask a
reader what the other one argues. Then give the page to one person who disagrees with you.

## The story

At the residents' association meeting about the gate, one man has written his page. His *for*
paragraph is long and full of examples. His *against* paragraph says only *Some people may not like
it*.

The nurse from the third floor reads it and says, *He didn't even mention the night shifts*. After
that, nobody believes his *for* paragraph either.

The woman from the fifth floor has written both sides with the same care. The nurse reads her
*against* paragraph and says, *Yes, that's exactly my problem*. Then she reads the *for* paragraph,
and she listens to it.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **advantage**, **disadvantage**, **for and against**, **proposal**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-two-paragraphs-two-topic-sentences.md).
- **example**: one real case that shows a reason is true.
- **cover test**: covering one paragraph and asking a reader what the other says, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-the-line-first.md).
- **fair**: giving both sides the same care.

A fair page has two paragraphs of about the same size, each with the same parts:

| Paragraph | Topic sentence | Reason + example | Reason + example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| for | *One advantage of … is …* | ✔ | *Another advantage is …* ✔ |
| against | *On the other hand, …* | ✔ | *Another problem is …* ✔ |

A real example is something that happened, a number, a place or a person: *two bicycles were stolen
last month*, not *crime is a problem*.

**Step three: write the two paragraphs.** Four to six sentences each. Topic sentence first. A blank
line between the paragraphs.

**Step four: the cover test.** Cover paragraph 2 and ask a reader: *What does the other paragraph
argue?* Then cover paragraph 1 and ask the same question. Write down both answers.

**Step five: the fairness check.** Give the page to one person who is on the side you're *not* on.
Ask: *Did I say your side fairly?* Write down what they say.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the thin other side**: one paragraph of six sentences, the other of
one. The reader decides you're not being fair.

The second is **examples that aren't examples**: *For example, it is useful.* That's the reason
again. Give a real case.

The third is **your verdict inside the paragraphs**: *On the other hand, some people work late, but I
think they are wrong.* Each paragraph argues one side. Your own view, if you give one, comes after
both.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

In real decisions, a page that's fair to both sides is the one people pass around, because both
sides can point to it.

Under pressure, arguing about something close to home, the side you disagree with gets the weakest
example. Give it your best one. Your own side will look stronger for it.

A reader who disagrees with you and still says *yes, that's my side* has read a fair page.
