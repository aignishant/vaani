## One-line answer

Make a copy of your two paragraphs where the *against* paragraph deliberately slides back into
reasons *for* halfway through. Ask two readers to mark every sentence *F* for for or *A* for against.
The slid copy gets mixed marks. Your page should get F all through paragraph 1 and A all through
paragraph 2.

## The story

Your neighbour's page about the gate starts well. Paragraph 1 is for, with examples. Paragraph 2
opens *On the other hand, some residents work late*. Then it goes on:

> But the gate is also safer for them, because nobody can follow them in. And the watchman would be
> there anyway.

Those are reasons *for*. The nurse on the third floor reads it and says, *So where's my side?* It
started in paragraph 2 and was gone by the third sentence.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly write. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **for and against**, **topic sentence**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-two-paragraphs-two-topic-sentences.md).
- **slide**: here, a paragraph that starts on one side and drifts to the other without saying so.
- **sort test**: a reader marks each sentence F for *for* or A for *against*.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A paragraph promises one side in its topic sentence. Every sentence after it has to keep that
promise.

| Copy | Paragraph 2 marks a reader should give | What a slid paragraph gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| your page | A A A A | — |
| S, slid (deliberate) | — | A A F F |

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate asks for one paragraph for and one against. If the against paragraph slides, you've written
two paragraphs for, and the gate fails, however good the sentences are. This is the check that
catches it.

## The rule

It puts "the advantages or reasons in favour" in one paragraph and "the disadvantages or reasons

**Step one: make copy S** from your page in [part 2.2](2.2-write-both-sides.md). Keep paragraph 1.
In paragraph 2, keep the topic sentence and the first reason, then replace the last two sentences
with reasons *for*, starting with *But*. Write *deliberate* at the top.

**Step two: the sort test.** Give copy S to one reader and your page to another. Ask each to write F
or A beside every sentence, without reading the topic sentences aloud first.

**Step four: the repair.** If your page went red, find the sentence that got the wrong mark. It's
usually one that starts with *But* or *However* inside a paragraph. Move it to the other paragraph, or
rewrite it so it argues the paragraph's own side, and give the page to a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a slide that's too obvious**, such as *Now the
reasons for*. Real slides are quiet. Start with *But* and keep the same subject.

The second is **a reader who reads your mind**. They mark sentence 3 as A because it's in paragraph 2.
Ask them to mark what each sentence argues, not where it sits.

The third, in real writing, is **answering the other side inside its own paragraph**. You feel you
have to reply straight away. The gate's two paragraphs don't need the reply there. It belongs after
both sides, in your own view.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Slid paragraphs are very common when people write about something they care about. They start the
other side fairly and can't help answering it. Readers on the other side notice at once.

Under pressure, writing fast before a meeting, check only the sentences starting with *But*,
*However* or *Although*. That's where slides begin.

A reader who can mark every sentence without hesitating has read two paragraphs that each kept their
promise.
