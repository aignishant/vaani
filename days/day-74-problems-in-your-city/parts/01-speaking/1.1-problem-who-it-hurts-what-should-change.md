## One-line answer

To talk about a problem where you live, name it exactly: where it is, and what you see. Then say who
it hurts, with one real person. Then say what should change and who should do it: *I think the
council should …* A problem with a change in it is something a listener can act on.

## The story

The ward councillor walks down your lane before an election, asking people about problems. The first
man says:

> Everything is bad here. The roads, the water, everything.

The councillor nods and walks on. There's nothing to write down.

The woman next door says:

> The streetlight at the corner by the temple has been broken since March. My daughter comes home
> from tuition at eight, and she walks that corner in the dark. I think the council should fix it
> this month, and check the other lights on the lane too.

The councillor writes it down and asks her name. She named one place, one person and one change.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **problem**: something wrong that makes life harder.
- **opinion**: what you think, which other people might not share, from
  [day 61](../../../day-61-giving-opinion/parts/01-speaking/1.1-opener-reason-example.md).
- **opener**: the few words that show an opinion is coming, such as *I think*, from
  [day 61](../../../day-61-giving-opinion/parts/01-speaking/1.1-opener-reason-example.md).
- ***should***: a word for saying what you think is the right thing to do.
- **council**: the local office that runs a town or city: roads, lights, water, rubbish.
- **concrete**: real and exact. Something you can see or point to.

A complaint says *this is bad*. A problem said well says three more things:

| Piece | What it says | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the problem | where, and what you see | *the streetlight at the corner by the temple is broken* |
| who it hurts | one real person or group | *my daughter walks there at eight* |
| the change | what should happen, and who should do it | *the council should fix it this month* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 4 gate asks for an opinion with reasons. A problem in your city is the commonest opinion
people have. Today the reason is a real person hurt by it. In phase 5 you'll make complaints to
offices and companies, and they act on the same three pieces.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[should](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/should) gives: "Ought to; indicating opinion, advice, or
instruction, about what is required or desirable", with the sense "Used to give advice or opinion
that an action is, or would have been, beneficial or desirable".
[Discussion](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/discussion): "Say
what you really think about the topic and explain why you think that."

**Piece 1: the problem, concrete.** Say *where* and *what you see*, not how you feel about it yet:

> The bus stop outside the hospital has no roof.

**Piece 2: who it hurts.** One real person, or one group you've seen:

> Old people wait there in the rain for the number twelve.

**Piece 3: what should change, and who should do it.** Open with *I think*, then *should*:

> I think the council should put a roof on it before the monsoon.

**Say *should* lightly, and push the change.** The loud word is the action, not *should*:

> I think the council should put a ROOF on it.

**Stop there.** Three pieces, three or four sentences.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the everything complaint**:

> Everything is bad here. Nobody does anything.

It's a feeling, not a problem. The listener can't do anything with it.

The second is **the change with nobody to do it**: *it should be fixed*. By whom? Name the council,
the building owner, the school or the residents.

The third is ***should* pushed hard**: *they SHOULD fix it*. It sounds angry and the listener gets
defensive. Push the action instead.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

People who get things changed usually say the smallest, clearest version: one place, one person, one
change. Offices can act on that. They can't act on *everything*.

Under pressure, when you're angry about a problem, you pile up every problem at once. Choose one.
You can bring the next one tomorrow.

A native listener hears *I think … should* as an opinion. That's polite. Without *I think*, *They
should fix it* can sound like an order.
