## One-line answer

Choose three real problems where you live, from different parts of life: getting around, safety or
health, and one about people. Say each in three pieces to a real listener, then ask them *what should
change, and who should do it?* If they can answer, your change was clear.

## The story

At the bus stop one morning, the bus is late again. A stranger next to you says, *This city*. You say:

> I know. The number four's been late every day this week. My neighbour lost half a day's pay on
> Monday because of it. I think the bus depot should put a second bus on at eight.

The stranger says, *Yes, a second bus at eight, that's it*. By the time the bus comes, three people
are talking about writing to the depot.

A problem said in three pieces isn't just a complaint. It's something other people can join.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **concrete**, ***should***, **council**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-problem-who-it-hurts-what-should-change.md).
- **say-back**: the listener says in their own words what they heard.
- **responsible**: the person or office whose job it is to fix something.
- **problem card**: a small paper with the three pieces for one problem.

Three kinds of problem, so your three sound different:

| Kind | Examples | Who's often responsible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| getting around | buses, roads, parking, footpaths | the council, the bus depot |
| safety or health | lights, water, rubbish, dogs | the council, the building owner |
| people | noise, queues, rules nobody follows | residents, a shop, a school |

## Why Vaani needs it

three reasons, said clearly enough for a stranger. Today they're problems, and the listener can agree
or disagree with each.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **three problems that are one**: *the roads are bad, the traffic is
bad, the buses are bad*. They're the same kind. Choose three different kinds.

The second is **the change that's too big**: *the government should fix the whole city*. A listener
can't picture it. Choose a change someone could start this month.

The third is **no real person**: *it's dangerous for people*. Which people? *Children walking to the
school on the main road.* The listener sees them.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Talk about problems is some of the commonest small talk there is: at bus stops, in queues, with
neighbours. The people who get listened to name one thing and one change.

Under pressure, when the person you're talking to is the one responsible, like a building owner,
people get vague to be polite. Being concrete *is* polite. It tells them exactly what to fix.

A native listener often answers a clear problem with their own example: *yes, and the one by the
market too*. That's agreement. They're joining you.
