## One-line answer

Record one real problem twice: once deliberately as a general complaint with no place, no person
and no change, and once in three pieces. Ask two listeners *what exactly should change, and who
should do it?* The complaint gets a shrug. The three-piece version should get a clear answer.

## The story

At a residents' meeting in your area, the man from the council asks what's wrong. Your neighbour
stands up:

> This area is a mess. The rubbish, the dogs, the roads. We pay taxes and nobody does anything.

People clap. The council man writes *general complaints*. Nothing happens.

Another neighbour stands up:

> The rubbish bins by the school gate haven't been emptied for two weeks. The children walk past them
> every morning. I think the council should empty them on Mondays and Thursdays, like the bins on the
> main road.

The council man writes it down word for word. The bins are emptied that Thursday.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the three pieces**: the problem, who it hurts, what should change, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-problem-who-it-hurts-what-should-change.md).
- **general complaint**: saying things are bad, with no place, no person and no change.
- **action test**: asking a listener *what exactly should change, and who should do it?*
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Version | Place | Person | Change and who |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| G, general (deliberate) | *this area* | *we* | none |
| T, three pieces | *the bins by the school gate* | *the children* | *the council, Mondays and Thursdays* |

## Why Vaani needs it

At the phase 4 gate, a stranger will judge whether your reasons are real. General complaints sound
like reasons and aren't. In phase 5, complaints to offices and companies only work when they name
the place, the person and the change.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[should](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/should): "Ought to; indicating opinion, advice, or
instruction, about what is required or desirable". A general complaint has no *should* in it at all.

**Step one: choose one real problem** from [part 1.2](1.2-three-problems-three-changes.md).

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **G**, deliberately general: *this place is bad*, several problems, no exact place, no real
   person, no change.
2. **T**, the three pieces.

**Step four: the repair.** If T went red, find the missing piece in the listener's answer. No change

council*, *the owner* or *the school*. Record T again and try a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a G that's still concrete**: you say *the bins by
the school*, out of habit. Say *the rubbish here* instead.

The second is **a listener who lives on your street**. They know which bins you mean. Use someone who
doesn't know the place.

The third, in real speech, is **the angry list at the end**. You say T well, and then add *and the
dogs, and the roads, and …*. The listener remembers the list, not the change. Stop after piece 3.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Meetings, phone calls to offices and conversations with landlords are full of general complaints. The
people who get things fixed are the ones who sound like T.

Under pressure, when everyone around you is angry, G feels natural and gets applause. T gets
results.

A listener who can repeat your change and name who should do it, without asking, has heard a problem
they could help with.
