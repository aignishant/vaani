## One-line answer

To say exactly which person or thing you mean, put a short clause straight after it: *who* or *that*
for people, *which* or *that* for things. *The bag that has a broken zip is mine.* The clause points
at one bag out of many. The word *who*, *which* or *that* takes the place of *he*, *she* or *it*, so
those don't appear again.

## The story

You leave a bag on a train. At the station's lost-property counter, you write on the form:

> A black bag. It is mine.

The clerk sighs and points at a shelf with eleven black bags on it. You try again:

> The black bag that has a broken zip and a red ribbon on the handle.

She takes one down straight away. The first sentence named a kind of bag. The second one pointed at
yours.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **clause**: a part of a sentence with its own subject and verb, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-four-joining-words.md).
- **subject**: the person or thing doing the verb, from
  [day 11](../../../day-11-asking-what-where-who/parts/02-writing/2.1-questions-with-be.md).
- **noun**: a word for a person, place or thing, from
  [day 4](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md).
- **relative pronoun**: *who*, *which* or *that* when it starts a clause about the noun just before
  it.
- **relative clause**: the clause that starts with a relative pronoun and says which one you mean.
- **defining**: here, telling the reader exactly which one.

A relative clause joins two sentences about the same noun into one:

| Two sentences | One sentence with a relative clause |
| --- | --- |
| *She's the woman. She cuts my hair.* | *She's the woman **who cuts my hair**.* |
| *The laptop has a strange noise. I bought it last week.* | *The laptop **that I bought last week** has a strange noise.* |

In each one, *who* or *that* has taken the place of *she* or *it*.

## Why Vaani needs it

Tomorrow's writing is defining and describing things, *a tool that you use to …*, which is built on
this. The gate argument needs sentences that say exactly which people and which things a reason is
about: *residents who work late*, *the bins that are never emptied*. In phase 5, forms and complaint
letters need you to identify the exact order, the exact person and the exact bill.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Relative clauses: defining relative clauses](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/relative-clauses-defining-relative-clauses).
It says: "We can use who or that to talk about people. that is more common and a bit more informal"
and "We can use which or that to talk about things. that is more common and a bit more informal."
Its examples include:

> She's the woman who cuts my hair.
> The laptop that I bought last week has started making a strange noise!

It also says: "we can usually leave out who, which or that if it is followed by a subject", and "We
can't usually leave it out if it is followed by a verb."

**Which word.**

| The noun is … | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a person | *who* or *that* | *the man who lives upstairs* |
| a thing | *which* or *that* | *the bus that goes to the station* |

**Where it goes.** Straight after the noun it's about. Nothing between.

```text
   The man  [who lives upstairs]  fixed my tap.
   ───────   ───────────────────   ─────────────
    noun      relative clause       the rest of the sentence
```

**Leaving the word out.** If a subject comes next, like *I*, *we* or *she*, you can leave *that* out.
If a verb comes next, you can't:

> The bag (that) I left on the train … ← *I* comes next: can leave it out
> The man who lives upstairs … ← *lives* comes next: must keep it

**No commas today.** None of the page's examples puts commas round these clauses, and neither do
yours.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***which* for a person**:

> The man which lives upstairs fixed my tap.

A reader understands you, and knows at once it's wrong. Use *who* or *that*.

The second is **the pronoun said twice**:

> The bag that I left it on the train is black.

*That* has already taken the place of *it*. Take *it* out.

The third is **the clause in the wrong place**: *The man fixed my tap who lives upstairs.* It's
unclear whether the tap lives upstairs. Put the clause straight after the noun it's about.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Relative clauses are everywhere in everyday writing: *the form that you sent*, *customers who paid
in cash*, *the room which is on the left*. In messages, people use *that* most of the time, as the
page says.

Under pressure, filling in a form quickly, people describe the kind of thing and not the one thing.
Adding one clause, *that I ordered on the 3rd*, saves a phone call.

A careful reader notices *which* for a person straight away, and a doubled *it* just after it.
