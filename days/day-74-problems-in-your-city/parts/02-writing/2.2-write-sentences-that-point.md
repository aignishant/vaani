## One-line answer

Write a real note that has to identify people and things exactly, such as a note for someone who's
looking after your home, or a message to a delivery person. Use at least four relative clauses. Then
give it to a reader and ask them to point to, or name, each thing. If they pick the right one every
time, your clauses are doing their job.

## The story

Your cousin is coming to water your plants while you're away. You leave a note:

> Water the plant. Give the fish food. The key is with the neighbour.

She texts you three times. There are six plants. There are two kinds of fish food. There are two
neighbours.

The next time, you write:

> Water the plant that's by the window, not the cactus. Give the fish the food which is in the blue
> tin. The key is with the neighbour who lives opposite.

No texts this time. Every thing on the note pointed at one thing in the flat.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **relative pronoun**, **relative clause**, **defining**: from [part 2.1](2.1-who-which-that.md).
- **identify**: show exactly which one.
- **the pointing test**: a reader points to, or names, the thing each sentence is about.

A note that points has one relative clause wherever the reader could pick the wrong one:

| Could the reader pick the wrong one? | Add a clause? | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| yes: there are several | yes | *the neighbour who lives opposite* |
| no: there's only one | no | *the fridge* |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **clauses everywhere**: *the fridge which is in the kitchen*, when
there's only one fridge. It makes the note longer and no clearer.

The second is **a clause that doesn't narrow anything**: *the plant that is green*. They're all
green. Choose the thing that makes it different: where it is, what's on it, who owns it.

The third is **the clause that runs on**: *the neighbour who lives opposite and she has a dog and
she's usually in*. One clause, one fact.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Notes for people minding a home, instructions for deliveries, and messages at work all live on
relative clauses. The best ones have exactly as many clauses as there are chances to get it wrong.

Under pressure, writing a note on your way out, people write the kind of thing, *the key*, *the
plant*, because they can see which one they mean. The reader can't.

A reader who never has to ask *which one?* has been given sentences that point.
