## One-line answer

Make a copy of your note with three deliberate relative-clause mistakes: *which* for a person, a
doubled *it*, and a clause moved away from its noun. Give both copies to two readers and ask them to
underline anything that looks wrong or points at the wrong thing. The broken copy gets underlined.
Your note should come back clean.

## The story

A neighbour asks you to check a note she's leaving for the man who fixes the water tank:

> The man which came last month fixed the pipe. The tank that we use it is on the roof. Please check
> the tap in the kitchen that leaks.

The tank man can follow it. But he texts back, *Which tap leaks? The kitchen one or another one?*
The last clause had drifted to the end, and it could be about the kitchen or the tap.

You fix it together:

> The man who came last month fixed the pipe. The tank we use is on the roof. Please check the tap
> that leaks, in the kitchen.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly write. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **relative clause**, **relative pronoun**: from [part 2.1](2.1-who-which-that.md).
- **broken copy**: a copy of your page with mistakes put in on purpose, labelled *deliberate*, from
  [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/02-writing/2.3-if-i-would-have.md).
- **underline test**: a reader underlines anything that looks wrong or unclear.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Deliberate mistake | Example | What a reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *which* for a person | *the man which came* | a line that looks wrong |
| doubled pronoun | *the tank that we use it* | a line that looks wrong |
| clause away from its noun | *the tap in the kitchen that leaks* | unsure what the clause is about |

## Why Vaani needs it

The first two mistakes look wrong on the page, and on a formal page they cost you the reader's trust.
The third one can send someone to the wrong thing. The gate argument and every phase 5 letter need
clauses that read cleanly and point at one thing.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Relative clauses: defining relative clauses](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/relative-clauses-defining-relative-clauses).
It gives *who* or *that* for people and *which* or *that* for things. In each of its examples, the
relative pronoun stands where *he*, *she* or *it* would have been, and the clause comes straight after
its noun.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who only checks spelling**. Ask them to read
each sentence and say which thing it's about.

The second is **fixing copy B as you copy it**. Your hand wants to write *who*. Write *which*
deliberately.

The third, in real writing, is **two nouns side by side**: *the door of the flat that is broken*. Is
the door broken, or the flat? The clause belongs to the nearest noun. If you mean the door, write
*the broken door of the flat*, or *the door that is broken, on the flat*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Friends reading a note forgive *the man which*. An office reading a complaint letter notices it, and
some readers take a letter less seriously because of one wrong word.

Under pressure, correcting your own writing, you read what you meant, not what you wrote. Read each
relative clause on its own, and ask *who or what is this about?*

A reader who never underlines a clause and never asks *which one?* has been given clauses that work.
