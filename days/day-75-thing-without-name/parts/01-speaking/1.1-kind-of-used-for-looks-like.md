## One-line answer

When you don't know the name of a thing, describe it in four moves: what kind of thing it is, what
you use it for, what it looks like, and where it goes. *It's a kind of … You use it to … It looks
like … It goes on …* Then ask *What do you call it?* The listener hands you the word.

## The story

Your kitchen tap drips all night. At the hardware shop, you don't know the English word for the
little rubber ring inside it. You say:

> I need … a thing. For the tap.

The man shows you a whole tap, then a spanner, then a pipe. You shake your head each time.

Then you try again:

> It's a kind of small rubber ring. You put it inside the tap so the water doesn't come out. It's
> flat and round, about as big as a coin. What do you call it?

He says *a washer*, and reaches into a drawer. You leave with the right one, and the word.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the ask**: the part of a request that says what you want, from
  [day 21](../../../day-21-at-the-shop/parts/01-speaking/1.1-excuse-me-and-the-ask.md).
- **purpose**: what something is for, from
  [day 70](../../../day-70-explaining-how-it-works/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-its-for-what-its-like-how-it-works.md).
- **describe**: say what something is like, so the listener can picture it.
- **material**: what something is made of: rubber, metal, plastic, cloth, wood.
- **talking around a word**: describing a thing because you don't know its name. The dictionary word
  for it is *circumlocution*.

Four moves, and each one narrows the listener's guess:

| Move | Shape | What it rules out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| kind | *It's a kind of …* · *It's a sort of …* | everything that isn't that kind |
| use | *You use it to …* · *It's for …-ing* | things of that kind with other jobs |
| look | *It looks like …* · *It's made of …* · *It's about this big* | the wrong size or shape |
| place | *It goes on …* · *You find it in …* | things that belong somewhere else |

Then ask for the word: *What do you call it?* or *What's it called?*

## Why Vaani needs it

You will run out of words. Everybody does, in their own language as well. The phase 4 gate is an
unscripted opinion, and the phase 6 gate is a talk on a topic you didn't choose. When a word goes
missing, this is how you keep going instead of stopping.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Circumlocution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumlocution) says it "is often used by beginner and
intermediate second language speakers to convey the meaning of a word they don't know in their target
language." Its examples include describing scissors as:

> a tool used for cutting things such as paper and hair

and a pomegranate as "a kind of fruit; it's red, and it has lots and lots of little seeds in it". It
names "relative clauses, synonyms, and similes" as common ways to do it.
[thingamajig](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/thingamajig) gives an informal word for "Something that
one does not know the name of." It's a real word, but on its own it tells the listener nothing.

**Start with the kind.** It's the biggest step. *A kind of tool*, *a kind of food*, *a kind of
medicine*, *a sort of bag*.

**Then the use.** *You use it to …* is often enough on its own: *you use it to open tins*.

**Then one or two looks.** Size with your hands, or against something everyone knows: *as big as a
coin*, *about as long as my arm*. Colour, material, shape.

**Then the place, if it helps.** *It goes on the wall*, *you find it in the bathroom*.

**Ask for the word, and say it back.** *A washer. A washer.* Say it once aloud in the shop, and once
into your recorder later.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the empty thing**:

> I need a thing. That thing. For the … you know.

*Thing* and *that* are pointing words, and the listener can't see what you're pointing at.

The second is **the look only**: *it's small and round and black*. So is a button, a coin and a
bottle cap. Say the kind and the use first.

The third is **giving up**: *never mind*. You leave without the washer. Four moves almost always get
there.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers do this all the time, in their own language, when a word won't come: *the thing you
use to … what's it called?* Nobody minds. It's how people get words from each other.

Under pressure, at a busy counter, people give up after one try. The second try, with the kind and
the use, usually works.

A native listener enjoys finding the word for you. It's a small game, and when they say *oh, a
washer!* you've both won.
