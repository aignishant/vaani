## One-line answer

Find five real things around you whose English names you don't know. Hide each one, and describe it
in four moves to a listener who can't see it. Count how many moves it took before they guessed or
understood. Then find the real word and record it.

## The story

At a chemist's counter, a woman needs the plastic thing for giving medicine to a baby. She says *a
small spoon*. The chemist gives her a small spoon. She shakes her head.

She tries again:

> It's a kind of plastic tube, with a number on the side. You fill it with medicine and push it into
> the baby's mouth.

The chemist says, *Oh, one of these?* and holds up two different ones. She says, *The tube one, with
the numbers*. Two moves had got her close. One more, the look, and she had it.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the four moves**: kind, use, look, place, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-kind-of-used-for-looks-like.md).
- **talking around a word**: describing a thing because you don't know its name, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-kind-of-used-for-looks-like.md).
- **guesser**: here, the listener who tries to name or picture what you're describing.
- **move count**: how many moves it took before the guesser got it.

Some things need all four moves, and some need two. The move count tells you which moves are doing
the work.

| Move count | What it usually means |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 | your kind and use were sharp |
| 3–4 | normal: the look finished it |
| more than 4, or no guess | the kind was missing, or too wide |

## Why Vaani needs it

A long turn at the gate, a meeting in phase 5 or an unscripted talk in phase 6 will each have a
missing word somewhere. Five real rescues today make the next one automatic.

life.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **choosing things you can name**. It feels safer, and it teaches
nothing. Choose things you really can't name.

The second is **all four moves at once**, in one breath. The guesser can't guess in the middle. Give
one move, pause, and let them try.

The third is **pointing at it**. The guesser sees it, and the move count means nothing. Hide it, or
describe it on a call.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Every learner has a pocket of everyday things they can't name: kitchen tools, bathroom things, car
parts, medicine. Clearing that pocket, one word at a time, is how everyday English grows.

Under pressure, with a queue behind you, people point and say *this one*. It works when the thing is
in front of you. It fails on the phone and in a shop where it's behind the counter.

A native listener who gets it in two moves has heard a sharp kind and use. That's the skill.
