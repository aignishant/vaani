## One-line answer

A written definition names the group a thing belongs to, then the difference that makes it this one
and not the others: *A ladle is a large spoon that you use to serve soup.* After the definition, one
or two describing sentences give its size, shape or material. Plain words only, and never the word
you're defining.

## The story

At the community kitchen near your home, a new volunteer arrives every week. The cook is tired of
saying *No, not that spoon, the other spoon*. She asks you to write a word list and stick it on the
wall.

Your first try says:

> Ladle: a kitchen thing.

The next volunteer brings her a sieve. Your second try says:

> A ladle is a large, deep spoon that you use to serve soup and dal. It has a long handle, and it's
> made of steel.

The volunteer after that reads the wall and brings the ladle first time.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **noun**: a word for a person, place or thing, from
  [day 4](../../../day-04-v-and-w/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-and-many-add-s.md).
- **relative clause**: a clause with *who*, *which* or *that* that says which one you mean, from
  [day 74](../../../day-74-problems-in-your-city/parts/02-writing/2.1-who-which-that.md).
- **definition**: a sentence that says exactly what a word means.
- **group**: the bigger kind of thing that this thing belongs to: *spoon*, *tool*, *fruit*.
- **difference**: what makes this thing different from the others in its group.
- **circular**: using the word itself, or a word from it, to explain it.

A definition has two parts, and a relative clause usually carries the second:

| Word | Group | Difference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *ladle* | *a large, deep spoon* | *that you use to serve soup* |
| *sieve* | *a kitchen tool* | *with small holes, which you use to separate flour from lumps* |
| *apron* | *a piece of cloth* | *that you tie over your clothes to keep them clean* |

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definition). It describes a definition that "takes a large
category (the genus) and narrows it down to a smaller category by a distinguishing characteristic
(i.e. the differentia)", with the example:

> Triangle: A plane figure that has three straight bounding sides.

Among its rules for a good definition: it "Should avoid circularity"; it "Must not be too wide or
narrow" and should "apply to everything the term describes, nothing else"; it "Must not be obscure"
and should use "commonly understood language".

In plain words: the *genus* is the group, and the *differentia* is the difference.

**The shape.**

```text
   A ladle   is   a large, deep spoon   that you use to serve soup.
   ───────        ───────────────────   ───────────────────────────
    word              group                 difference
```

**Choose a group the reader knows.** *Spoon* is better than *utensil*. If the reader doesn't know the
group word, the definition doesn't help.

**Choose a difference that rules the others out.** *That you use to serve soup* rules out teaspoons.
*That's made of steel* doesn't: lots of spoons are.

**Then describe, in one or two sentences.** Size, shape, material, where you find it. These come
after the definition, not instead of it.

**A or an, and a full stop.** *A ladle is …*, *An apron is …*. It's a full sentence.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the circular definition**:

> A cooker is a thing that cooks.

The reader learns nothing they didn't know from the word.

The second is **the group that's too wide**:

> A ladle is a kitchen thing.

That's also a pan, a sieve and a fridge.

The third is **the description with no definition**: *It's long and made of steel.* That describes a
ladle and also a knife. Say the group and the use first.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Word lists on walls, instructions for new staff and notes for someone minding your home all use
definitions like this, often without the word *definition* anywhere near them.

Under pressure, writing for someone new, people write the word and a picture in their own head. The
reader has the word and nothing else. The group and the difference put the picture on the page.

A reader who finds the right thing from your sentence alone has been given a working definition.
