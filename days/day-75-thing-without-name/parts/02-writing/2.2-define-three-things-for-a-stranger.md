## One-line answer

Write a short word list for a real newcomer: three things from your home, work or neighbourhood, each
with a definition and one or two describing sentences. Then cover each word and ask a reader to guess
it from your sentences alone. A definition that gets the right word, or the right picture, works.

## The story

At the community kitchen, a college student comes to volunteer on Sundays. The cook gives her your
word list. She reads:

> A tawa is a flat, round iron pan that you use to cook rotis. It has no sides, and it sits straight
> on the flame.

She finds the tawa, even though she's never used the word. The next line says:

> A rolling board is a round wooden board with short legs that you roll the dough on.

She picks up the right one, and not the chopping board beside it. The list works because every line names a group she knows and a
difference she can see.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **definition**, **group**, **difference**, **circular**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-group-then-difference.md).
- **word list**: a short list of words with a definition for each.
- **cover-the-word test**: hiding the word and asking a reader to guess it from the definition.
- **stranger**: here, a reader who doesn't know your home, work or language.

Each entry on a word list has the same three lines:

| Line | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the word | the name you want them to learn | *tawa* |
| the definition | group + difference | *a flat, round iron pan that you use to cook rotis* |
| the description | size, shape, material, place | *It has no sides, and it sits straight on the flame.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

A word list for a newcomer is writing that somebody uses the next day. The gate argument, and every
phase 5 piece for colleagues, has to be clear to a reader who wasn't in your head. This is where you
test that on one sentence at a time.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definition). Its rules include that a definition "Must not
be too wide or narrow; apply to everything the term describes, nothing else", and "Must not be
obscure; should explain unclear terms using commonly understood language."

or put it where they'll see it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a group word the reader doesn't know**: *a tawa is a griddle*. If
they don't know *griddle*, they're no closer. Choose *pan*.

The second is **a difference that's only in your house**: *the one we use on Sundays*. A stranger
doesn't know your Sundays. Use something they can see or do.

The third is **the word inside its own definition**: *a rolling pin is a pin that you roll*. Use
different words: *a smooth wooden stick that you use to flatten dough*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

New staff at shops, clinics and offices often get a list like this on their first day. The good ones
are short, use the words the newcomer already has, and fit on one page.

Under pressure, writing fast for someone who's already there and waiting, people write *the big one*,
*the red one*, which only works while you're standing next to them.

A reader who guesses your covered word from the sentence alone has read a definition that stands on
its own.
