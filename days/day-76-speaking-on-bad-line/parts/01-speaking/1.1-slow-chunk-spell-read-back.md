## One-line answer

On a bad line, say the important things slowly and in small chunks with a pause between them. Say
numbers one digit at a time. Spell hard words with a word for each letter: *B for bus*. Then ask the
other person to read it back. Shouting the same fast string doesn't pull the sounds apart. Chunks,
digits and letter words do.

## The story

You're on a bus, booking a gas cylinder by phone. The line keeps cutting out. The person at the
agency asks for your customer number. You say it the way you'd say it to someone next to you:

> Fifteen-forty-eight-sixty.

She types *50486*. Two days later, the cylinder goes to somebody else's flat.

Next month, on the same bus, you say:

> My number is … one, five. (pause) Four, eight. (pause) Six, zero. Can you read that back to me?

She reads it back. It's right. The cylinder comes to your door.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **chunk**: a small group of words said as one piece, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/01-speaking/1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md).
- **teens** and **tens**: *thirteen* to *nineteen*, and *twenty*, *thirty* and so on, which sound
  alike, from [day 10](../../../day-10-numbers-times-dates/parts/01-speaking/1.3-thirteen-or-thirty.md).
- **stress**: the loud syllable in a word, from
  [day 7](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-loud-syllable.md).
- **bad line**: a phone call where the sound cuts out, crackles or is covered by noise.
- **digit**: one number sign: 0, 1, 2 … 9.
- **spelling alphabet**: a list with one word for each letter, so a letter can't be misheard.
- **read-back**: the listener says your important words back to you, so you can check them.
- **breaking up**: what people say when the other person's voice is cutting out.

Four tools, each for a different kind of loss:

| Tool | What it protects | What you say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| slow and chunked | whole sentences | two or three words, pause, two or three words |
| digit by digit | numbers | *one, five* not *fifteen* |
| a word for each letter | names, codes, emails | *S for sun, H for house* |
| read-back | everything | *Can you read that back to me?* |

In phase 5 you'll give reference numbers, dates and email addresses on calls to banks, offices and

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Voice procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_procedure) says that radios "reduce the quality
of human speech in such a way that it becomes harder to understand", and that "During difficult
conditions, or when extra care is necessary to avoid misunderstanding, numbers are sent figure by
figure." It says "the person receiving the message may repeat critical parts of the message back to
the sender." It also gives *say again* for "Repeat all of your last transmission."
[NATO phonetic alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATO_phonetic_alphabet) says it exists
because "the usual names of the letters are likely to be misheard".
[break up](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/break_up): "Of a conversation, to cease to be understandable
because of a bad connection", with "You're breaking up. Can you repeat that?"

**Why numbers go wrong: hear it yourself.** Listen to both pages, then say each word with the
recorder on, then play both back. [fifteen](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/fifteen) has an audio
file. It gives two stresses, "/fɪfˈtiːn/" and "/ˈfɪf.tiːn/", and uses the second, with the loud part
first, "When the next word is stressed near the first syllable". [fifty](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/fifty)
gives "/ˈfɪfti/", loud part first, with audio files. So in *fifteen forty*, *fifteen* can come out
FIF-teen, very close to FIF-ty. On a clear line the *-een* saves you. On a bad line, it's the first
thing lost. Digits don't have this problem: *one, five*.

**Chunks for numbers.** Two or three digits, then a pause:

```text
   98   │   45   │   02   │   17        ← said:  nine, eight │ four, five │ zero, two │ one, seven
```

**Letters with words.** The letter names *B, C, D, E, G, P, T, V* all end in the same sound, so on a
bad line they blur. Give each one a word: *B for ball, D for dog, P for pen*. The page lists an
international set, *Alfa, Bravo, Charlie …*. Any common word the listener knows will work.

**Say the frame first.** *My number is …*, *The spelling is …*. Then the listener knows what's
coming and starts writing.

**Ask for the read-back.** *Can you read that back to me?* Listen to every digit. If one is wrong,
repeat only that chunk.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the fast, grouped number**:

> Fifteen-forty-eight-sixty.

Every group is a teen or a ten, and on a bad line they swap.

The second is **louder, not clearer**. You shout the same fast string. The other person hears more
noise and the same blur.

The third is **no read-back**. You hang up sure it went through. The mistake only shows up when the
delivery doesn't.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

People who take numbers on the phone all day, at banks, clinics and call centres, read them back
without being asked. If they don't, ask. It isn't rude. It's what careful callers do.

Under pressure, on a bus or in a market, you speed up to finish the call. Slow down only for the
number and the name. Everything else can stay fast.

A native listener on a bad line says *sorry, you're breaking up* or *say that again?* When you hear
it, don't repeat the whole sentence. Repeat the chunk they missed, more slowly.
