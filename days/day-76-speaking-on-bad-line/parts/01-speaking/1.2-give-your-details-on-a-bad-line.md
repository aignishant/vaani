## One-line answer

Give five real details on a real phone call where the other person is somewhere noisy: your name,
your phone number, your address, a date and a code. Use the four tools. The other person writes down
exactly what they hear. Then compare their page with the truth, detail by detail.

## The story

Your aunt calls from a crowded railway station. She needs your new address to send a parcel. Trains
are announced over her voice. You say it as a sentence:

> Flat 14B, Green Park Road, near the school.

She writes *40D*. The parcel goes round the colony for a week.

The next time a relative asks, you say:

> Flat … one, four, B for ball. (pause) Green Park Road. G for garden, P for pen. (pause) Can you
> read that back?

She reads *one, four, B*. You say *yes*. The parcel arrives.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **bad line**, **digit**, **spelling alphabet**, **read-back**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-slow-chunk-spell-read-back.md).
- **detail**: here, one exact piece of information: a number, a name, a date.
- **noise partner**: the person you call, who stands somewhere noisy on purpose.
- **catch rate**: how many details they wrote down exactly right, out of five.

Each kind of detail needs its own tool:

| Detail | Main danger | Tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| name | letters that sound alike | a word for each hard letter |
| phone number | teens and tens | digit by digit, in chunks |
| address | a number and a letter together | digits, then *B for …* |
| date | *thirteenth* or *thirtieth* | *the one-three*, or the day of the week too |
| code | letters and digits mixed | a word for each letter, digits alone |

## Why Vaani needs it

Every phase 5 phone call to an office has these five details in it. Doing it once with a real
person in real noise shows you which detail you lose first. That's the one to practise.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a partner in a quiet room**. The test is too easy and your catch
rate means nothing. The noise is the point.

The second is **your partner guessing**: *oh, it's your address, I know it's 14*. Use details they
don't already know, or a partner who doesn't know you well.

The third is **a word for every letter**, even the clear ones. It makes the call three times as long
and nobody listens by the end. Use words for the letters that blur.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Most people lose the same detail every time: usually the number with a letter in it, or the email
address. Once you know which one is yours, you slow down only there.

Under pressure, when the person at the other end sounds busy, you rush. A busy person makes more
mistakes, not fewer. Chunks help them too.

A native listener who reads your details back without asking has understood you're being careful.
Most will match your pace.
