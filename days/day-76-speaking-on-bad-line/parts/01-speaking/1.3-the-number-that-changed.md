## One-line answer

Record the same set of details twice. First, say them deliberately fast, with numbers in teens and
tens and letters with no words. Second, use the four tools. Play each one through noise to a
different listener who writes down what they hear. The fast version changes numbers. The careful
version should arrive exactly.

## The story

Your cousin calls a clinic from a market to change his appointment. The receptionist asks for his
file number. He says:

> Thirteen-B-fifty.

She finds file *30-D-15*, somebody else's, and moves *that* appointment. On the day, your cousin
arrives and isn't on the list. A stranger gets a call about a time they never asked for.

*One, three, B for ball, five, zero*, and a read-back, would have kept both appointments where they
belonged.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the four tools**: slow and chunked, digit by digit, a word for each letter, read-back, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-slow-chunk-spell-read-back.md).
- **noise track**: a recording played through a real noise, such as a fan, a running tap or a busy
  road.
- **exact-copy test**: a listener writes down exactly what they hear, and you compare it with the
  truth.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Version | Numbers | Letters | Read-back |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Q, quick (deliberate) | *thirteen-fifty* | bare: *B* | none |
| C, careful | *one, three … five, zero* | *B for ball* | *can you read that back?* |

## Why Vaani needs it

A wrong digit doesn't sound wrong. It sounds like a different right number, and nobody knows until
something goes to the wrong place. This is the check that shows you the mistake before it costs
anything.

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **Q**, deliberately quick: numbers as teens and tens, letters bare, no pauses, no read-back.
2. **C**, careful: the four tools.

**Step three: the exact-copy test.** Play Q to one listener and C to a different one, through a noise
track. Let the noise be loud enough to hear over. Ask each to write exactly what they hear.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **noise that's too soft**. Both versions arrive, and
the test says nothing. Turn it up until Q starts to break.

The second is **a Q that's secretly careful**. Your careful habit creeps in and you pause. Say Q the
way people say numbers to someone standing next to them.

The third, in real speech, is **the read-back you don't listen to**. They read the number back, you
say *yes* while you're still thinking about the next thing, and the wrong digit goes through anyway.
Listen to every chunk of the read-back as if you'd never heard the number.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Wrong-digit mistakes happen on the phone every day, in every language. Careful callers read numbers
back, and careful call-takers ask for them digit by digit.

Under pressure, when you've already said your number twice, you say it faster the third time. Say it
slower instead.

A listener who writes down every digit right through noise has been given chunks their ears could
keep.
