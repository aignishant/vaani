## One-line answer

To report what someone said without their exact words, write *she said (that)* and move the verb one
step back: *is* becomes *was*, *will* becomes *would*, *can* becomes *could*. Change *I* and *you* to
fit the person reporting, and change *tomorrow* to *the next day*. If what they said is still true,
you don't have to move the verb.

## The story

Your flatmate is out when the landlord comes round. He tells you:

> I'll send the plumber tomorrow. I can't come myself.

You write a note for your flatmate and leave it on the fridge:

> The landlord came. He said he'd send the plumber the next day. He said he couldn't come himself.

Your flatmate reads it on Friday morning. You wrote it on Wednesday. *The next day* tells them it's
Thursday. A copied *tomorrow* would keep them waiting in all Friday for a plumber who came the day
before.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **speech marks**: the marks round somebody's exact words, from
  [day 49](../../../day-49-what-someone-said/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-marks-around-the-words.md).
- **direct speech**: somebody's exact words, inside speech marks, from
  [day 49](../../../day-49-what-someone-said/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-marks-around-the-words.md).
- **reported speech**: telling what somebody said without their exact words and without speech marks.
  It's also called *indirect speech*.
- **backshift**: moving the verb one step into the past when you report it.
- ***would***: the helper word that goes before a base form, from
  [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/02-writing/2.1-if-past-would.md). Here, the past
  of *will*.

The same message, two ways:

| Direct speech | Reported speech |
| --- | --- |
| He said, "I'll send the plumber tomorrow." | He said he'd send the plumber the next day. |
| She said, "I'm tired." | She said she was tired. |

## Why Vaani needs it

Tomorrow's writing is a summary of a conversation, made almost entirely of reported speech. In
phase 5 you'll write meeting notes and emails passing on what a manager, a customer or an office
said. If a verb or a time word is wrong, the reader acts on the wrong day.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Reported speech: statements](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/reported-speech-statements)
says: "In indirect speech, we often use a tense which is 'further back' in the past". It gives
"tomorrow changes to the next/following day", with *this* becoming *that* and *here* becoming
*there*. It also says: "If what the speaker has said is still true or relevant, it's not always
necessary to change the tense", with "He told me he's working in Italy for the next six months."
[Reported speech](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/reported-speech)
lists "can changes to could; will changes to would", and gives "We went yesterday" → "She said they
had been the day before".

**The step back.**

| They said | You write |
| --- | --- |
| *am / is / are* | *was / were* |
| present simple: *I live* | past simple: *she lived* |
| *will* | *would* (*'d*) |
| *can* | *could* |
| past simple: *I went* | past perfect: *she had gone* |

**The people change.** *I* becomes *he* or *she*. *You* becomes *me* or *us* if it meant you.

**The time and place words change.**

| They said | You write |
| --- | --- |
| *tomorrow* | *the next day* |
| *yesterday* | *the day before* |
| *this* | *that* |
| *here* | *there* |

**Said and told.** On both pages, *told* always has a person straight after it: *He told me …*,
*I told them …*. *Said* has none: *She said she was …*. Keep that pattern. *That* after *said* is
optional: *He said (that) he'd come.*

**No speech marks, no comma** before the reported words. The comma belongs to direct speech.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the time word copied**:

> The landlord said he will come tomorrow.

The reader doesn't know which tomorrow. Fix: *He said he'd come the next day*, or better, the day's
name: *He said he'd come on Thursday.*

The second is **the person not changed**: *She said I am tired.* Now it sounds like you're the tired
one.

The third is **speech marks on reported speech**: *He said "he would come".* Those words aren't his
exact words any more, so they don't go in marks.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

In notes and messages, people often skip *the next day* and write the day's name or the date. It's
clearer than either *tomorrow* or *the next day*, and it doesn't depend on when the note is read.

Under pressure, passing on a message fast, people copy the words they heard: *he said he will come
tomorrow*. Among family it's usually worked out. In a work email it causes a wrong day.

A careful reader checks two things in reported speech: whose *I* it is, and which day the time word
means.
