## One-line answer

Write a real message that passes on what two or three people said, for someone who wasn't there. Put
every report in reported speech, with the verbs stepped back and every time word replaced with a day
or a date. Then give it to its reader and ask *who said what, and when is it happening?*

## The story

Your mother had a busy morning while your father was at work. The doctor's clinic phoned, the school
phoned, and the electrician came. She asks you to write it all down for him.

Your first note says:

> Clinic said come tomorrow. School said your son is sick. Electrician said I will come back.

Your father reads it in the evening and asks who's coming back, which son, and whether *tomorrow*
means today now. You write it again:

> The clinic phoned. They said your appointment had moved to Thursday at ten. The school told Mum that
> Ravi had a fever, so she brought him home. The electrician said he'd come back on Saturday morning.

Your father reads it once and puts Thursday in his diary.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **reported speech**, **backshift**: from [part 2.1](2.1-said-that-and-the-step-back.md).
- **pass on**: tell one person what another person said.
- **message**: here, a short note written for someone who wasn't there.
- **who-what-when test**: asking the reader who said each thing, what they said, and when it's
  happening.

A message that passes on speech has one line per speaker:

| Line | What it carries | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| who | the speaker, clearly named | *The clinic phoned.* |
| what | the report, stepped back | *They said your appointment had moved …* |
| when | a day or a date, not *tomorrow* | *… to Thursday at ten.* |

## The rule

[Reported speech](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/reported-speech).

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the speaker left out**: *said come tomorrow*. Who said it? Name them
first.

The second is ***said* with a person after it**, where the pages always use *told*: *She said me she
was coming.* Following the pages' pattern, write *She told me she was coming*, or *She said she was
coming*.

The third is **every little word reported**: *He said hello and he said how are you and he said …*
Pass on what the reader needs, not everything.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Messages that pass on speech are some of the most common things people write: notes at home, texts to
family, emails at work. The ones that work name the speaker and the day.

Under pressure, writing fast, people write the speaker's words with no speaker and a *tomorrow* that
belongs to yesterday. The reader then has to phone someone to check.

A reader who can say who, what and when without asking has got a message that does its job.
