## One-line answer

Report three real conversations to three people who need to know about them, each in three pieces:
who, the point with a reporting verb, and *so*. Then ask each listener to tell you what they'll do
now. If their answer matches what the conversation meant, your report carried the point.

## The story

Your father comes back from the bank. Your mother, your sister and you all want to know what
happened, and he tells each of you differently.

To your mother:

> I saw the manager. She told me to bring the old passbook on Monday. So I'll need you to find it
> tonight.

To your sister:

> The manager asked for your college letter too. So bring it home tomorrow.

To you:

> They promised to fix the name spelling this week. So you don't need to go.

Three listeners, and three reports of one conversation. Each one has the point that matters to that
listener, and a *so* for them.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **report**, **the point**, **so for us**, **reporting verb**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-who-the-point-what-it-means.md).
- **the right listener**: the person the conversation actually affects.
- **next step**: what the listener will do because of your report.

The same conversation can have a different *so* for different listeners. That's not changing the
report. It's choosing the point that matters to them.

| Listener | The point for them | *So* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the person who has to act | what was asked of them | *so you need to …* |
| the person who was worried | whether it's sorted | *so it's fine* or *so we still need …* |
| the person who doesn't need to act | what changed | *so nothing for you to do* |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the everyday version of what you'll do at work in phase 5: going to one meeting and reporting
it to several people who each need a different piece. It also practises the reporting verbs in real
speech, with real people waiting for the news.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Reported speech: reporting verbs](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/reported-speech-reporting-verbs).
It lists verbs that take *to* + verb, including "advise, agree, … invite, offer, … promise, refuse
and remind", verbs that take *-ing*, including "admit, apologise for, complain about, deny, … and
suggest", and *tell*, *warn* and others for instructions, with "The police officer warned us not to
go in there."

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the same report for everyone**. Your sister gets the passbook and
the manager's hello, and misses the college letter. Put their piece first.

The second is **the reporting verb that says nothing**: *she said something about the passbook*.
*She told me to bring the passbook* says what she wanted.

The third is **no real listener**: you tell it to someone it doesn't affect, and their *what will you
do?* is *nothing*. Choose the person who has to act.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

In a family or a small office, one person often goes to the appointment and reports back to three
people. Doing it well saves three more phone calls.

Under pressure, when everyone asks at once, you tell the whole thing once to the room. Then give each
person their *so* straight after.

A listener who tells you their next step without you asking has understood what the conversation
meant for them.
