## One-line answer

Report one real conversation twice. First, deliberately replay it line by line, *and then she said,
and then I said*, with no point and no *so*. Second, use the three pieces. Play each to a different
listener and ask *what was the point, and what do we do now?* The replay leaves them guessing. The
report should give them both.

## The story

Your neighbour comes back from the electricity office, where she went about a wrong bill. Her
husband asks what happened. She says:

> So I got there, and the man said *take a token*, and I said *I have a token*, and he said *wait*, and
> then another man came, and he said *what's the problem*, and I said *the bill*, and he looked, and he
> said *hmm*, and then …

It goes on. Her husband still doesn't know whether they have to pay.

Their daughter, who went too, says:

> The officer told us the meter was read wrongly. He promised to send a new bill by the fifteenth.
> So we don't pay the old one.

Her father puts the old bill away.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the three pieces**: who, the point, *so*, from
  [part 1.1](1.1-who-the-point-what-it-means.md).
- **replay**: telling a conversation line by line, in the order it happened, with every *he said*
  and *I said*.
- **point-and-next test**: asking a listener *what was the point, and what do we do now?*
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Version | Shape | Length | What a listener gets |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R, replay (deliberate) | *and then he said … and then I said …* | long | the order, not the point |
| P, report | who, the point, *so* | short | the point and the next step |

## Why Vaani needs it

A replay feels complete, because nothing is left out. But the listener has to do the reporting
themselves. In phase 5, a replay in a meeting is what makes people stop listening. The point and the
*so* are what they came for.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Reported speech: reporting verbs](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/reported-speech-reporting-verbs).
Each of its examples, such as "She promised to do the report by Friday" and "She suggested taking a
break", puts a whole exchange into one verb and one action. That's the job a replay never does.

**Step one: choose one real conversation** from [part 1.2](1.2-pass-on-three-real-messages.md), or a
new one with at least five back-and-forth lines.

**Step two: record two versions.**

1. **R**, deliberately a replay: every line in order, *and then … said*, no point and no *so*.
2. **P**, a report in three pieces.

**Step three: the point-and-next test.** Play R to one listener and P to a different one. Ask each:

> What was the point? And what do we do now?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an R that's secretly short**, because you've already
summarised it in your head. Replay every line, including the hellos.

The second is **a listener who was there**. They know the point already. Use someone who wasn't.

The third, in real speech, is **the report that starts well and slides into replay**: *The officer
promised a new bill … and then he said, and I said …*. Once the *so* is out, stop.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Replays are how most people tell a conversation when they're still upset about it. It's natural. The
listener, though, is waiting for one sentence.

Under pressure, when someone asks *so what happened?* at the door, say the *so* first: *We don't pay
the old bill*. Then the point. Then, if they want it, the story.

A listener who can say the point and the next step after one hearing has been given a report, not a
recording.
