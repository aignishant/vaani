## One-line answer

Make a copy of your summary written deliberately as a script: *X said … then Y said …*, in the order
it happened, with no labels. Give both versions to two readers who weren't there, and ask them what
was decided, who does what, and what's still open. The script hides the answers. Your summary should
give all three.

## The story

The residents of your building have a meeting about repainting the stairs. The secretary writes it up
for the people who missed it:

> Mr Shah said the paint is peeling. Mrs Rao said it's been like that for years. Mr Shah said yes but
> it's worse now. Someone asked about the cost. Mrs Das said her brother-in-law is a painter. Mr Shah
> said let's get quotes. Everyone agreed. Mrs Das said she'll call him. Then they talked about the
> parking …

Three residents who weren't at the meeting read it. One thinks the painting is already booked. One
thinks nothing was decided. One stopped reading at the parking.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly write. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **summary**, **transcript**, **decision**, **still open**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-decided-who-does-what.md).
- **script copy**: here, a copy of your summary rewritten as *X said … then Y said …* in order, with
  no labels.
- **three-answer test**: asking a reader what was decided, who does what, and what's still open.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The facts in both versions are the same. What changes is where the reader has to look for them.

| Version | Where the decision is | Where the actions are | What a reader does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S, script (deliberate) | somewhere in the middle | scattered | guesses, or stops reading |
| your summary | under *Decided:* | under *Who does what:* | finds them in a second |

## Why Vaani needs it

A script feels safer, because you haven't left anything out. But a summary that hides its decisions
doesn't do its job. Meeting notes in phase 5 are read by busy people, often on a phone, often only the
first few lines.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minutes): "a verbatim report (transcript) is typically not
useful", and minutes "should contain mainly a record of what was done at the meeting, not what was
said by the members."

**Step one: make copy S** from your summary in
[part 2.2](2.2-summarise-a-real-conversation.md). Rewrite it as *X said … then Y said …* in the order
things were said, including a line or two of small talk. Remove the labels. Keep every fact. Write
*deliberate* at the top.

**Step two: the three-answer test.** Give copy S to one reader and your summary to another. Neither
should have been in the conversation. Ask each:

> What was decided? Who does what, by when? What's still open?

**Step three: fill the table.**

| Version | Decided | Who does what | Still open |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S (deliberate) | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| your summary | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when your summary's reader gets any of the
three answers wrong or missing. It is **GREEN** when they get all three right and copy S's reader
misses or gets wrong at least one.

**Step four: the repair.** If your summary went red, find which answer they missed. A missed decision
means it wasn't near the top, or wasn't labelled. A missed action usually has no owner or no date. A
missed *still open* means it wasn't written at all. Fix it and give it to a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a script that's accidentally a summary**, because
you wrote the decision first out of habit. Put it where it came in the conversation.

The second is **a reader who was there**. They answer from memory. Use someone who wasn't.

The third, in real writing, is **the half-summary**: labels at the top, then a script under each one.
*Decided: Mr Shah said the paint is peeling, and Mrs Rao said …* The label doesn't help if what's
under it is still a script.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Scripts of meetings get sent all the time, and they are the reason people ask *so what did we decide?*
in the next meeting.

Under pressure, when you're afraid of leaving something out, it's tempting to write it all down. Keep
your full notes for yourself, and send the four parts.

A reader who answers all three questions after one read, without being there, has been given a
summary and not a script.
