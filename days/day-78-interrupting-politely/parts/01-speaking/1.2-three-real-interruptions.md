## One-line answer

In real conversations, do three things: interrupt politely once, get yourself back on track once
after being interrupted, and help someone else back on track once with *You were saying …?* After
each one, write down what happened. The skill is working if nobody looked annoyed and every point got
finished.

## The story

At the residents' meeting the next month, three things happen in one evening.

The treasurer is reading out numbers, and you need to know if one is a mistake. You wait for a
breath: *Sorry, quick question. Was that four thousand or fourteen?* She checks, answers, and goes
on.

You're explaining the new gate rule, and the neighbour from the ground floor cuts in about his
scooter. You let him finish, then say, *Anyway, as I was saying, the gate closes at ten from next
week.* People nod.

Your neighbour is interrupted halfway through a story about the lift. Nobody gives her the turn back.
You say, *Sorry, you were saying, about the lift?* She smiles and finishes.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **interrupt**, **hand back**, **back on track**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-sorry-to-interrupt-then-back.md).
- **three roles**: the interrupter, the one interrupted, and the helper.
- **the helper's line**: *You were saying …?*, to give the turn back to someone else who was cut off.
- **field note**: a quick written note made straight after something happens.

Each role has one job:

| Role | You … | Words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| interrupter | get in politely, say it short, hand back | *Sorry to interrupt, but …* · *Sorry, please go on.* |
| interrupted | let them finish, then return | *Anyway, as I was saying, …* |
| helper | give someone else their turn back | *Sorry, you were saying …?* |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the version you'll use in every phase 5 meeting. Doing it in a real conversation, with real
people, is the only way to find out whether your *sorry* sounds warm enough and whether you can find
your place again.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[How to Interrupt Someone in a Nice Way](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/a/how-to-interrupt-someone-in-a-nice-way/4735900.html)
gives phrases for different reasons to interrupt, including "Sorry to interrupt but may I ask a
question?" and "I'm sorry for the interruption but I have a quick question."
[as I was saying](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/as_I_was_saying) gives *you were saying* as a related
term.

**Step one: rehearse the three lines.** Say each one aloud with the recorder on, three times, with a
warm *sorry*:

> Sorry to interrupt, but …
> Anyway, as I was saying, …
> Sorry, you were saying …?

**Step two: watch for the chances.** Over the next day or two, in real conversations at home, at work,
at a meeting, on a call or in a shop, use each line once when a real chance comes. Don't make a chance
that isn't there.

**Step four: record the best one again.** After the conversations, record the interruption that went

**Step five: fix the one that didn't work.** If someone looked annoyed, look at your note. Did you come
in mid-word? Did you forget *sorry*? Did you keep the turn? Record it again the right way.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **waiting too long**. You wait for the perfect gap, and the meeting
ends. A breath at the end of a sentence is enough.

The second is **forgetting the hand-back**. You get in politely, make your point, and keep going. The
first speaker never finishes.

The third is **getting back on track too early**: you say *anyway* before the other person has
finished their interruption. They feel cut off. Let them finish, then return.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Conversations with three or more people are full of interruptions. The helper's line, *you were
saying?*, is one of the kindest things you can say in a group, and people remember it.

Under pressure, in a group that talks fast, you'll hear fluent speakers skip *sorry* with friends.
With people you don't know well, or anyone senior, keep it.

A native listener who says *oh, thanks* after *you were saying?* has noticed your kindness. In a
meeting at work, that's noticed too.
