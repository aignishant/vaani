## One-line answer

With a willing partner, act out one real interruption twice while they're telling you something.
First, deliberately barge in: mid-word, no *sorry*, no reason, no hand-back. Second, interrupt
politely. After each one, ask *how did that feel, and can you finish what you were saying?* The
barge-in feels rude and loses their point. The polite one shouldn't.

## The story

At the residents' meeting, the secretary is reading out the money spent on the lift. Through the
window, you see rain falling on her scooter, and its headlight is on. You cut in:

> No — your scooter — the light —

She stops, confused and a bit hurt, and loses her place in the numbers. Long after that, she's
still looking for the line she was on.

At the next meeting, the same kind of thing happens. You say:

> Sorry to interrupt, it's quick. Your scooter light's on. Sorry, go on, the lift money?

She says *oh, thanks*, and goes straight back to the number.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **barge in**, **hand back**, **back on track**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-sorry-to-interrupt-then-back.md).
- **thread**: here, the point someone was making, which they have to find again after an interruption.
- **feel-and-finish test**: asking the person you interrupted how it felt, and whether they can finish
  what they were saying.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Version | Timing | *Sorry* and reason | Hand-back | What the speaker feels |

| B, barge-in (deliberate) | mid-word | none | none | cut off, loses the thread |
| P, polite | at a breath | *Sorry to interrupt, it's quick* | *Sorry, go on, the lift money?* | helped back to the thread |

## Why Vaani needs it

At work in phase 5, a barge-in with a manager or a customer costs more than the point you made. This
check lets you hear, from a real person, what it feels like on the other side. That's the only way to
know how your interruption lands.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[How to Interrupt Someone in a Nice Way](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/a/how-to-interrupt-someone-in-a-nice-way/4735900.html):
"when we interrupt a speaker for any reason, we almost always begin with 'Sorry,' 'I'm sorry' or, for
some kinds of interruptions, 'Excuse me.'"
[as I was saying](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/as_I_was_saying): "Used to refer back to one's own
previous statement in a discourse."

**Step one: choose a partner and a real thing to interrupt with.** Something true and short that you
really need to tell them. Ask them to tell you about something from their day, at length.

**Step two: act out B.** While they're mid-sentence, deliberately barge in: no *sorry*, no reason, say
your thing, and don't hand back. Record it if they agree.

**Step three: ask.** *How did that feel? Can you finish what you were saying?*

**Step four: act out P.** Ask them to tell you about something else. At a breath, interrupt politely:
*sorry*, reason, your point, and the hand-back with their last word or two. Then ask the same
questions.

**Step five: the repair.** If P went red, ask them what made it feel rude. It's usually timing, so wait

say their last few words back to them. Fix that one thing and try again with a new partner.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a partner who's too kind** to say B felt rude. Tell
them the exercise works only if they're honest.

The second is **a B that's too gentle**. You add a small *sorry* out of habit. Leave it out
deliberately.

The third, in real speech, is **the long *sorry***: *Oh I'm so sorry, I'm really sorry to interrupt,
I know you're talking, sorry …* It takes longer than the interruption and still doesn't say why. One
*sorry* and a reason.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Barge-ins happen in every family and office, usually when someone's excited or worried. Nobody means
to be rude. But the person cut off remembers the feeling longer than the news.

Under pressure, when your news is urgent, the barge-in comes out on its own. Take one breath for
*sorry*. It costs almost nothing.

A person who finishes their thought after your interruption, and thanks you for the news, has been
interrupted well.
