## One-line answer

Write a real page that someone will read, such as a letter, a request or a notice. Leave a gap, then
do the second read in three passes and mark every change. Write a clean copy, and give both the
marked page and the clean copy to one reader. Ask which one they'd rather receive, and why.

## The story

Your building's residents want a letter to the local council about the broken footpath outside. You
offer to write it. At night you write:

> Dear sir, the footpath infront of our building is broken since six months, old peoples are falling
> and we request you please to repair it urgent. Many complaints we have given but no action has been
> taken and this is very bad and we are very upset because it is dangerous and …

In the morning you read it aloud. Pass 1 finds that the building's address isn't in it. Pass 2 finds
one sentence that goes on for four lines. Pass 3 finds *infront*. The clean copy is shorter, and has
the address, one fact per sentence, and a clear request.

The councillor's office replies to the clean copy within a week.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the second read**, **edit**, **distance**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-wait-then-read-as-a-stranger.md).
- **marked copy**: your page with every change shown: a line through, new words above, a mark in the
  margin.
- **clean copy**: the page written out again with all the changes made.
- **change count**: how many changes each pass found.

What each pass usually finds in a real page:

| Pass | Often finds | The fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sense | a missing fact (a date, an address, a name); the request buried at the end | add it; move the request up |
| sentences | one sentence running on for three or four lines; a missing or doubled word | split it at *and*; fix the word |
| surface | a wrong spelling; a missing capital or full stop | correct it |

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Proofreading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofreading). It advises getting "some distance from the
text before attempting to proofread it", and describes copy editors doing "sentence-by-sentence
analysis" to improve "grammar, spelling, punctuation, and style".

**Step one: write a real page** that someone will actually read: a request to an office, a letter to
a school, a notice for your building, or an email to someone you don't know well. Around a hundred to
a hundred and fifty words.

**Step two: make a gap.** At least a sleep, if you can.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the edit that adds more than it cuts**. The second read is mostly
for cutting and fixing. If the clean copy is much longer, check that every new sentence earns its
place.

The second is **fixing in your head and not on the page**. You notice *infront*, think *I'll remember*,
and copy it out wrong again. Mark every change as you find it.

The third is **a new mistake in the clean copy**. Copying out is where words get dropped. That's why
you read the clean copy once more.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Offices, schools and companies get many letters. The ones that get action are short, have the facts
and the request near the top, and have no mistakes that make the reader stop.

Under pressure, writing a complaint while you're angry, the first draft is always too long and too
hot. The second read, after a sleep, is where it becomes a letter someone will act on.

A reader who says *the clean one* straight away, and can say why, has seen what the second read does.
