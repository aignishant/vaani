## One-line answer

Write a new page, and read it twice for mistakes. First, deliberately straight after writing,
silently. Then after a gap, aloud, in three passes. Keep the two lists of what each read found. Then
give the page to a reader who marks everything they see. The straight-after read misses things the
gap-and-voice read catches, and the reader should find nothing your second read missed.

## The story

A neighbour asks you to help with a letter to her son's school. She wrote it at lunchtime and read it
through straight away:

> I am writing to inform that my son will absent on Monday and Tuesday because we are going to to my
> sister marriage in Nagpur.

She says, *I checked it, it's fine*. You read it aloud and trip three times: *inform* whom?, *will
absent*, *to to*. She reads it again aloud herself, and trips at the same places. She'd read it with
her eyes, straight after writing, and her eyes had read what she meant.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly write. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the second read**, **distance**: from [part 2.1](2.1-wait-then-read-as-a-stranger.md).
- **marked copy**: from [part 2.2](2.2-edit-a-real-page.md).
- **find list**: a list of every mistake one read found.
- **stranger check**: a reader who didn't write the page marks everything that looks wrong.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Read | When | How | What it usually finds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A (deliberate) | straight after writing | silently, once | the surface, and not much else |
| B | after a gap | aloud, three passes | missing and doubled words, run-ons, missing facts |

## Why Vaani needs it

Many people think they've checked a page because they've looked at it. This is the test that shows you,
with your own page and a real reader, what a straight-after silent check misses. From here on, every
page that matters gets read B.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Proofreading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proofreading). It says proofreaders should "get some
distance from the text before attempting to proofread it, and best not to proofread one's own text".
Read A breaks both halves of that advice. Read B keeps the first half. The stranger check keeps the
second.

**Step one: write a new real page** of around a hundred words, for a real purpose. Don't correct as
you write.

**Step two: read A, deliberately.** Straight after you finish, read it once silently. Write every
mistake you find on a find list. Don't fix the page yet. Label this list *A, deliberate*.

**Step three: the gap, then read B.** After a sleep, or at least a long break, read the same page
aloud with your finger under the words, in three passes. Write every mistake on a second find list,
*B*.

**Step four: the stranger check.** Give the page, still unfixed, to a reader who didn't write it. Ask
them to mark everything that looks wrong or makes them stop.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the stranger found a mistake that read B
missed. It is **GREEN** when read B found everything the stranger found, and read A missed at least
one.

**Step six: the repair.** If B went red, look at the mistake it missed. Was it a word you skipped over
because you knew what came next? Slow down and keep your finger under every word. Was it a missing
fact? Add a question to pass 1: *what does the reader need that isn't here?* Try the improved read B on
your next page, and check it with a new stranger.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **fixing the page after read A**. Then read B has
nothing to find. Leave the page alone until the stranger has seen it.

The second is **a stranger who's too kind**. They mark nothing. Ask them to mark anything, even if
they're not sure it's wrong.

The third, in real writing, is **trusting a spell checker for read B**. It can't know which real word you
meant, *form* or *from*, and it never finds a missing fact. It's a help for pass 3, not a second read.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Almost everybody reads their own page straight after writing it and thinks it's fine. That's why
emails with missing words get sent every day, by fluent writers too.

Under pressure, with no time for a sleep, a gap of any size plus reading aloud still catches most of
what read A misses.

A stranger who finds nothing your second read didn't find has read a page that's been properly edited.
