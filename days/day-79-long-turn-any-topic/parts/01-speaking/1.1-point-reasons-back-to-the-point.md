## One-line answer

When you're handed a topic you didn't choose, hold a long turn with one frame. Say your point in one
sentence. Give a first reason with a real example. Give a second reason with a real example. Then come
back to your point in new words: *So that's why I think …* The markers from day 71 join the pieces,
and your voice falls only at the very end.

## The story

At a cousin's house one evening, everyone writes a topic on a slip of paper and drops it in a bowl.
You each draw one and talk until you've said something real. Your uncle draws *mobile phones for
children* and says:

> Yes, it's a big problem these days. Everyone has phones. Even my neighbour's boy has a phone, he's
> always on it, and his mother … actually, his mother works at the hospital, she's a nurse, she was
> telling me about the night shifts …

Ten sentences later he's talking about hospital food, and everyone has forgotten the topic.

Your sister draws *should children do chores?* She says:

> Well, I think they should, from quite young. First, it teaches them that a home doesn't clean
> itself. My son started making his bed at six, and now he notices mess. And the thing is, it gives
> them confidence. When he cooked rice for the first time, he told everyone at school. So yes, that's
> why I think chores are good for them, as long as they're small.

Everyone remembers her point, and both reasons.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **long turn**: a turn of several sentences, where you keep speaking and the listener waits, from
  [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/01-speaking/1.1-well-so-the-thing-is.md).
- **marker**: a small word like *well*, *so* or *the thing is* that shows where you are in your turn,
  from [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/01-speaking/1.1-well-so-the-thing-is.md).
- **example**: one real case that shows your reason is true, from
  [day 61](../../../day-61-giving-opinion/parts/01-speaking/1.1-opener-reason-example.md).
- **topic**: what you've been asked to talk about.
- **frame**: a simple shape you can pour any topic into.
- **wander**: drift away from the topic, one story at a time, without noticing.

The frame has four pieces, and a marker opens each one:

| Piece | Marker | What it carries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| point | *Well, I think …* | your view on the topic, in one sentence |
| reason 1 + example | *First, …* | why, and one real case |
| reason 2 + example | *And the thing is, …* | another why, and another real case |
| back to the point | *So, that's why I think …* | the point again, in new words |

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Speaking tips](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/speaking-tips):
"Always say something. Don't just say 'yes' or 'no'. Explain your answer with a reason." It also says:
"Slow down! It's not a race. Before you speak, think carefully about what to say".
[Taking turns](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/taking-turns) gives
"For example …" and "What I mean is …" for "Making your turn longer".
[Discussion](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/discussion) lists, among
its *don'ts*: "Talk about things that are not relevant to the topic."

**Take one breath before you start.** In that breath, choose your point. Just the point, not the whole
turn.

**The point first, in one sentence.** *Well, I think children should do chores.* Don't start with the
background. The listener needs to know where you're going.

**Each reason carries one real example.** Something you saw, did or know. The example is what makes it
yours and not a newspaper's.

**Come back.** *So, that's why I think …* in slightly different words. This is the sentence that stops
you wandering. When you say it, you're finished, and your voice falls.

**Check each sentence against the topic.** If a sentence is about something the topic didn't ask
about, like the nurse's night shifts, it's the start of a wander. Stop, and say *anyway* and your next
reason.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the wander**: an example that leads to a story, which leads to
another story. The listener loses the topic, and so do you.

The second is **the point at the end**: three sentences of background, and the view only at the
end. Many listeners have stopped listening by then.

The third is **reasons with no examples**: *it's good for them, and it's important, and it helps*.
Three reasons, and nothing to picture.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

In job interviews, in meetings and at family gatherings, people are handed topics all the time. The
ones who answer well often use a frame like this without thinking about it.

Under pressure, with a topic you've never thought about, it's tempting to talk around it until
something comes. Choose a point, even a small one, in the first breath. You can soften it: *I think
so, mostly*.

A native listener remembers two things from a long turn: your point and one example. Make sure both
are there.
