## One-line answer

Ask someone to write ten topics on slips of paper. Draw three without looking, and for each one take
one breath and record a long turn in the frame: point, two reasons with examples, back to the point.
Then play each turn to a listener and ask them to say your point and your two reasons. If they can,
the frame held.

## The story

At the cousin's house, the bowl comes round again. This time nobody's allowed to choose their own
topic. The slips were written by the children.

Your turn: *Is it better to have a dog or a cat?* You've never owned either. You take a breath:

> Well, I think a dog's better for a family. First, a dog gets everyone outside. My neighbour walks
> his every evening, and he knows everyone on our street because of it. And the thing is, a dog shows
> you it's happy to see you. When I visit my friend, her dog runs to the gate. So, that's why I'd
> choose a dog, even though I've never had one.

Your youngest cousin says, *You said outside and happy to see you*. Point and two reasons, said back
by a nine-year-old.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **frame**, **wander**, **topic**: from [part 1.1](1.1-point-reasons-back-to-the-point.md).
- **slip**: a small piece of paper with one topic on it.
- **draw**: here, take a slip without looking.
- **point-and-reasons say-back**: the listener says your point and your two reasons in their own words.

Topics you didn't choose come in three kinds. The frame works for all of them, but the first breath
is different:

| Kind | Example | In the first breath, ask yourself |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a choice | *dog or cat?* | *which one, and why?* |
| a yes/no | *should shops close on Sundays?* | *yes or no, mostly?* |
| an open question | *what makes a good neighbour?* | *what's the one thing?* |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **choosing the easy slips**: you peek, and put back the hard one. The
hard ones are where you learn the most. Draw blind.

The second is **thinking for a long time first**. The longer you plan, the more you try to say. One
breath: just the point.

The third is **the same example for every topic**: your son, your son, your son. Real examples from
different parts of your life make each turn sound fresh.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Being asked your opinion on something you've never thought about happens at dinners, interviews and
work meetings. People who answer with a clear point and a real example sound confident, even when the
point is small.

Under pressure, with a topic you know nothing about, it's fine to say so in your point: *I've never
had a dog, but I think …*. That's honest, and it's still a point.

A listener who can say back your point and both reasons after one hearing has followed a turn that
held together.
