## One-line answer

Draw one topic and record two long turns on it. First, deliberately let it wander: let each example
lead to a new story, and end somewhere else. Second, keep it in the frame. Play each to a different
listener and ask *what was the topic, and what was my point?* The wandering turn loses one or both.
The frame should keep both.

## The story

At the cousin's house, your aunt draws *Is it better to cook at home or eat out?* She starts well:

> Oh, cook at home, definitely. Last week we ate out at that new place near the station, and the
> waiter was so slow, and then my husband's friend came in, the one who went to Dubai, and his
> daughter's getting married now, to a boy from …

By the end, the bowl is back on the table, and the children are asking who's getting married. Nobody
remembers *cook at home*.

Your cousin draws the same topic later, for fun, and says it in the frame: point, *it's cheaper* with
the price of last Sunday's lunch, *you know what's in it* with her son's allergy, and back to the
point. Everybody remembers both reasons.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **frame**, **wander**: from [part 1.1](1.1-point-reasons-back-to-the-point.md).
- **drift sentence**: the first sentence in a turn that isn't about the topic.
- **topic-and-point test**: asking a listener *what was the topic, and what was my point?*
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Version | Starts | Middle | Ends | What a listener keeps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| W, wander (deliberate) | on the topic | a story leads to a story | somewhere else | the last story |
| F, frame | on the point | reason, example, reason, example | back on the point | the point and two reasons |

## Why Vaani needs it

Tomorrow's gate is a recording a stranger listens to, and a wander on a recording can't be rescued by
a question. This check finds your drift sentence, the place where your turns leave the topic, before
the gate does.

**Step two: record two turns.**

1. **W**, deliberately wandering: start on the topic, then let each example lead into another story
   about people or places, and end wherever it ends.
2. **F**, the frame: point, two reasons with examples, back to the point.

**Step four: find the drift sentence in W.** Play it back and write down the first sentence that isn't
about the topic. That's where your real turns are most likely to leave the topic too.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when F's listener gets the topic or the point
wrong. It is **GREEN** when F's listener gets both right, and W's listener gets at least one wrong.

**Step five: the repair.** If F went red, look at where it went wrong. A missed point usually means it
wasn't your first sentence. A missed topic usually means an example ran on into a story. Cut the
example to one sentence, add *anyway* before the next reason, and try F on a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a W that won't wander**. You're too used to the frame
now. Let the example lead you. Follow a person in it: *and his sister …*.

The second is **a listener who hears both**. They know the topic from the first turn. Use two
listeners.

The third, in real speech, is **the wander you notice and then keep going with**. You hear yourself
drift, feel embarrassed, and go on. Just say *Anyway*, and your next reason. Listeners forgive a
drift. They don't forgive a turn that never comes back.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Everyone wanders, in every language. Good speakers wander and come back. *Anyway* and *so, that's why
I think* are how they come back.

Under pressure, a good example makes you want to tell the whole story. Tell one sentence of it. The
rest is for another turn.

A listener who can name your topic and your point after a long turn has heard a turn that stayed
where it was asked to be.
