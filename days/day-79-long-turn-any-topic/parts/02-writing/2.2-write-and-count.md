## One-line answer

Write a real opinion piece of between 140 and 160 words in four paragraphs. Count every paragraph and
write the count in the margin. Do the second read after a gap. Then give it to a reader who might
disagree, and ask them to tell you your view, your two reasons, and the other side you answered.

## The story

The newsletter prints the park letter. The next issue asks: *Should our street have speed bumps?*

You write your piece in the evening. It comes to two hundred and ten words, because paragraph 2 has
two examples and a story. You count each paragraph and write the numbers in the margin: 30, 95, 55, 30.
Paragraph 2 is the problem. You keep the better example and cut the story. The count goes to 152.

In the morning you read it aloud and fix a doubled *the*. Your neighbour, who doesn't want speed bumps,
reads it and says: *You want them because of the school and the scooter accident. You said some people
worry about the noise, and that the bumps are the soft kind. I still don't agree, but I get it.*

That's a piece that works on someone who disagrees.

This is a practice scene. It is not a mistake we have seen you make.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **opinion piece**, **word budget**: from [part 2.1](2.1-four-paragraphs-in-150-words.md).
- **the second read**: reading your page again after a gap, out loud, in three passes, from
  [day 78](../../../day-78-interrupting-politely/parts/02-writing/2.1-wait-then-read-as-a-stranger.md).
- **margin count**: the word count of each paragraph, written in the margin next to it.
- **cut**: take words out to make a piece shorter and clearer.

When the count is wrong, the margin tells you where:

| Margin counts | What it usually means | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| one paragraph much bigger than its budget | an example turned into a story | keep one sentence of the example |
| the total under 140 | a reason with no example | add the real example |
| the opening over 40 | background in front of the view | cut to the topic and the view |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **cutting the examples to hit the count**. Examples are the part that
makes a reader believe you. Cut repeats and background first.

The second is **counting only the total**. The total can be 150 with a 90-word paragraph 2 and a
10-word closing. The margin counts show you that.

The third is **a reader who already agrees**. They'll say it's great. A reader who might disagree tells
you whether your reasons actually work.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Newsletters, application forms and online comment boxes often have a word limit. Pieces that are
counted and cut read much better than pieces that were chopped off at the limit.

Under pressure, with a deadline, it's tempting to send the first draft at 210 words and hope. One round
of cutting is what makes the difference, and the margin counts tell you where to cut.

A reader who disagrees with you, and can still say your view, your reasons and the other side you
answered, has read a fair and clear piece.
