## One-line answer

Make a copy of your opinion piece that reaches a hundred and fifty words deliberately with padding:
the view said again in every paragraph, empty openers, *very very*, and one reason with no example.
Ask two readers to mark every sentence *N* if it says something new and *R* if it repeats. The padded
copy fills with R. Your piece should be N all the way, apart from the closing view.

## The story

A college student in your building is asked to write a hundred and fifty words for the newsletter on
the speed bumps. He has one idea and a word count to reach. His piece begins:

> In my opinion, I personally think that speed bumps are a very very good idea. Speed bumps are good.
> This is because they are good for safety, which is very important. In my view, safety is important,
> so speed bumps are a good idea for our street …

It reaches a hundred and fifty-one words. A neighbour reads it and says, *He said speed bumps are good
eleven times, and I still don't know why*.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly write. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **opinion piece**, **word budget**: from [part 2.1](2.1-four-paragraphs-in-150-words.md).
- **margin count**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-and-count.md).
- **padding**: words added to reach a count that don't tell the reader anything new.
- **N/R test**: a reader marks each sentence N for *new* or R for *repeat*.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

Padding comes in a few shapes, and each is easy to spot once you know it:

| Padding | Example | What it adds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the view in every paragraph | *In my view, speed bumps are good.* | nothing new |
| a doubled opener | *In my opinion, I personally think …* | two words that say *I think* twice |
| *very very*, *really really* | *a very very good idea* | length, not meaning |
| a reason that's the view again | *They are good because they are good for safety.* | a circle |

## Why Vaani needs it

A word count is a target that's easy to hit badly. Tomorrow's gate page and every word-limited piece in
phase 5 will tempt you to pad. This check shows you what padding does to a reader, on your own page.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[An opinion essay](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/opinion-essay).
Its example uses each paragraph for a different point: "First of all, I think that active video games
are a good way to keep fit"; "In addition, you can play them whenever you want"; and only the last
paragraph, "To sum up", repeats the view.

**Step one: make copy P** from your piece in [part 2.2](2.2-write-and-count.md). Take out one reason
and its example, then pad back up to a hundred and fifty with the four shapes in the table. Write
*deliberate* at the top.

**Step two: the N/R test.** Give copy P to one reader and your piece to another. Ask each to mark every
sentence N if it tells them something they didn't know from earlier sentences, or R if it repeats.

**Step three: fill the table.**

| Copy | Sentences | N | R | R outside the closing paragraph |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P (deliberate) | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| your piece | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when your piece has any R outside the closing
paragraph. It is **GREEN** when every R in your piece is in the closing paragraph, and copy P has R's
elsewhere.

**Step four: the repair.** If your piece went red, look at each R outside the closing paragraph. Cut
it, and if the count drops below 140, add a real detail to an example instead: a number, a name, a
place. Give it to a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a copy P that's still good**, because you can't bring
yourself to pad. Pad on purpose, and badly. The contrast is the point.

The second is **a reader who marks the closing view as R and counts it against you**. The closing
paragraph is meant to repeat the view. Only R's elsewhere count.

The third, in real writing, is **the polite padding**: *I would like to take this opportunity to say
that in my humble opinion …*. It sounds formal and says *I think*. Write *I think*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Padded pieces are very common wherever there's a minimum word count. Readers notice quickly and start
skimming, and a skimming reader misses your one good reason.

Under pressure, a few words short of the count, the easiest fix is *very*. The better fix is a detail
in an example. It adds words and makes the reader believe you.

A reader who marks every sentence N until the closing paragraph has read a hundred and fifty words
that were all working.
