## One-line answer

Record the gate take in one go from your gate card. Write out the transcript and mark the view, the
three reasons, the counter-argument and the answer. Then play it to one person who doesn't know your
view, and ask them to list what they heard. The gate passes only if the transcript and the stranger
both find all four.

## The story

After the wedding lunch, a cousin who wasn't at the table asks what you said about the city job. You
play her the voice note you recorded on the way home. She listens once and says:

> You think he should go. Because of the salary, because he'll learn more, and because he can come
> back if it doesn't work. Your aunt thinks it's too expensive. And you said the salary covers that,
> even after rent.

Everything you said, in her words, after one listen. That's the gate.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate card**: the six lines from [part 1.1](1.1-what-the-gate-asks-for.md).
- **counter-argument**, **answer**, **transcript**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-the-gate-asks-for.md).
- **marked transcript**: the transcript with the view, each reason, the counter-argument and the
  answer marked in the margin.
- **stranger's list**: what a person who doesn't know your view says they heard, written down in their
  words.

The gate is checked twice, once on paper and once by a person:

| Check | Who does it | What it must find |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the marked transcript | you | the view, three different reasons, a counter-argument, an answer |
| the stranger's list | a listener who doesn't know your view | the same four, in their words |

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Discussion](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/discussion). Among its
tips: "Say what you really think about the topic and explain why you think that." Its list of *don'ts*
includes "Only give your own opinion but also respond to your partner": in other words, don't only
give your own view; respond to the other person too. Your recording has no partner, so the
counter-argument is where you respond to one.

**Step one: one quiet look at the gate card.** Then put it face down. You can pick it up if you're
lost, but try not to.

**Step two: the gate take.** Press record, set the phone down, and speak from your opener to your last
sentence without stopping. If a word goes wrong, correct it and keep going.

**Step three: the marked transcript.** Play the take back and write every word. Mark in the margin:
*V* for your view, *R1*, *R2* and *R3* for your reasons, *C* for the counter-argument, and *A* for
your answer.

**Step four: the stranger's list.** Play the take once to a person who doesn't know your view. Ask
them:

> What does the speaker think? What are their reasons? What's the other side, and how did they answer
> it?

Write down their answer word for word.

**Step five: decide.** The gate take passes when the transcript has all eight rows and the stranger
names the view, three reasons, the counter-argument and the answer. If either check fails, record a
new take from the card. Keep every take and don't delete the failures.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the take stopped and restarted**. Nine openings and no gate. Record
through.

The second is **the transcript made from memory**. You write what you meant to say. Play it back and
write what you said.

The third is **a stranger who agrees with you**. They'll hear your reasons as obvious and may not hear
the counter-argument at all. Someone neutral, or someone who disagrees, is a fairer test.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate recording. This day was written before your
own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard you make.

## In real life

A stranger's list is exactly what happens when your opinion is passed on: *she thinks he should go,
because …*. If what they pass on is right, your turn worked.

Under pressure, the third reason is the one that goes missing, because two feel like enough. The card
has three lines for a reason.

A native listener who can repeat your counter-argument and your answer has heard you take the other
side seriously.
