## One-line answer

Record take B of the same opinion with the counter-argument said and deliberately left unanswered: no
*but*, no answer, straight to the end. Play B and your gate take to two different strangers, and ask
*what does the speaker think now?* Take B's stranger often thinks you've changed your mind, or aren't
sure. Your gate take's stranger should hear your view, standing.

## The story

At the wedding lunch, your cousin gives his view on the city job: three good reasons. Then he says:

> Some people say the city's too expensive, and rent is very high there.

And he stops. His aunt says, *So you agree with me, then. He should stay.* He didn't mean that. But
the last thing anyone heard was the other side, and he never answered it.

His sister, later, says the same three reasons and the same counter-argument, then:

> But his salary would be double, and even after rent he'd save more than he does here. So I still
> think he should go.

Her aunt says, *Hmm. Maybe.* The view was still standing at the end.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce in a
first gate recording. They are not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **counter-argument**, **answer**: from [part 1.1](1.1-what-the-gate-asks-for.md).
- **gate take**: the take that passed in [part 1.2](1.2-record-the-opinion.md).
- **take B**: a copy of the gate take recorded with the counter-argument deliberately left
  unanswered.
- **last-word test**: asking a stranger *what does the speaker think now?* straight after the
  recording ends.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

The listener remembers the last thing they heard. What comes last changes what they think you think.

| Take | Ends with | What a stranger often thinks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B (deliberate) | the counter-argument | *they agree with the other side*, or *they aren't sure* |
| gate take | the answer, then the view | *they still think X, and they've dealt with the other side* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate asks for one counter-argument **answered**, and the answer is the word that's easiest to
drop. This check proves, with real strangers, that your answer is doing its job.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[Discussion](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/discussion). Among its
phrases for disagreeing, from its transcript: "I see your point, but". The *but* is where an answer
begins.

**Step one: record take B**, deliberately: the same opener, view and three reasons as your gate take,
then the counter-argument, and then stop. No *but*, no answer, no return to your view.

**Step two: the last-word test.** Play take B to one stranger and your gate take to a different
stranger. Straight after each, ask:

> What does the speaker think now? Are they sure?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when the gate take's stranger gets your view
wrong, or says you're not sure. It is **GREEN** when they get your view right and sure, and take B's
stranger gets it wrong or unsure.

**Step three: the repair.** If the gate take went red, listen to your answer. It's usually too short,
*but I don't agree*, which is a disagreement, not an answer. An answer gives a reason: *but the salary
covers the rent*. Or the answer was there, but you never came back to your view. Add *So I still
think …*, record a new gate take, and try it on a new stranger. A new gate take also has to pass
part 1.2 again.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a B that sneaks in an answer**, because it feels
wrong to leave it. Stop straight after the counter-argument.

The second is **a stranger who's heard the question before**. They answer from what they think, not
from what they heard. Use someone new to it.

The third, in real speech, is **the answer that attacks**: *but that's a stupid argument*. It isn't an
answer, and the listener who holds that view stops listening. A fair counter-argument deserves a
reason back.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate recording. This day was written before your
own error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Arguments at family tables and in offices are often lost at the end, not the beginning. The person
who says the other side and then goes quiet gives the room away.

Under pressure, facing someone who disagrees, you'll want to either skip their point or give in to
it. Say it, answer it, and come back.

A stranger who says *they still think X* after your recording has heard an opinion that survived its
counter-argument.
