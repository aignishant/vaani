## One-line answer

The phase 4 writing gate is a two-paragraph argument, one for and one against, with a topic sentence
in each. This part teaches nothing new, because a gate that teaches isn't a gate. It sets out the
four conditions and how each one is checked.

## The story

Your children's school sends a note home: the parents' committee is deciding whether to ban phones in
the school bag. Before the meeting, it wants one page from each family setting out the arguments on
both sides, so that parents can read them and decide.

The page has to work for a parent who's never thought about it, and for a parent who already
disagrees with you. It has to be fair to both sides, and each side has to be findable at a glance.
It's read, not heard. You won't be there to explain.

That's today. Nineteen days of this folder have taught the pieces: *going to* and *will*, the opinion
paragraph, the present perfect, experiences, *should* and *must*, a reply to a problem, the first
conditional, a plan, the passive, a process, the second conditional, the wish paragraph, for and
against, relative clauses, definitions, reported speech, the summary, the second read, and the
opinion piece. None of it is repeated here, and none of it is made easier.

**This is a rehearsal, and it says so.** This day was written before your error ledger had rows in
it. Until the `docs/ERRORS.md` re-read on the checklist is ticked, the checks below are the general
ones rather than your own recurring mistakes, and the gate is not yet a gate.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **gate**: the check at the end of a phase, which the whole phase was for, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-the-page-must-carry.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, which says what it's about, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **for and against**: a piece of writing that gives the reasons on both sides, from
  [day 73](../../../day-73-reasons-for-and-against/parts/02-writing/2.1-two-paragraphs-two-topic-sentences.md).
- **the second read**: reading your page again after a gap, out loud, in three passes, from
  [day 78](../../../day-78-interrupting-politely/parts/02-writing/2.1-wait-then-read-as-a-stranger.md).
- **argument**: here, a piece of writing that gives reasons about a question people disagree on.
- **fair copy**: the final, clean version of a page, from
  [day 20](../../../day-20-gate-self-introduction/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-the-page.md).

The gate wording is in the plan's §7, and it's copied here without a word changed:

> Writing: a two-paragraph argument, one for and one against, with a topic sentence in each.

Four conditions, and all four can be checked.

| Condition | What it means | How it's checked |
| --- | --- | --- |
| an argument | about a real question people disagree on, with reasons | a reader can say what the question is |
| two paragraphs | exactly two, with a clear break between them | you can see it, and count to two |
| one for and one against | paragraph 1 argues one side and paragraph 2 the other, with no slide | a reader marks every sentence F or A |
| a topic sentence in each | the first line of each paragraph says which side it argues | a reader says what each paragraph argues from its first line only |

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the point of the phase on paper. A page that lays out both sides fairly, with each side
findable, is what phase 5's formal letters and reports are built on, and it's the middle of the
argumentative essay in phase 6.

## The rule

There's no new rule today. What follows is the setup.

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[A for and against essay about the internet](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b2-writing/against-essay-about-internet).
Its tips give "One advantage of X is …" and "Another advantage of X is …" for the paragraph in
favour, and "You can start this contrasting paragraph with On the other hand, …" for the paragraph
against. The full essay on that page also has an opening and a closing paragraph. The gate asks for
the two middle paragraphs only.

**Choose the question before anything else.** It must be real, and people you know must disagree.
Good kinds: a rule at a school, a building or a workplace; a change in your town; a decision a family
or a group is making. A question with no real other side gives you a weak *against* paragraph.

**Fill the paragraph plan.**

| Paragraph | Topic sentence (a few words) | Reasons, each with a real example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1: for | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| 2: against | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**What must be on the page**, because these are what the phase taught:

- a title that names the question;
- exactly two paragraphs, with a blank line or an indent between them;
- a topic sentence at the start of each, naming its side;
- at least two reasons in each paragraph, each with a real example;
- no sentence arguing the other paragraph's side;
- commas after *On the other hand*, *For example* and *In addition* at the start of a sentence.

**No third paragraph.** The gate says two. Your own view, if you want to give it, isn't part of the
gate page.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the one-sided pair**: a strong *for* paragraph and a thin *against*
one with no real example. It's two paragraphs, but it isn't one for and one against in any fair sense.

The second is **the slide**: paragraph 2 opens *On the other hand* and drifts back to *for* with
*But …*.

The third is **the topic sentence that's an example**: *Last week my son's phone was stolen from his
bag.* It's a good example and a poor first line, because the reader doesn't yet know which side it's
on.

The fourth is **a third paragraph** with your opinion. It's a good habit in an essay, and it breaks
this gate's wording.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate page. This day was written before your own
error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen you make.

## In real life

Parents' committees, residents' associations and workplaces ask for exactly this kind of page before a
decision. A page that's fair to both sides is the one people bring to the meeting.

Under pressure, writing about something you care about, the side you disagree with gets less care.
The gate reader will notice, and so will any real reader who disagrees.

A careful reader reads the two topic sentences first. If those two lines give both sides, the page is
well built.
