## One-line answer

Write the draft of the two paragraphs in one sitting. Leave a gap, then check it in four passes, one
for each condition, and then the second read for the surface. Write the fair copy into `lab/writing/`
and give it to the real reader it's for. The page passes when every pass is clean and the reader can
say both sides.

## The story

The school's parents' committee collects the pages on phones in school bags. One father's page is a
single block, with the *against* side in the last two lines. Another mother's page has two
paragraphs, but both of them are really for the ban.

Your neighbour's page is two paragraphs. The first begins *One advantage of banning phones in school
bags is fewer fights over them*, and gives two real reasons. The second begins *On the other hand,
many parents need to reach their children after school*, and gives two real reasons. At the meeting,
the chairperson reads her page aloud, because both sides can point to it.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **argument**, **topic sentence**, **fair copy**: from [part 2.1](2.1-what-the-argument-must-do.md).
- **pass**: one read-through of a page looking for one thing only, from
  [day 60](../../../day-60-gate-story-from-life/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-the-page.md).
- **sort test**: a reader marks each sentence F for *for* or A for *against*, from
  [day 73](../../../day-73-reasons-for-and-against/parts/02-writing/2.3-the-against-paragraph-that-agreed.md).
- **cover test**: covering one paragraph and asking what the other says, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-the-line-first.md).

Four passes, one for each gate condition, then the second read:

| Pass | Looks for | Clean when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. the question | a title and an argument about a real question | the title names it |
| 2. two paragraphs | exactly two, with a clear break | you count two, and see the break |
| 3. the sides | every sentence on its paragraph's side | you mark F all through 1 and A all through 2 |
| 4. topic sentences | each first line names its side | reading only the two first lines gives both sides |
| second read | sense, sentences, surface, aloud | nothing trips you |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **editing while drafting**. You fix every sentence as you go and never
finish. Draft first, fix after.

The second is **all the passes at once**. You read once and look for everything, and find the commas
and miss the slide. One thing per pass.

The third is **a fair copy that's checked and never given to anyone**. The gate page is written for a
reader, so give it to one.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate page. This day was written before your own
error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen you make.

## In real life

A page like this, handed round before a meeting, shapes the whole discussion. People quote its topic
sentences back to each other.

Under pressure, the night before a deadline, pass 3 is the one that gets skipped, and it's the one
that catches the slide. Do it with a pencil, one sentence at a time.

A reader who can name the question and the best reason on each side, after one read, has been given a
gate page.
