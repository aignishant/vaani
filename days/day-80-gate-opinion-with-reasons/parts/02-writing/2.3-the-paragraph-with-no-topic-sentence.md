## One-line answer

Make copy T of your fair copy with both topic sentences deliberately removed, so each paragraph starts
straight on an example. Give copy T and your fair copy to two readers. Let them see only the first line
of each paragraph, and ask *which side does each paragraph argue?* Copy T's reader has to guess. Your
fair copy's reader should know both sides from two lines.

## The story

At the parents' committee, the chairperson has thirty pages and no time to read them all. She reads
the first line of each paragraph and decides which pages to bring to the meeting.

One page's paragraphs begin:

> My son's friend lost his phone in March.
> My daughter's bus is sometimes late.

She can't tell which side either one is on, and she puts the page aside. The page underneath begins:

> One advantage of banning phones in school bags is that nothing can be stolen.
> On the other hand, parents need to reach their children when a bus is late.

She puts that one on the table for the meeting.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly write in a first
gate page. They are not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **topic sentence**, **fair copy**: from [part 2.1](2.1-what-the-argument-must-do.md).
- **copy T**: a copy of the fair copy with both topic sentences deliberately taken out.
- **first-line test**: showing a reader only the first line of each paragraph and asking which side it
  argues.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Copy | Paragraph 1 starts | Paragraph 2 starts | What a first-line reader gets |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T (deliberate) | an example | an example | two stories, and no sides |
| fair copy | *One advantage of … is …* | *On the other hand, …* | both sides in two lines |

## Why Vaani needs it

The gate's last condition is *a topic sentence in each*. It's the easiest one to believe you've met,
because your first lines feel clear to you. This check shows whether they're clear to a reader who
reads only them, which is how busy readers read.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23:**
[A for and against essay about the internet](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b2-writing/against-essay-about-internet).
The first lines of its two middle paragraphs, "One advantage of the internet is that …" and "On the
other hand, there are also negative consequences", each say which side the paragraph argues before
any example appears.

**Step one: make copy T** from your fair copy in [part 2.2](2.2-write-the-argument.md). Delete both
topic sentences, so each paragraph begins with its first example. Write *deliberate* at the top.

**Step two: the first-line test.** Cover everything except the first line of each paragraph. Show
copy T to one reader and your fair copy to a different reader. Ask each:

> Which side does paragraph 1 argue? Which side does paragraph 2 argue? What's the question?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when your fair copy's reader gets either side

**Step three: the repair.** If the fair copy went red, look at the topic sentence they got wrong. It
probably names the topic but not the side: *Phones in school bags are a big question.* Add the side:
*One advantage of …* or *On the other hand, …*. Change the fair copy, update the lab, and try the
first-line test on a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who reads the whole page** before answering.
Cover everything but the first lines. The test is about what the first lines carry alone.

The second is **a copy T whose first example gives the side away**: *The best thing about a ban is
that my son's friend …*. That's a topic sentence in disguise. Start with the plain example.

The third, in real writing, is **the topic sentence that promises both sides**: *There are good and bad
points about phones in bags.* It's true, and it tells the reader nothing about this paragraph. Each
paragraph gets its own side in its first line.

These are errors beginners commonly make in a first gate page. This day was written before your own
error ledger had rows in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Busy readers, such as committee members, managers and teachers marking forty pages, often read only the
first line of each paragraph first. The topic sentence decides whether they read the rest.

Under pressure, writing about something you've lived through, you start with the story, because
that's how you remember it. Put the side first and the story second.

A reader who gets both sides from two lines has been given a page with two real topic sentences.
