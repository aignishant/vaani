## One-line answer

The same request can be said on different steps of a ladder. *Can you …?* is the bottom step, for
people you know well. *Could you …?* is the middle, polite almost anywhere. *Would you mind …ing?* and
*Do you think you could …?* are the top, for strangers, officials and anyone you need something from.
The higher you climb, the longer the question gets. Pick the step by who is listening, not by how
much you want the thing.

## The story

Your electricity bill is double what it usually is. At home that evening you ask your brother to
look at it with you:

> Can you check this bill? It looks wrong.

He takes it from your hand. Nobody thinks twice.

The next morning you take the same bill to the counter at the electricity office. There's a queue, a
tired clerk, and a sign that says *Complaints and corrections*. You say exactly what you said at home:

> Can you check this bill? It looks wrong.

The clerk doesn't look up. *Fill in the form*, she says, and points at a pile of paper.

The woman behind you steps up with her own bill and says:

> Good morning. Sorry to trouble you. Would you mind checking this reading for me? I think there
> might be a mistake.

The clerk looks up, takes the bill and reads it.

Your words weren't wrong. They were the right words for your brother, said in the wrong room.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **request**: asking someone to do something they're free to refuse.
- **polite**: said in a way that treats the other person as a person, not a machine, from
  [day 21](../../../day-21-at-the-shop/parts/01-speaking/1.1-excuse-me-and-the-ask.md).
- **formal**: the way you speak to strangers, officials, older people you don't know, and anyone
  at work above you. Careful, complete, a little distant.
- **informal**: the way you speak to family, friends and people your own age you know well. Short,
  relaxed, close.
- **register**: how formal or informal your English is at a given moment.
- **the ladder**: this course's picture for register. The same request can stand on a low step or
  a high one.

Every request you make in English stands somewhere on this ladder:

| Step | The question starts | Who it's for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1, bottom | *Can you …?* | family, close friends |
| 2 | *Could you … , please?* | almost anyone: a shop, a colleague, a neighbour |
| 3 | *Would you mind …ing … ?* | a stranger, an official, someone busy |
| 4, top | *Do you think you could …?* | someone you're asking a real favour of, or someone above you |

Nothing on any step is wrong English. The question is only whether the step matches the room.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Requests, offers and invitations](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/requests-offers-invitations)
gives *could you* and *would you* as polite ways of asking someone to do something, and says *can*
and *will* are "less polite".
[Can, could and would for invitations, offers, requests and permission](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/can-could-would-invitations-offers-requests-permission)
marks *Can you do me a favour?* as "more informal" and *Could you …?* as "more polite", and says:
"We also like to use longer structures in more formal situations". Its examples of the longer
structures are *Do you think you could do me a favour?* and *Would you mind closing the window,
please?*

**Climbing is adding words.** Each step up is longer than the one below:

> Can you check this?
> Could you check this, please?
> Would you mind checking this for me?

Say those three out loud. You can hear the ladder. Each one takes more breath than the last.

**Step 3 changes the verb.** After *would you mind*, the verb takes *-ing*: *would you mind
checking*, *would you mind waiting*. It's not *would you mind to check*.

**A formal request comes with two things around it.** Before it, an opener, like *Good morning* or
*Excuse me* or *Sorry to trouble you*. After it, a reason: *I think there might be a mistake*. Your
brother doesn't need either. The clerk does.

**The voice climbs with the words.** Start the important word higher and let it fall, as on
[day 65](../../../day-65-polite-intonation/parts/01-speaking/1.1-start-high-let-it-fall.md). A
step 3 request in a flat, bored voice still sounds cold.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **step 1 in a formal room**:

> Check this bill. It's wrong.

Or *Can you check?*, with no opener and no reason. Nothing in it is bad English. The listener hears
someone treating them like a machine, and machines follow the rules and nothing more.

The second is **step 3 said wrong**: *Would you mind to check this?* A listener understands you, but
they hear the slip, and in a formal room a slip in the politest words stands out.

The third is **climbing too high with people you know**, which is on the far side of this day's
[failure part](1.3-the-request-in-the-wrong-room.md).

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers change step without thinking. They'll say *Can you pass that?* to a colleague they
sit next to every day, and *Would you mind sending that over?* to someone from another office,
straight after, in the same meeting.

Under pressure, like a queue behind you or a long wait, people drop down the ladder. The clerk has
had a hard morning too. The person who stays on step 3 when they're annoyed usually gets served
first.

When you're not sure, go one step higher. A native listener forgives a request that's a little too
polite much more quickly than one that's a little too blunt.
