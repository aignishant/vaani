## One-line answer

Take five real requests from your own week. Say each one twice: once to someone you know well, and
once to a stranger in an office. Record both. Then play them to a listener, jumbled, and ask *who was
I talking to?* If they can tell every time, your two registers really are different.

## The story

Back at home after the electricity office, your mother asks you to do three things before dinner:
pick up her medicine, call your aunt, and ask the building watchman about the water tank. You say
yes to her in two words. Then you go downstairs and realise you don't know the watchman well. He's
older than you, and you need him to do something that isn't really his job.

You stand at the bottom of the stairs, working out the sentence. It isn't a new sentence. It's the
same request you'd make to your brother, moved up the ladder.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the ladder**, **step**, **formal**, **informal**, **register**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-the-request-ladder.md).
- **opener**: the few words before a formal request, like *Good morning* or *Excuse me* or *Sorry to
  trouble you*.
- **pair**: here, the same request said twice, once low and once high on the ladder.
- **jumbled**: mixed up, so the order gives nothing away.
- **the room test**: playing a request to a listener and asking who you were talking to.

A pair keeps everything the same except the step. Same need, same thing asked for, different room:

| | Informal, to someone you know | Formal, to a stranger in an office |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opener | none, or *Hey* | *Good morning* / *Excuse me* / *Sorry to trouble you* |
| The ask | step 1: *Can you …?* | step 3 or 4: *Would you mind …ing?* / *Do you think you could …?* |
| Reason | only if they ask | always, one sentence |
| Close | *Thanks* | *Thank you very much* / *That's very kind of you* |

## Why Vaani needs it

A register you can only produce when you've planned it isn't much use at a counter. This part makes
the switch a habit, with your own requests. Later this phase you'll introduce yourself at work,
answer an interviewer, complain, negotiate and say no. Each of those starts with choosing the step.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a formal pair that only changed one word**: *Can you check the
tank?* becomes *Could you check the tank?* and nothing else. That's step 2, and it's fine for a
neighbour you know. For the watchman, it's still missing an opener and a reason. A listener doing
the room test will often say *friend*.

The second is **an informal pair that's secretly formal**, where you're so careful that even your
brother gets *Would you mind*. Keep the informal one real. You'll need both.

The third is **the reason that's longer than the request**. One sentence is enough. The clerk needs
to know why, not the whole story.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Most fluent speakers have two or three versions of every common request ready. They don't build
them fresh each time. That's what these five pairs are: ready versions.

With a stranger who's clearly friendly, you can come down a step after the first exchange. Once the
watchman smiles and says *Yes, yes, what is it?*, *Could you …?* is fine. Start high. Come down only
when invited.

A native listener hears the opener before anything else. *Sorry to trouble you* tells them you know
you're asking for their time, and that alone changes how they listen.
