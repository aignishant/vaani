## One-line answer

Take one of your pairs and swap them on purpose. Say the informal version to the stranger and the
formal version to the friend. Play each to a listener and ask *how does this person feel about the
one they're asking?* The informal one in the office sounds rude. The formal one at home sounds cold,
or like a joke. Both are the wrong room, and a listener hears it straight away.

## The story

At the electricity office, you heard what step 1 sounds like at a counter.

That evening you try the other mistake without meaning to. You've been practising step 3 all day, and
when you want the remote control you say to your brother:

> Excuse me. Would you mind passing me the remote, please?

He stares at you. *What's wrong? Are you angry with me?*

You weren't. But to him, a sentence that careful, at home, only means one thing: something's wrong
between you.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the ladder**, **formal**, **informal**, **register**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-the-request-ladder.md).
- **pair**, **opener**: from [part 1.2](1.2-one-request-two-rooms.md).
- **blunt**: direct in a way that lands harder than you meant, from
  [day 21](../../../day-21-at-the-shop/parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-bare-want.md).
- **distant**: polite in a way that keeps someone away. Fine with a stranger, strange with a friend.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

A request in the wrong room fails in one of two directions:

| Swap | What you say | What the listener hears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Too low, deliberate | step 1 to a stranger: *Can you check this?* | *This person thinks I'm here to serve them.* |
| Too high, deliberate | step 4 to a friend: *Do you think you could possibly …?* | *Something's wrong between us*, or *they're joking*. |

## Why Vaani needs it

You can't hear your own register from inside your mouth, because you know what you meant. This part
lets you hear it from outside. Every formal situation in this phase, from the job interview to the
angry customer, depends on the step you choose. It's much cheaper to hear the wrong one here, on your
phone, than at a real counter.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Requests, offers and invitations](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/english-grammar-reference/requests-offers-invitations):
*could you* and *would you* are polite ways of asking; *can* and *will* are "less polite".

**Step one: choose one pair** from [part 1.2](1.2-one-request-two-rooms.md). Pick the request where
the friend and the stranger are the most different.

**Step two: record the swap, labelled deliberate.**

1. **L**, too low: say the informal version, with no opener and no reason, as if to the stranger.
   Start the recording with *To the* and the stranger's role, for example *To the clerk*.
2. **H**, too high: say the formal version, with the opener and step 4, as if to the friend. Start the
   recording with *To my* and the friend, for example *To my brother*.

**Step three: record your right versions again**, F for the formal one to the stranger, I for the
informal one to the friend.

**Step four: the feeling test.** Play the four recordings to two listeners, in a jumbled order. Each
time, tell them who it was said to, and ask:

> How does this person feel about the one they're asking?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** when a listener hears F as rude or cold, or
hears I as distant or strange. It's **GREEN** when F and I both sound right for their rooms, and at
least one of L and H sounds wrong.

**Step five: the repair.** If F went red, it's usually missing its opener or its reason, or your voice
was flat. Add what's missing and let the key word start high and fall. If I went red, you've
climbed too high at home. Cut it back to what you'd really say. Record the repair and try one new
listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an L that isn't really low**. You've practised the
ladder all day, and a *please* sneaks in. Let L be exactly what you'd say to your brother, with
nothing added.

The second is **the listener who hears the words and not the room**. If they say *it's fine English*,
ask again: *If you were the clerk, would you help?*

The third, in real life, is **the drop under pressure**. You start on step 3, the clerk is slow, and
by the third sentence you're saying *Just check it*. That's L, said for real.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Too low is the mistake that costs you. People won't say you were rude. They'll just help you less,
more slowly, by the rules.

Too high costs less, but it isn't free. With friends and family, a very careful request sounds like
anger, or sarcasm, which means saying the opposite of what you mean to make a point. With colleagues
you see every day, it can sound as if you don't trust them.

A native listener places you on the ladder in the first few words. The opener does most of the work.
If you only fix one thing, fix that.
