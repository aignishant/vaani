## One-line answer

To turn an informal message into a formal one, flip five switches: the greeting, the contractions,
the request, the everyday words, and the sign-off. *Hi Ravi* becomes *Dear Mr Shah*. *I'm* becomes
*I am*. *Can you* becomes *I would be grateful if you could*. *Cheers* becomes *Yours sincerely*. A
formal reader sees every switch you forget.

## The story

Your daughter's school sends a notice: the trip fee has gone up, and the form must go back by
Friday. You want to ask for a few more days.

First you message the other parent you know from the school gate:

> Hi Meena! Did u see the trip thing? I can't pay till next week. Are you ok for Friday? x

Then you write to the school office, which means the head teacher, Mrs Rao, whom you've met once.
You start typing the same way, *Hi Mrs Rao!*, stop, and delete it. Something is wrong, and you can
see it's wrong before you can say why.

The two messages ask nearly the same thing. What's different is everything around the asking.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **informal**: friendly writing, for somebody you know, from
  [day 29](../../../day-29-weather-and-small-talk/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-five-parts-of-a-friendly-email.md).
- **formal**: careful writing for someone you don't know well, someone in an office, or someone
  above you at work.
- **register**: how formal or friendly a piece of writing is, from
  [day 19](../../../day-19-can-and-cant/parts/02-writing/2.2-write-a-request-and-a-rule.md).
- **greeting**, **sign-off**: the first line and the last line before your name, from
  [day 29](../../../day-29-weather-and-small-talk/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-five-parts-of-a-friendly-email.md).
- **contraction**: two words joined into one shorter word with an apostrophe, such as *I'm* or
  *can't*, from
  [day 35](../../../day-35-contractions-aloud/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-mark-that-holds-the-missing-letters.md).
- **switch**: one place in a message where the formal version is different from the informal one.
- **surname**: your family name, the one that often comes last, such as *Rao* in *Anita Rao*.

A formal message isn't a different language. It's the same message with five switches flipped:

| Switch | Informal | Formal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Greeting | *Hi Meena!* | *Dear Mrs Rao,* |
| 2. Contractions | *I can't*, *I'm*, *it's* | *I cannot*, *I am*, *it is* |
| 3. The request | *Can you …?* | *I would be grateful if you could …* |
| 4. Words | *u*, *ok*, *the trip thing*, *till* | *you*, *all right*, *the school trip*, *until* |
| 5. Sign-off | *x*, *Cheers*, *See you* | *Yours sincerely*, then your full name |

Every switch is something a reader can **see**. That's why they matter.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-23.**
[Unit 4: Starting and finishing emails](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/english-emails/unit-4-starting-finishing-emails)
lists *Dear Mr Piper,* and *Dear Sir or Madam,* as formal greetings. It lists *Hi Tim,* as informal,
*Yours sincerely,* and *Yours faithfully,* as formal sign-offs, and *Cheers,* and *See you soon,* as
informal. It lists *Dear Tim,*, *Regards,* and *With best wishes,* as ones that work in both.
[An email request](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/c1/email-request)
says: "To make polite requests use the phrase *I would be grateful if you could …*".
[Writing academically: Contractions](https://libguides.hull.ac.uk/writing/contractions) says: "You
should avoid using contractions in academic writing as they are too informal."

**Switch 1: the greeting.** Informal is *Hi* and a first name. Formal is *Dear*, a title (*Mr*, *Mrs*,
*Ms*, *Dr*), the surname, and a comma: *Dear Mrs Rao,*. If you don't know the name, *Dear Sir or
Madam,*. Never a title with a first name, as in *Dear Mrs Anita*.

**Switch 2: the contractions.** Write both words in full. *I'm* becomes *I am*, *can't* becomes
*cannot*, *don't* becomes *do not*, *we'll* becomes *we will*. **Here's a choice, named honestly.**
The academic-writing guide above says to avoid contractions. Not every business writer agrees, and
some learner's pages show formal emails that use *I'm*. This course leaves them all out of formal
writing, because leaving them out is never wrong, and because the phase 5 gate asks for none.

**Switch 3: the request.** In an informal message, a question: *Can you …?* In a formal one, a softer
sentence built from more words, like the softened question from
[day 31](../../../day-31-asking-for-help/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-softened-question.md), but one step
further: *I would be grateful if you could …*. It isn't even a question, so it needs a full stop,
not a question mark.

**Switch 4: the words.** Text-message shortcuts go: *u*, *pls*, *thx*, *ok*. Vague names go: *the trip
thing* becomes *the school trip on 14 March*. Everyday short words like *till*, *get* and *a bit*
often have a fuller partner, *until*, *receive*, *slightly*. You don't need a big formal word list.
You need to notice the short, chatty ones.

**Switch 5: the sign-off.** Formal ends with *Yours sincerely,* when you used their name, and your full
name on the next line. No *x*, no emoji, no *Cheers*.

This is what the two messages look like, side by side, with the switches marked:

```text
INFORMAL                           FORMAL
Hi Meena!          <- 1 ->         Dear Mrs Rao,
I can't pay        <- 2 ->         I am unable to pay
Are you ok for     <- 3 ->         I would be grateful if you could
the trip thing     <- 4 ->         the school trip on 14 March
x                  <- 5 ->         Yours sincerely,
                                   Anita Sharma
```

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **four switches out of five**. The whole message is formal, and then
it ends:

> Dear Mrs Rao,
> I am writing about the school trip on 14 March. I would be grateful if you could give me until
> Monday to pay the fee.
> Thanks!!

A formal reader stops at the last line and wonders which of the two messages you meant.

The second is **a contraction that slipped through**: *I would be grateful if you could give me until
Monday, as I'm waiting for my salary.* One *I'm* in a formal letter is the thing a reader's eye goes
to first.

The third is **the half-greeting**: *Dear Mrs Anita,* or *Dear Rao,*. Either use the title and the
surname, or use neither.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A fluent writer decides the register before the first word, from one question: *would I call this
person by their first name to their face?* If not, the message is formal.

Under pressure, writing quickly on a phone, people write the formal greeting and then fall back into
their message voice. The sign-off is where it shows, because it's the last thing written and the
first thing checked.

A formal reader, such as a head teacher, a bank officer or a manager, forgives a small grammar slip
far more easily than a wrong register. A slip reads as *not a native writer*. A wrong register reads
as *didn't take this seriously*.
