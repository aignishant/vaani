## One-line answer

Choose one real thing you need to ask or tell, and write it twice: once to someone you know well,
and once to someone in an office. Keep the facts the same. Flip all five switches. Then give only the
formal one to a reader and ask them to circle anything that sounds like a message to a friend.

## The story

At the school, Mrs Rao reads your email and replies the same afternoon: yes, Monday is fine. Meena,
meanwhile, has sent back three laughing faces and *same here!!*

Both of them got what they needed from you, in the form they expected. Neither would have liked the
other's version. Meena would have found *Dear Mrs Sharma, I am writing regarding…* strange. Mrs Rao
would have found *Did u see the trip thing?* careless.

You wrote one message, twice. That's the skill.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **switch**, **register**, **formal**, **informal**: from [part 2.1](2.1-five-switches.md).
- **the facts**: what actually happened, what you need, and by when. These stay the same in both
  versions.
- **reason for writing**: the sentence that says why the message exists, from
  [day 29](../../../day-29-weather-and-small-talk/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-five-parts-of-a-friendly-email.md).
- **circle test**: a reader circles every word or line in the formal version that sounds informal.

The two versions share one skeleton. Only the clothes change:

| Piece | Stays the same | Changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who it's to | the person | the greeting |
| Why you're writing | the reason | informal: jump straight in. Formal: *I am writing about …* |
| What you need | the request, the date | the request's words and the contractions |
| How it ends | thanks | the sign-off and your name |

**Step two: write the facts first.** Three lines, no register yet: what happened, what you need, and

**Step three: write the informal version.** To someone you know well. Write it the way you'd really
send it, in English. Contractions are fine. Short is fine.

**Step four: write the formal version.** To a named person in an office, if you know one. Use the five
switches:

1. *Dear* + title + surname + comma, or *Dear Sir or Madam,*
2. No contractions anywhere.
3. *I would be grateful if you could …*, ending with a full stop.
4. No text shortcuts, and every vague *thing* named properly, with its date.
5. *Yours sincerely,* (or *Yours faithfully,* if you wrote *Dear Sir or Madam*), then your full name.

Start the formal message with your reason: *I am writing about …* or *I am writing to ask …*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a formal version that has lost a fact**. It's so busy being polite
that the date or the reason has gone. *I would be grateful if you could help me with the trip.* Help
how? By when? Step five exists to catch this.

The second is **the contraction you can't see**. *I would be grateful if you could let me know, as
it's quite urgent.* You'll read *it's* as normal because you've written it a thousand times. Read
the formal version once only looking for apostrophes.

The third is **the formal version that's just longer**. Three extra sentences of *I hope you are well
and I am very sorry to disturb you and I hope this is not a problem*. Formal doesn't mean long. The
five switches add very few words.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers keep formal messages short. The reason in the first line, the request in the second,
thanks and the sign-off. An office reader has many emails, and the short, clear one gets answered
first.

Under pressure, people send the informal version to the office because it's already written. Write
the formal one from the facts, not by editing the informal one. Editing leaves switches unflipped.

A formal reader notices the apostrophe hunt you did. They won't say so. They'll just read the rest of
your message as careful.
