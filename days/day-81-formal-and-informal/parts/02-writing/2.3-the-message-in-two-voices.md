## One-line answer

Make a copy of your formal message and break it on purpose. Put back three informal pieces, such as
a *Hi*, one contraction and *Thanks!!* at the end, and leave the rest formal. Give that copy and your
real formal version to two readers, and ask each to underline every place the message changes voice.
They'll find the mixed copy's three breaks quickly. Your real version should have none.

## The story

A week later, the school sends a second notice about the trip, and a parent you don't know replies
to the whole list. It begins *Dear Mrs Rao, I am writing regarding the revised itinerary*, and it ends
*anyway lmk, thx!!!*

You read it twice. The formal start made you take it seriously. The ending made you wonder whether
the first half was copied from somewhere. You don't remember what the parent asked for. You remember
the ending.

That's a message in two voices. The reader doesn't average the two. They believe the worse one.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **switch**, **register**: from [part 2.1](2.1-five-switches.md).
- **voice**: here, the feel of the writing: friendly and close, or careful and distant.
- **mixed register**: a message that changes voice partway through.
- **break**: one place where the voice changes.
- **underline test**: a reader underlines every break they find.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Copy | What's in it | What a reader should find |
| --- | --- | --- |
| M, mixed (deliberate) | your formal message with three informal pieces put back | three breaks |
| F, formal | your formal message from [part 2.2](2.2-one-message-written-twice.md) | none |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate checks for "the correct greeting, sign-off and no contractions". A single slip in
any of those is a mixed register, and a gate reader will underline it. This part trains your eye to
see a break the way a formal reader sees it, before a real reader does.

[Unit 4: Starting and finishing emails](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/english-emails/unit-4-starting-finishing-emails):

**Step one: make copy M.** Copy your formal message from [part 2.2](2.2-one-message-written-twice.md)
and write *deliberate* at the top. Change exactly three things:

1. The greeting to an informal one: *Hi* and their first name.
2. One full form back to a contraction, somewhere in the middle: *I am* to *I'm*, or *cannot* to
   *can't*.
3. The sign-off to an informal one: *Thanks!!* or *Cheers*.

Leave everything else formal.

**Step two: the underline test.** Give M to one reader and F to a different reader. Don't tell them
how many breaks there are. Ask:

> Underline every place where this message stops sounding like the rest of it.

your own eye is most likely to miss too. Write it down.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who underlines formal words they don't
know**. *Regarding* isn't informal just because it's unusual. Ask them: *does this sound like a
friend, or does it just sound hard?*

The second is **the contraction nobody sees**. Readers often find the *Hi* and the *Thanks!!* and miss
the *I'm* in the middle. That's the real lesson. Contractions hide.

The third, in real writing, is **the break at the end**. People write formally until the last line,
relax, and sign off *Thanks!* or *Best* followed by a first name. The end is the part that's
remembered.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Most real mixed-register messages break at the start or the end: a *Hi* before a formal body, or a
*Cheers* after one. Check those two lines last, every time, before you press send.

Under pressure, writing on a phone, contractions come back without your noticing. Read a formal
message once only for apostrophes. It's the fastest check there is.

A formal reader doesn't underline anything. They just decide, from the breaks, how seriously you
meant the rest.
