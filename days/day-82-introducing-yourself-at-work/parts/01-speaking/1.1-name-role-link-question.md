## One-line answer

At work, introduce yourself in four moves: your **name**, your **role** (what you do here and since
when), one **link** (why this person will see you again), and a **question** back. *Hi, I'm Ravi. I've
just joined the accounts team. I think I'll be sending you the invoices. How long have you been
here?* The person you're meeting doesn't need your life story. They need to know who you are, what
you do, and what you have to do with them.

## The story

It's your first morning at a new job. The supervisor walks you round and leaves you at the tea
point. A woman comes in, fills a cup and says, *Oh, hello. Are you new?*

You've practised an introduction before. So you give it:

> Hello. My name is Ravi. I am from Nashik. I live in Thane with my brother. I like cricket and
> cooking.

She smiles and says *Nice*. Then she goes back to her desk, and you realise she still doesn't know
what you're doing here. At lunch she asks somebody else, *Who's the new person? Are they a trainee?*

Next morning a man stops at your desk. This time you say:

> Hi, I'm Ravi. I've just joined accounts. I think I'll be checking your team's expense forms.
> Which team are you in?

He says *Oh, good, I've got three for you already*, and sits on the edge of your desk to explain
them.

It was the same person and the same English. What changed was that you answered the questions he
really had.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **self-introduction**: saying who you are to somebody who doesn't know you, from
  [day 9](../../../day-09-introducing-yourself/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-five-spoken-sentences.md).
- **formal**, **informal**: careful and a little distant, or relaxed and close, from
  [day 81](../../../day-81-formal-and-informal/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-request-ladder.md).
- **role**: your job in this place. Not only the name of the job, but what you do and which team
  you're in.
- **colleague**: somebody who works in the same place as you.
- **link**: the reason the two of you will meet again. It's the work you'll do together, or
  something they'll need from you.
- **question back**: a short question at the end, so the other person gets to talk.

The introduction on [day 9](../../../day-09-introducing-yourself/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-five-spoken-sentences.md)
was for a stranger who wants to know *you*: your name, your town, your family. The introduction at
work is for a colleague who wants to know *how you fit in*. They're asking three quiet questions:

| Their quiet question | Your move | What it sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who are you? | 1. Name | *Hi, I'm Ravi.* / *Good morning, I'm Ravi Kulkarni.* |
| What are you here for? | 2. Role | *I've just joined the accounts team.* / *I started on Monday, in the warehouse.* |
| What has that got to do with me? | 3. Link | *I think I'll be working with you on the new orders.* |
| — | 4. Question back | *How long have you been here?* / *Which team are you in?* |

Then *Nice to meet you* closes it. Everything about your town and your cricket can come later, if
they ask.

## Why Vaani needs it

This phase ends with an interview role-play recorded in full sentences. Tomorrow's part on *tell me
about yourself* is this introduction grown longer, and it starts from these four moves. On the job,
every meeting, phone call and new colleague for the next month opens with some version of it.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Meeting a new team member](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/meeting-new-team-member)
is a recorded conversation between two new colleagues. It carries each move. For the name, *Hi, my
name's Peter* and *I'm Carla. I'm new here.* For the role, *I work in the design team* and *I'm in
the marketing team. I started last week.* For the link, *So I think we'll work together sometimes.*
For the question back, *Where do you work?* and *How long have you been here?* The welcome comes
from the older colleague: *Welcome to the company, Carla.*

**Move 1: the name.** First name only at the tea point. First name and surname with somebody senior,
or in a meeting. Say it a little slower than the rest. It's the one word they don't already know.

**Move 2: the role.** Say which team, and say that you're new, with *just* or a day:

> I've just joined the sales team.
> I started on Monday, on the night shift.
> I'm the new receptionist.

*I've just joined* is the way English gives news that matters right now. You don't need to think
about its grammar here. Learn the line whole, as one chunk.

**Move 3: the link.** One sentence, about them:

> I think we'll be working together on the stock count.
> I'll be taking over the orders from Sunita.

If you truly don't know the link, ask for it instead: *What does your team do?* That still does the
job.

**Move 4: the question back.** Short, and about work, not their private life:

> How long have you been here?
> Which floor are you on?

**The shape, from above:**

```text
  NAME      ROLE                  LINK                       QUESTION
  Hi, I'm → I've just joined … → I think I'll be … with you → How long have you been here?
  (slow)    (team + "new")        (about THEM)               (hand the turn over)
```

**Which step of the ladder?** With a colleague at your level, informal: *Hi, I'm Ravi.* With a
manager's manager or a visitor, formal: *Good morning. I'm Ravi Kulkarni. I've just joined the
accounts team.* The four moves don't change. The step of the ladder does.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the day-9 introduction brought to work**:

> My name is Ravi. I am from Nashik. I live in Thane. My hobby is cricket.

Every sentence is correct, and the listener learns nothing they need. They walk away not knowing
your job.

The second is **the name swallowed**. Beginners often rush the one new word: *Hi-I'm-Ravi-I've-just-joined*.
The listener hears *I'm … something*. They're too polite to ask twice, and they call you *the new
one* for a week.

The third is **no question back**. You finish, they say *Nice to meet you*, and there's a silence that
neither of you knows how to leave.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A fluent speaker doesn't wait to be asked. The new person usually speaks first: *Hi, I don't think
we've met. I'm Ravi.* That line is worth learning whole.

Under pressure, in a meeting where everyone introduces themselves round the table, the four moves
shrink to two: name and role. *Ravi, accounts, I joined on Monday.* Nobody wants a link or a
question there, and the next person is waiting.

A native listener forgives an accent at once. What they notice is whether they know what to do with
you afterwards. If they can tell someone else *That's Ravi, from accounts. Ravi's doing our expense forms*, your
introduction worked.
