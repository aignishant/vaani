## One-line answer

Record two copies of your middle-size introduction and break them on purpose. Copy **P** is a
personal one: name, town, family, hobby, and no job. Copy **R** is a rushed one: the right four
moves, but with your name run into the next word. Then play P, R and your real version **W** to two
listeners and run the tell-back test. P loses the role and R loses the name. W should keep both.

## The story

Three weeks in, a new person joins the team next to yours. At the tea point she tells you, warmly
and in good English, that she's from Kolhapur, that she's living with her cousin, and that she loves
old films. You like her at once.

That afternoon your manager asks, *Has the new person in purchasing started yet? I need to send her
the supplier list.* You say *Yes, I met her*, and then you realise that you don't know her name,
and you aren't sure she's in purchasing. You know about the films.

You've been on the other side of this. This part lets you hear it from the listener's side, with
your own voice.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **rushed**: said too fast, so that words run together.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Copy | What's in it | What a listener should lose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P, personal (deliberate) | name, town, family, hobby, no role | your role and your link |

## Why Vaani needs it

In the phase 5 gate interview, the first question is almost always some form of *tell me about
yourself*. The two ways an answer fails there are the two copies here: personal detail where the job
should be, and a name nobody caught. This part lets you hear both in your own voice before an
interviewer does.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Meeting a new team member](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/listening/a1/meeting-new-team-member):
even in a short, friendly first conversation, both colleagues give their team (*I work in the design
team*, *I'm in the marketing team*) before where they're from.

**Step one: record P.** Say *deliberate* at the start of the recording. Then give the personal
version, with your real town and family, a hobby, and no job at all.

**Step two: record R.** Say *deliberate* again. Then give your real four moves, but run your name
into the next word, as if you'd said it a hundred times: *Hi-I'm-Ravi-I've-just-joined-accounts*.

**Step three: record W.** Your real middle size, with your name slow and clear.

**Step four: the tell-back test, twice.** Play P, R and W in a jumbled order to two listeners, one
at a time. After each recording, ask:

> What's this person's name, and what do they do here?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if either listener can't give your name and
your role for W. It's **GREEN** when both get W right, and at least one of them loses the role in P
or the name in R.

**Step five: the repair.** If W went red, find which it lost. A lost name means you need a pause
after it. A lost role means you need the team word: *accounts*, *the warehouse*, *reception*. Record
W again and play it to a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a P that sneaks the job in**. Beginners are so used
to saying the job that it comes out anyway. Then P isn't broken, and the test proves nothing. Check
P for any work word before you play it.

The second is **a listener who already knows you**. They'll give your name from memory, not from the
recording. Use someone who'll listen as a stranger would, or ask them to answer only from what they
heard.

The third, in real life, is **the name said once, at the start, fast**. That's when it's most often
lost. A fluent speaker who thinks it wasn't caught says it again at the end: *…anyway, I'm Ravi.
Nice to meet you.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

At work, people remember a new person by their job first and their name second. So if something
has to go, keep the role. *The new one in accounts* can be found. *The nice one who likes films*
can't.

Under pressure, when you're nervous, the name is the first thing to speed up, because you've said
it all your life. Slow it on purpose. It will feel far too slow to you, and it will sound normal to
them.

A native listener who missed your name will usually not ask. They'll wait to hear it from someone
else. Saying it twice, at the start and the end, saves them the trouble.
