## One-line answer

A formal email has a fixed frame of seven pieces: a **subject line** that names the matter, **Dear**
with a title and surname, a first line that says **why you are writing**, a short body, a **closing
line**, a **sign-off that matches the greeting**, and your **full name** with a way to reach you.
*Dear Sir or Madam* goes with *Yours faithfully*. *Dear Mr Shah* goes with *Yours sincerely*. The
reader sees the frame before they read a word of the middle.

## The story

You want to join an evening computer course at the college near your home. The website gives one
email address: the admissions office. You've never met anyone there.

You write your question well. It's clear and polite, with no contractions. Then you look at the
empty box at the top marked *Subject*, and at the space before your first sentence, and at the space
after your last one. You don't know what goes in any of them. You type *Hello* in the subject box,
*Dear Sir* at the top, *Thanks* at the bottom, and press send.

Nobody replies. Two weeks later, you phone. The woman who answers says they get a hundred emails a
day, and she searched for *computer course* and found nothing from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **subject line**: the short line at the top of an email that says what it's about, from
  [day 29](../../../day-29-weather-and-small-talk/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-five-parts-of-a-friendly-email.md).
- **greeting**, **sign-off**: the first line and the last line before your name, from
  [day 29](../../../day-29-weather-and-small-talk/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-five-parts-of-a-friendly-email.md).
- **formal**, **surname**, **switch**: from
  [day 81](../../../day-81-formal-and-informal/parts/02-writing/2.1-five-switches.md).
- **paragraph**: a group of sentences about one idea, starting on a new line, from
  [day 57](../../../day-57-pausing-right-place/parts/02-writing/2.1-a-new-idea-a-new-paragraph.md).
- **frame**: the fixed pieces at the top and bottom of an email that stay the same whatever it's
  about.
- **reason line**: the first sentence after the greeting, which says why you're writing.
- **closing line**: the last sentence before the sign-off, which says what happens next.
- **matching pair**: a greeting and a sign-off that belong together.

On [day 29](../../../day-29-weather-and-small-talk/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-five-parts-of-a-friendly-email.md)
a friendly email had five parts. A formal email keeps them and adds two: a closing line, and your
full name with a way to reach you. It also makes every piece stricter.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Email writing](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/teaching-resources/teaching-adults/lesson-plans/intermediate-b1/email-writing):
"Use an informative subject line, which clearly says what the email is about", and it warns against
"'hello' as your subject line". [Email](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email) describes the subject
field as "A brief summary of the topic of the message."
[Unit 4: Starting and finishing emails](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/english-emails/unit-4-starting-finishing-emails)
lists *Dear Mr Piper,* and *Dear Sir or Madam,* as formal greetings, and *Yours sincerely,* and
*Yours faithfully,* as formal sign-offs. It says "We normally write a comma after the opening phrase"
and "after the closing phrase."
[Unit 7: Organising your writing](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/english-emails/unit-7-organising-your-writing)
says to "make clear why you are writing" with *I am writing to* and a verb. Its model ends with *I
look forward to hearing from you soon*. It says "A paragraph in an email is often two or three
sentences long".
[yours faithfully](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/yours_faithfully): "used especially when the sender
does not know the recipient's name". [yours sincerely](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/yours_sincerely):
"especially when the sender knows the recipient's name".

**The frame, as it sits on the screen:**

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Subject: Evening computer course – enquiry about fees      │ 1  names the matter
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Dear Sir or Madam,                                         │ 2  Dear + title + surname, comma
│                                                            │
│ I am writing to ask about ...                              │ 3  reason line
│                                                            │
│ ...two or three sentences...                               │ 4  body: one idea per paragraph
│                                                            │
│ I look forward to hearing from you.                        │ 5  closing line
│                                                            │
│ Yours faithfully,                                          │ 6  matches line 2, comma
│ Anita Sharma                                               │ 7  full name
│ Phone: 98xxx xxxxx                                         │    and a way to reach you
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**1. The subject line.** A few words that name the matter, specifically enough to be found by a
search. Say what it's about and, if there is one, the detail that makes it yours: a course name, a
date, a reference number.

| Weak | Works |
| --- | --- |
| *Hello* | *Evening computer course – enquiry about fees* |
| *Question* | *Application reference 44817 – missing document* |
| *URGENT!!!* | *Water leak at Flat 12 – repair request* |

**2. The greeting.** *Dear*, a title (*Mr*, *Mrs*, *Ms*, *Dr*), the surname, and a comma. If you
don't know the name, write *Dear Sir or Madam,*.

**3. The reason line.** The first sentence says why you're writing: *I am writing to ask about …*,
*I am writing to apply for …*, *I am writing to inform you that …*. Say it before any background.

**4. The body.** Short paragraphs, two or three sentences each, one idea each, with a blank line
between them.

**5. The closing line.** One sentence that says what you hope happens next: *I look forward to
hearing from you.* It ends in a full stop.

**6. The sign-off, matched.** This is the pair beginners break:

| If the greeting is … | the sign-off is … |
| --- | --- |
| *Dear Sir or Madam,* (name not known) | *Yours faithfully,* |
| *Dear Mr Shah,* (name known) | *Yours sincerely,* |
| *Dear Mr Shah,* in a slightly less stiff email | *Kind regards,* is also accepted |

The comma goes after the sign-off, and your name goes on the next line.

**7. Your full name, and a way to reach you.** First name and surname. A phone number underneath helps
an office that would rather call.

**Name the trap.** *Yours faithfully* and *Yours sincerely* are the British pair. The dictionary page
above notes that in the United States *yours faithfully* is rarely used, and a plain *Sincerely,* is
common there. Either way, the rule that doesn't change is that the sign-off must fit the greeting.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the broken pair**:

> Dear Sir or Madam,
> …
> Yours sincerely,

A formal reader in Britain or India notices it at once, the way you'd notice a shirt buttoned one
hole wrong.

The second is **the empty subject line**: *Hello*, *Query*, *Mail*, or nothing at all. The email
arrives, and nobody can find it again.

The third is **the reason line buried in the middle**: *I saw your website last month and my friend
also told me about the college and I have always wanted to learn computers, so I would like to ask
about the fees.* The reader has to dig for the question.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

An office worker's inbox is a list of subject lines. They open the ones they can understand from
the list, and they file by the subject. A clear subject line is the difference between being
answered and being searched for.

Under pressure, writing on a phone, people leave the subject empty and sign off with whatever their
phone suggests. Check the top line and the bottom two lines before you press send, every time.

A formal reader forgives a small grammar slip in the body. The frame is different. A broken pair or a
*Hello* subject line looks like a missing button, and it's the first thing they see.
