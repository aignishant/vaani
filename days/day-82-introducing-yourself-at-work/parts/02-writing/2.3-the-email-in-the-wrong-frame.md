## One-line answer

Make a copy of your formal email and break its frame on purpose in three places: a subject line
that says *Hello*, a sign-off that doesn't match the greeting, and the reason line moved to the
end. Show that copy and your real email to two readers as if they were in an inbox, and ask two
questions: *which would you open first?* and *what does the writer want?* The broken copy loses on
both. Your real email should win.

## The story

At the college, the admissions office has a new assistant. On her first morning, she is given the
inbox and told to answer everything about courses. She sorts by subject line. *Tuesday computer
course – fees* goes to the top. *Hello*, *Hi*, *Query* and *(no subject)* go to the bottom, to be
opened last.

When she does open *Hello*, she reads three sentences about the writer's cousin, the writer's old job and the
college's new building before she finds the question in the last line. She answers the other
twenty emails first.

Nobody meant to be rude. The frame just told her where to put it.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The rule

[yours sincerely](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/yours_sincerely): *faithfully* "especially when the
sender does not know the recipient's name", *sincerely* "especially when the sender knows the

**Step one: make copy B.** Copy your email from [part 2.2](2.2-write-one-real-formal-email.md), and
write *deliberate* at the top. Break exactly three things:

1. Change the subject line to *Hello*.
2. Change the sign-off so it breaks the pair. If you wrote *Dear Sir or Madam*, sign off *Yours
   sincerely*. If you wrote *Dear Mr* or *Ms* and a surname, sign off *Yours faithfully*.
3. Move the reason line to the end of the body, and start with background instead.

Leave everything else as it was.

**Step two: the inbox test.** On one sheet, write the two subject lines one under the other, as an
inbox would show them. Show that sheet to a reader first:

> You work in this office. Which one do you open first?

Then give them both emails, and ask about each one:

> In one sentence, what does this person want?

**Step three: check the pair yourself.** Most readers won't know the *faithfully* and *sincerely*
rule, so don't ask them. Put your finger on the greeting of each copy, then on the sign-off, and
check it against the table in [part 2.1](2.1-the-formal-frame-top-to-bottom.md). B's should be
broken. E's must match.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if either reader opens B first, if either
can't say what E wants in one sentence, or if E's pair doesn't match. It's **GREEN** when both open
E first, both can say what E wants, and E's pair matches.

**Step four: the repair.** If E went red, fix the piece that failed: the subject line, the reason
line or the pair. Then show it to a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a B whose reason line is still near the top**.
Beginners keep the question visible out of habit. Move it right to the end, or the test doesn't
show anything.

The second is **a reader who just picks the longer subject line**. Ask them why they chose it. If
the answer is *it says what it's about*, the test worked. If it's *it's longer*, show them two new
subject lines of the same length and ask again.

The third, in real writing, is **the reply that inherits the broken subject**. When an office answers
a *Hello* email, their reply says *Re: Hello*. Now neither of you can find the conversation again.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Frame mistakes rarely stop an email from being answered. They make it slower to answer and harder to
find again. For a job application or a complaint, where you may need to point back to the email in a
month, that matters.

Under pressure, people change the greeting at the last minute when they find the person's name, and
forget to change the sign-off. So the sign-off is the last thing to check, every time.

A formal reader doesn't write back to say *your pair was wrong*. They just notice, and it shapes how
carefully they read the rest.
