## One-line answer

*Tell me about yourself* in an interview isn't a request for your life story. It's a request for
three things: what you do **now**, one or two things from **before** that fit this job, and **why
this job**. Five to seven sentences, ending on the job in front of you. *I work at … . Before that, I
… . That's why I applied for this role: …* The interviewer wants to know why you're the person for
this job, not where you were born.

## The story

You're sitting across a desk from two people at a hotel. The job is front desk, evenings. One of
them smiles, puts down your papers and says:

> So, tell me a little about yourself.

You've practised introducing yourself. So you start with your name, your town and your family, then
your school, then your college. Somewhere around your college, you see the second person look at the
clock on the wall. You haven't said a word about hotels, or evenings, or customers.

A week later, at a different hotel, you say:

> I work at a mobile phone shop in Andheri, mostly with customers who come in with a problem. Before
> that, I spent a year at my uncle's travel agency, booking rooms for families. I've always liked
> the part of a job where someone arrives worried and leaves sorted. That's why I applied for the
> front desk.

The interviewer writes something down and says, *Tell me more about the travel agency.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **self-introduction**: saying who you are to somebody who doesn't know you, from
  [day 9](../../../day-09-introducing-yourself/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-five-spoken-sentences.md).
- **role**, **link**: your job in a workplace, and the reason you'll see someone again, from
  [day 82](../../../day-82-introducing-yourself-at-work/parts/01-speaking/1.1-name-role-link-question.md).
- **long turn**: speaking for several sentences without being interrupted, from
  [day 71](../../../day-71-holding-long-turn/parts/01-speaking/1.1-well-so-the-thing-is.md).
- **interviewer**: the person asking you questions for a job.
- **relevant**: connected to the thing you're talking about. Here, connected to this job.
- **the land**: the last sentence of your answer, which lands on the job in front of you.

The interviewer isn't asking *who are you?* They're asking *why are you sitting in this chair?* So
the answer has three parts, in this order:

| Part | The question under it | Length | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Now | What do you do today? | one or two sentences | *At the moment I work at … , where I …* |
| Before | What have you done that fits this job? | two or three sentences | *Before that, I … . I learned …* |
| Why this job (the land) | Why here? | one or two sentences | *That's why I applied for this role.* / *So this job is the next step for me.* |

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Job interviews](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/business/magazine/job-interviews)
lists "Tell us about yourself." among the common interview questions. It says these questions let
"the interviewer to get to know you and to see if you're the best person for the job". It warns
against "Giving a detailed life history or telling long stories that are irrelevant to the job or to
the company", and it says to "link your experience to the job you're applying for".

**Start with now, not with birth.** Your first sentence is today:

> At the moment I work as a cashier at a supermarket.
> I'm in my final year of a commerce degree.

If you're not working, *now* is still something true: *At the moment I'm looking after my father, and
I've been doing an evening course in accounts.*

**Choose one or two things from before, and only the relevant ones.** Look at the job. What does it
need? Then pick the thing from your past that shows it. For a front desk, it's the travel agency, not
the cricket team. Say what you did, and one thing you learned or got good at.

**Land on the job.** The last sentence names this job and says why it fits. It's the sentence the
interviewer remembers:

> That's why I applied to work here.
> So a front desk role feels like the right next step.

**Stop.** After the land, stop talking. The interviewer will pick the part they want more of.

**The shape:**

```text
  NOW            BEFORE                     WHY THIS JOB
  today      →   1–2 things that fit    →   "That's why I applied …"   → STOP
  (1–2 sent.)    (2–3 sentences)            (1–2 sentences)
```

**Tense, by ear.** *Now* uses the present: *I work*, *I'm studying*. *Before* uses the past, with a
finished time: *Before that, I worked at …*, *In 2022, I spent a year …*. You've heard both on
earlier days. Here they carry the whole shape: present, then past, then back to now.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the life story**:

> I was born in Latur. I did my schooling at … . Then I went to … college. Then I …

The interviewer waits, politely, for the part about the job, and it never comes.

The second is **the list of good words**:

> I am hardworking, honest, punctual and a team player.

Everyone says it, so nobody hears it. The page above says to give examples, not a list.

The third is **no land**. The answer stops after *before*, and the interviewer has to guess why you
applied.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent candidates prepare this answer before every interview and change the *before* and the *land*
for each job. The *now* stays the same.

Under pressure, nervous speakers go back to the answer they know best, which is the school
introduction. If you hear *I was born …* come out of your mouth, stop and say: *Actually, let me
start with what I do now.* A clean restart costs one sentence. Carrying on costs the answer.

An interviewer usually picks one thing from your answer and asks about it next. So only put in what
you're happy to talk about for longer. Whatever you mention, you'll be asked about.
