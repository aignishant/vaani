## One-line answer

Find one real job advert. Underline three things it asks for. Build your *tell me about yourself*
answer so that the *before* part meets at least one of them, and the land names that job. Record it
without notes. Then play it to a listener and ask: *What job was I applying for, and why would I be
good at it?* If they can say both, your answer is doing its work.

## The story

Your cousin sends you a photo of a notice on a clinic door: *Receptionist wanted. Must be good with
patients, able to use a computer, and able to work Saturdays.*

You have your answer from the hotel interview. You say it in your head, and it ends on *front desk*.
It doesn't mention patients or computers or Saturdays. The *now* still works. The *before* and the
*land* need to change.

The same person, the same past. Only the choice of what to tell is different.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **now**, **before**, **the land**, **relevant**: from [part 1.1](1.1-now-before-why-this-job.md).
- **job advert**: a notice, paper or online, that says a job is open and what it needs.
- **what they ask for**: the skills or experience the advert names, such as *good with customers*
  or *able to drive*.
- **match**: a thing from your past that shows one thing the advert asks for.
- **what-and-why test**: playing your answer to a listener, then asking which job it was for and why
  you'd fit.

| Step | You write or say | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | three things the advert asks for | the advert |
| 2 | your *now* | today |
| 3 | one or two *before* things, each matching something from step 1 | your past |
| 4 | a land that names this job | the advert's job title |

You don't need a match for every row. One strong one is enough. Two is better.

**Step two: write only the key words.** Not sentences. Three to five words for each part: *now: phone
shop, customers with problems*; *before: uncle's agency, booking rooms*; *land: clinic, patients*.
You'll speak from these, not read.

**Step three: record without the page.** Look at your key words once. Put the page face down. Record.
Aim for five to seven sentences.

**Step four: listen once for the three parts.** Can you hear where *now* ends and *before* begins? A
small pause and *Before that,* make the join clear. Can you hear the land? If not, record again.

**Step five: the what-and-why test.** Play it to one listener. Don't show them the advert. Ask:

> What job was I applying for, and why would I be good at it?

didn't show a match.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the land with no job in it**: *So I think I would be good.* Good at
what? Name it: *So I think I'd be good on your reception desk.*

The second is **a *before* that's true but doesn't match**. It's a good story about something the
advert doesn't need. Put it away for another interview.

The third is **reading the key words as a list**: *Phone shop. Customers. Agency.* Key words are a
map, not the words you say. Each one should turn into a sentence with a verb.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Before a real interview, fluent candidates read the advert again on the way there and change one
sentence of the *before* to use the advert's own words. If it says *patients*, they say *patients*,
not *customers*.

Under pressure, the *before* part grows. You add a second story, then a third. Two is the most. The
interviewer will ask for more if they want it.

Many interviewers will follow up on your match at once: *You said you booked rooms. What was hard
about that?* That's a good sign. Tomorrow's standard questions are built for exactly that moment.
