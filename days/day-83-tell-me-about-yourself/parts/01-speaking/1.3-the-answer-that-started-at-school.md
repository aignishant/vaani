## One-line answer

Record two broken versions of your answer on purpose. **S** starts at school and walks forward year by
year. **L** is a list of good words with no examples. Then record your real answer, **J**. Play all
three to two listeners, jumbled, and run the what-and-why test. S and L should leave them unable to
say why you'd fit the job. J should not.

## The story

At a friend's office, they're hiring a delivery supervisor, and your friend is asked to sit in on the
interviews. That evening, you ask how it went.

> The first one started at primary school. By the time he got to his first job, we'd run out of
> questions. The second one said *I am dynamic, hardworking, honest, a people person* and then just
> looked at us. The third one said *I run the evening shift at a courier depot, and before that I
> drove for two years, so I know what the drivers are dealing with.* Guess who we called back.

All three were probably good at the job. Only one of them told the interviewers so.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate role-play opens with this question. A gate take that starts at school will run out
of room before it reaches the job. You'll only know your own habit if you've heard it, so this part
makes you hear it.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Job interviews](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/business/magazine/job-interviews):
don't simply list "your hobbies, strengths or work background"; "give examples and use them to show
your personality"; avoid "Giving a detailed life history or telling long stories that are irrelevant
to the job".

**Step one: record S.** Say *deliberate* first. Then start with where you were born and walk forward:
school, college, anything after. Stop after about seven sentences, wherever that is. Don't reach the
job.

**Step two: record L.** Say *deliberate* first. Then say four or five good words about yourself, *I am
… , … and …*, and one sentence that you'd like the job. No examples at all.

**Step three: record J** again, fresh, from your key words.

**Step four: the what-and-why test, twice.** Two listeners, one at a time. Tell them which job it's
for this time, so the only question is the second one. Play S, L and J jumbled. After each, ask:

> From that answer alone, why would this person be good at the job?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if either listener can't give a real reason
from J, meaning a reason that comes from something you *did*, not just a good word. It's **GREEN**
when both give a real reason from J, and at least one of them struggles with S or L.

**Step five: the repair.** If J went red, look at your *before*. It probably names a quality instead of
showing one. Replace the quality with the thing you did: not *I am good with people* but *I handled
the complaints desk on Saturdays*. Record J again and try a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a listener who gives a reason from L anyway**: *You
said you're hardworking.* Ask them: *Did I show you, or just say it?* A reason from a good word isn't a
reason. That's the lesson.

The second is **an S that sneaks forward too fast**. If you jump to your job by sentence three, S
isn't broken. Stay in school and college on purpose.

The third, in a real interview, is **the half-S**. You start at school, notice, and rush forward. It's
better than a full S, and the fix is still the same: start with *now*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Interviewers hear the list version so often that they stop listening at *hardworking*. One example
does more than ten good words, because an example can't be copied from someone else's answer.

Under pressure, the life story feels safe because you know the order. That's exactly why it comes out.
The fix is to have *now* ready as your first sentence, so there's nowhere else to start.

A kind interviewer will rescue a life-story answer: *And what are you doing now?* A busy one won't. Plan
for the busy one.
