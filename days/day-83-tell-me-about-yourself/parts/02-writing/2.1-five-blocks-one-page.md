## One-line answer

A CV is five blocks in a fixed order: **contact details**, a short **introduction**, **work
history** with the most recent job first, **education**, and **references**. Under each job, write
**plain sentences** about what you did: a doing word, the thing you did it to, and a number or
result if you have one. *Served around forty customers a day at the counter* tells the reader
something. *Responsible for customer handling* doesn't.

## The story

You're at the print shop near the station, printing ten copies of your CV. The owner, who runs the
shop with three staff, glances at the first page as it comes out of the machine.

> *Seeking a challenging position in a reputed organisation where I can utilise my skills.* He reads it
> aloud and laughs, not unkindly. *Everyone writes that. I get twenty a month. What did you actually
> do at the phone shop?*

You tell him: you fixed settings on customers' phones, you sold SIM cards, you closed the till at
night. He says, *Write that. Exactly that.*

You go home and look at your CV. There's nothing on it that you did. There are only words about
yourself.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **CV**: a short document, usually one or two pages, that lists your work, your study and how to
  reach you. You send it when you apply for a job. In some countries it's called a *résumé*.
- **paragraph**, **topic sentence**: a group of sentences on one idea, and the sentence that says
  what the idea is, from
  [day 33](../../../day-33-your-job-or-studies/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-paragraph-with-a-spine.md).
- **past tense**: the verb form for finished things, often ending *-ed*, from
  [day 41](../../../day-41-what-you-did-yesterday/parts/02-writing/2.1-add-ed-and-what-happens-to-the-spelling.md).
- **claim and proof**: saying something about a person and then showing it with a fact, from
  [day 51](../../../day-51-describing-people/parts/02-writing/2.1-say-it-then-prove-it.md).
- **block**: one section of the CV, with a heading.
- **plain sentence**: a line that says what you did, with a doing word, in words anyone can picture.
- **reference**: a person who has worked with you and will say so if the employer asks.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate asks for a cover letter, and tomorrow you write one. A cover letter points at the
CV. If the CV says nothing you did, the letter has nothing to point at. The interview on day 100 will
also be built around your CV, because an interviewer asks about what's written there.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to write a CV](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/cv-sections) lists the
blocks. Contact details are "your name, phone number, email address and a link to your work network
profile". The introduction is "a few short lines that sum up who you are and what you hope to do".
For work history, "List the most recent experience first", with "the employer name", "the job title",
"the dates you worked there" and "what you did, usually 2 to 3 lines". Education is the
qualifications, where, and the dates. For references, say "references are available on request". It
also says "You should write your CV to match the job and company you're applying for", and not to
include "your age, your date of birth, whether you're married or your nationality".
[The STAR method](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method)
explains the letters the CV page uses for the work lines: Situation, Task, Action, and Result, "what
happened as a result of your action".

**The page, from top to bottom:**

```text
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ ANITA SHARMA                                  │ 1 CONTACT
│ Phone · Email · Town                          │
├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Introduction                                  │ 2 INTRODUCTION
│ 2–3 short lines: who you are, what you want   │
├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Work history                                  │ 3 WORK HISTORY
│ Sales assistant, [shop], 2023 – now           │   newest first
│   • plain sentence                            │   2–3 lines per job
│   • plain sentence                            │
│ Booking assistant, [agency], 2021 – 2022      │
│   • plain sentence                            │
├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Education                                     │ 4 EDUCATION
│ B.Com, [college], 2021                        │
├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ References available on request               │ 5 REFERENCES
└───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Name the trap: what the page leaves out.** The page above is a UK careers service, and it says to
leave out your age, date of birth, marital status and nationality. CVs in some countries still carry
them, and some local employers expect them. Look at what the job's country and the advert expect.
When the advert doesn't ask for them, leave them out.

**A plain sentence has three pieces.** A doing word in the past (or the present, for the job you're in
now), the thing you did it to, and a number or a result when you have one:

| Doing word | The thing | Number or result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Served* | *customers at the counter* | *around forty a day* |
| *Set up* | *new phones for customers* | *including moving their contacts* |
| *Closed* | *the till at night* | *with no shortfall in two years* |

**A choice, named honestly.** The CV page above doesn't say whether these lines start with *I*.
This course's choice is to drop it: not *I served customers* but *Served customers*. That keeps each
line short and puts the doing word first, where the reader's eye lands. Whichever you choose, do the
same on every line.

**The introduction is plain sentences too.** Two or three lines, with no *reputed*, no *dynamic*, no
*challenging position*. Say what you do and what you want:

> Sales assistant with two years at a busy phone shop. Good at fixing customers' problems at the
> counter. Looking for a front-desk role in a hotel or clinic.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the duty with no doing**:

> Responsible for customer handling and sales activities.

It says what the job was supposed to be, not what you did. A reader can't picture it.

The second is **the empty introduction**, copied from somewhere:

> Seeking a challenging position in a reputed organisation to utilise my skills for the growth of the
> company.

Every word could be on anyone's CV.

The third is **the oldest job first**. The reader looks at the top for what you do now and finds your
first job from five years ago.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A person hiring for a shop or a front desk reads a CV quickly, looking for one thing they can picture
you doing. A number helps most: *forty a day*, *three staff*, *two years*.

Under pressure, applying for many jobs at once, people send one CV everywhere. The page above says to
match the CV to the job. Changing the introduction and the order of your lines for each advert is
enough.

A formal reader forgives a plain CV. They don't forgive a CV they've read twenty times before, with
somebody else's words in it.
