## One-line answer

Write a one-page CV of your own, with all five blocks, for one real job advert. Write the introduction
and every work line as plain sentences. Then run the **picture test**: a reader points at each work
line and says what they can picture you doing. Any line they can't picture gets rewritten.

## The story

You go back to the print shop a week later with a new CV. The owner reads the work lines aloud, one by
one: *Set up new phones for customers, including moving their contacts. Closed the till at night.
Trained two new staff on the SIM card forms.*

He stops at the last one. *Trained them? How long did that take?* You tell him. He nods. *That's the
line an employer will ask you about. Good.*

Your CV has become something a stranger can ask questions about.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **CV**, **block**, **plain sentence**, **reference**: from [part 2.1](2.1-five-blocks-one-page.md).
- **job advert**: a notice that says a job is open and what it needs.
- **picture test**: a reader points at a line and says what they can picture you doing. If they can't,
  the line is rewritten.
- **gap**: a time with no job or study on your CV.

This part is the CV for real, for one advert. It's one page, and it's written from what you actually
did.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to write a CV](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/cv-sections): the blocks,
the most recent job first, "what you did, usually 2 to 3 lines", "write your CV to match the job", and
hobbies that "show some of the skills you have".
[The STAR method](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method):
the Result, "what happened as a result of your action".

**Step one: choose one advert.** Write down the job title and three things it asks for.

**Step two: list what you did, before writing any CV words.** For each job, or for anything you did
regularly, write everything you actually did, in any order, in any words. Don't polish anything yet.

**Step three: turn the list into plain sentences.** Pick the two or three per job that match the
advert best. Turn each into a doing word + the thing + a number or result.

| --- | --- | --- |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**Step four: write the introduction last.** Two or three lines: what you do, one thing you're good at
(with a hint of proof), and the kind of job you want, in the advert's own words.

**Step five: assemble the page.** Contact, introduction, work history (newest first), education,
*References available on request*. One page.

**Step six: the picture test.** Give the page to one reader. For each work line, ask:

> What can you picture me doing here?

| Line | What they pictured | Could picture it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

Rewrite every line they couldn't picture, and show it again.

**Name the trap: the gap.** If you have a time with no job, don't hide it by stretching dates. Put in
what you did: *2022 – 2023: cared for a family member full time*, or *2023: completed an evening
accounts course*. A reader forgives a gap. They don't forgive dates that don't add up.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the line that starts with a noun**: *Customer service. Sales. Cash
handling.* These are the names of skills. The reader still doesn't know what you did. Add the doing word
and the thing: *Handled cash for a shop taking around twenty thousand rupees a day.*

The second is **mixed tenses in one job**: *Served customers. Trains new staff. Closed the till.* Past
jobs are all past. The job you're in now is all present: *Serve*, *Train*, *Close*.

The third is **a number you can't stand behind**. If you don't know it was forty a day, say *around
forty* or leave the number out. An interviewer will ask.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Many small employers never read past the first half-page. So the introduction and your newest job carry
most of the weight. Put your strongest match there.

Under pressure, people pad a thin CV with long lists of skills. One real line about a family shop or a
college event is worth more than a list of ten skill names.

An interviewer will pick two or three of your lines and ask *tell me about that*. Every line on the page
is a line you should be ready to talk about in four or five sentences.
