## One-line answer

After *tell me about yourself*, most interviews ask some version of four more questions, and each has
its own shape. **Why this job?** needs the job plus you. **What's your strength?** needs a claim plus
one example. **What's a weakness?** needs a real one plus what you're doing about it. **Tell me about
a time when …** needs one story in the past: the situation, what you had to do, what you did, and
what happened. Learn the four shapes and you can answer questions you've never heard before.

## The story

You're interviewing for a help-desk job at a bank branch. The manager has a sheet of paper with
questions on it, and she reads them in order.

*Why do you want to work here?* You say, *Because it's a good bank.*

*What would you say is your main strength?* You say, *I'm very hardworking.*

*And a weakness?* You say, *I don't have any, really.*

*Can you tell me about a time you dealt with an unhappy customer?* You say, *I always try to help
customers and keep them happy.*

Every answer is polite and short. Every answer is true. And at the end, she knows almost nothing she
didn't know from your CV. Each question had a shape, and each of your answers was the wrong shape.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **interviewer**, **the land**: from
  [day 83](../../../day-83-tell-me-about-yourself/parts/01-speaking/1.1-now-before-why-this-job.md).
- **opinion with a reason and an example**: a view, why you hold it, and one real case, from
  [day 61](../../../day-61-giving-opinion/parts/01-speaking/1.1-opener-reason-example.md).
- **beginning, middle, end**: the three parts a listener waits for in a story, from
  [day 43](../../../day-43-story-with-three-parts/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-three-parts-a-listener-waits-for.md).
- **standard question**: a question that comes up in most interviews, whatever the job.
- **shape**: the parts an answer needs, in order.
- **strength**, **weakness**: something you're good at, and something you're not yet good at.
- **STAR**: a shape for *a time when* answers. It stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result.

| Question | Its shape | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why do you want this job? | this job's own detail + what you bring to it | *I'd like to work on a help desk because … , and I've already …* |
| What's your main strength? | one claim + one example | *I stay calm with upset people. For example, last month …* |
| What's a weakness of yours? | one real weakness + what you're doing about it | *I used to … , so now I …* |
| Tell me about a time when … | S, T, A, R, in the past | *Last year, at the shop, … I had to … So I … In the end …* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to prepare for a job interview in English](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/level/improve-your-english-level/how-prepare-job-interview-english)
lists "Why do you want this job?" and "What are your strengths and weaknesses?" among common
questions. It says "Your answers will be more persuasive if you include real examples of your work",
and on failures, "you can also say what you learned from it or how you avoid repeating it".
[How to answer common interview questions](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/top-10-interview-questions)
lists "what are your main strengths?", "Do you have any weaknesses?" and "when have you faced a
challenging situation?". It says "Be honest when answering questions on your weaknesses. Say how
you're working to improve them."
[Job interviews](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/business/magazine/job-interviews)
gives *time when* questions such as "Tell me about a time you had to work as a team." and recommends
the STAR technique.
[The STAR method](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method)
explains the letters: "the situation you had to deal with", "the task you were given to do", "the action
you took", and "what happened as a result of your action and what you learned from the experience".

**Why this job?** Two halves. First, something true about *this* job or *this* place, not any job:
*You're a branch where people come in with problems, not just to pay in.* Then what you bring:
*I've spent two years fixing customers' problems at a counter.* Never *for the money*, even when it's
true. Never *it's a good company* with nothing after it.

**Your strength.** One claim, then *for example* and one real case. One strength, not three:

> I stay calm when people are upset. For example, at the phone shop, a man came in shouting because
> his phone had been wiped. I got him a chair, and we got his photos back from his account.

**A weakness.** A real one that won't stop you doing this job, plus what you're doing about it. Use
*used to … so now I …*:

> I used to find it hard to say no to extra work. So now I write down what I've already agreed to
> before I say yes to anything new.

**A time when.** One time, not *always*. It happens in the past, so the verbs are past:

| Letter | What you say | Starts like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S, situation | where and when | *Last Diwali, at the shop, …* |
| T, task | what you had to do | *I had to …* |
| A, action | what *you* did | *So I …* |
| R, result | what happened, and what you learned | *In the end … . Since then, I …* |

**And the last question.** Most interviews end with *Do you have any questions for us?* The answer is
never *No*. Have one ready: *What does a typical day involve in this role?* The common-questions page
above lists "what does a typical day involve?" among the questions a candidate can ask.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the one-line answer**: *Because it's a good bank. I'm hardworking.
I don't have any weaknesses.* Each answer is the right topic and the wrong shape. It has no example
and no detail.

The second is **the fake weakness**: *I'm a perfectionist. I work too hard.* It's a strength dressed
up as a weakness, and it sounds like dodging. The page above asks you to "Be honest".

The third is **the *time when* with no time in it**: *I always help customers.* That's a habit, told in
the present. The question asked for one story, told in the past.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Interviewers rarely use the exact words above. *Why us?*, *What would your last manager say you're
good at?*, *What would you improve about yourself?*, *Give me an example of when …*. The words
change and the shape stays the same. Listen for which of the four shapes is being asked for.

Under pressure, the example is the first thing to go, because making it up on the spot is hard. So
prepare two or three real stories before any interview. One story can often serve as the example for
a strength *and* the answer to a *time when*.

A native interviewer forgives grammar mistakes far more easily than an answer with no example. An
example shows them something. A good word only tells them.
