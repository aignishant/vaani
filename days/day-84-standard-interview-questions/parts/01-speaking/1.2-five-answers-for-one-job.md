## One-line answer

Choose one real job. Get three real stories ready from your own life. Then ask a friend to play the
interviewer and put all five questions to you, *tell me about yourself* plus the four standard ones,
in an order you don't know, and record the whole thing. Listen back with a **shape sheet**. Tick
each part of each shape you actually said. The gaps show you what to practise.

## The story

Two days before the bank interview, your sister agrees to be the manager. She sits at the kitchen
table with a sheet of paper and reads the questions. On purpose, she asks them in a different order
from yours.

The first two go well. Then she asks for a *time when* before she asks for a strength, and the story
you'd saved for the strength comes out as the *time when*. When she asks for a strength, you have
nothing left.

That's the most useful thing that happens all evening. In the real interview, you'll have three
stories ready, not one.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **one story for everything**. You tell your best story as the strength,
and when the *time when* comes, you tell it again. Three stories stop that.

The second is **stopping at *T***. Beginners set up the story and describe the problem well, and then
say *so I solved it*. The *A* is the part the interviewer wanted. Say what you did, step by step.

The third is **a mock with a friend who helps**. If they nod and say *yes, good* after every answer,
you'll never learn what silence feels like. Ask them to say nothing between questions.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

An answer that's only been thought through in your head often comes out shorter and faster than
you planned. Practising out loud, with someone else asking, is the only way to find that out before
the day.

Under pressure, a question you didn't expect makes your mind go blank. Say *That's a good question.
Let me think for a moment*, then go to your story bank. A short pause is normal. A long silence isn't.

An interviewer can write down an example. A good word gives them nothing to write. A candidate with
three real stories leaves three things on the interviewer's page.
