## One-line answer

Record two answers that dodge their question on purpose. **H** answers *tell me about a time when*
with a habit in the present: *I always …*. **P** answers *what's a weakness?* with a strength in
disguise: *I'm a perfectionist.* Then record your real answers to both, **R1** and **R2**. Play all four
to two listeners with the question read first, and ask: *Did that answer the question?* The dodges
should fail. Your real ones shouldn't.

## The story

After the bank interview, you meet a friend who interviews people for her shop. You tell her how you
answered each question. She stops you at two of them.

> You said *I always help customers*. She asked for *a time*. That's not a time, that's a slogan. And
> *I'm too hardworking* for a weakness? Everyone says that. I stop listening at *perfectionist*.

Nothing you said was wrong English. It was an answer to a question nobody had asked.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **shape**, **STAR**, **weakness**: from [part 1.1](1.1-four-questions-four-shapes.md).
- **story bank**: from [part 1.2](1.2-five-answers-for-one-job.md).
- **habit**: something you do again and again. *I always …* describes a habit.
- **dodge**: an answer that avoids the question while sounding like an answer.
- **did-it-answer test**: a listener hears the question, then the answer, and says whether it
  answered.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to answer common interview questions](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/top-10-interview-questions):
"Be honest when answering questions on your weaknesses. Say how you're working to improve them."
[The STAR method](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/interview-advice/the-star-method):
the situation, the task, "the action you took", and the result.

**Step one: record H.** Say *deliberate*. Then answer *Tell me about a time when something went wrong*
with a habit: *I always stay calm and I always solve the problem …*. Keep it in the present. Use no
single time.

**Step two: record R1.** One story from your bank, in the past, with all four STAR parts.

**Step three: record P.** Say *deliberate*. Answer *What would you say is a weakness?* with *I'm a
perfectionist*, or *I work too hard*.

**Step four: record R2.** A real weakness that won't stop you doing this job, with *used to … so now
I …*.

**Step five: the did-it-answer test.** Two listeners, one at a time. For each of the four recordings,
say the question aloud yourself, then play the answer. Ask:

> Did that answer the question? Yes, no, or half?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if either listener says *no* or *half* to R1 or

**Step six: the repair.** If R1 went red, listen for the verbs. If you hear *I always* or *I usually*,
you slipped into a habit. Give it a day and a place. If R2 went red, the weakness was probably too
small or too clever. Pick a real one, and say what you're doing about it. Record again, and try a new
listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an R1 that starts well and slides into habit**: *Last
month a customer … and I always try to …*. The listener says *half*. Stay in that one day until *R*.

The second is **an R2 weakness that would stop you doing the job**: *I'm not good with people*, for a
help desk. The weakness should be real, and it should be one you can work round in this job.

The third, in a real interview, is **answering the question you prepared instead of the one asked**.
You hear *time*, and the story about your strength comes out. Listen to the last words of the
question. They tell you the shape.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Interviewers often ask a follow-up when an answer dodges: *Yes, but can you think of one particular
time?* That's a second chance, and it's also a sign the first answer didn't land. Take it: *Yes, sorry,
last March …*.

Under pressure, the present tense feels safer, because a habit needs no details. The past needs a
day, a place and a person. That's exactly why it's convincing.

A native interviewer can hear a dodge without being able to say why. What they notice is that they
have nothing to write down.
