## One-line answer

Write a complete cover letter for one real advert: four paragraphs, the formal frame, no
contractions. Then run two checks. First, underline every advert word in your letter. There should be
at least two. Second, give the letter and the advert to one reader and ask: *From this letter, give me
two reasons to interview this person.* If they find two, the letter works.

## The story

That evening you write to the pharmacy yourself. You put the advert beside the page: *counter
assistant, good with elderly customers, basic computer skills, weekends*.

Your first draft says *I am a hardworking person with good communication skills.* You look at the
advert and find neither *hardworking* nor *communication* in it. You cross it out and write about the
Saturdays you spent at your aunt's medical store, reading out dosage labels to customers who'd left
their glasses at home.

The advert said *elderly customers*. Now your letter does too, with something you actually did.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the matches that are qualities**: *I am good with elderly customers.*
It's the right advert word and no proof. Add what you did: *On Saturdays I read dosage labels aloud to
customers who could not read the small print.*

The second is **a contraction in the close**. Beginners relax at the end: *I'd be glad to attend an
interview.* That's the most common place for an apostrophe to slip back in. Write *I would*.

The third is **a letter longer than one page**. Four paragraphs of two or three sentences each fits
easily. If yours doesn't, a paragraph is doing two jobs.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

If the advert asks you to apply by email, the cover letter usually becomes the email itself, with the
CV attached. The page above says a cover letter can be written as an email. The four paragraphs don't
change.

Under pressure, applicants skip the name hunt and write *Dear Sir or Madam*. It's correct. But if a
name was easy to find, a reader may notice that you didn't look.

A formal reader reads your match paragraph and then turns to your CV to check it. If the CV says the
same thing, you've told them something true twice. That's the point.
