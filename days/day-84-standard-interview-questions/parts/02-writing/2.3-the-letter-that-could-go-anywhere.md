## One-line answer

Make a copy of your cover letter that could go to any employer, on purpose. Take out the job title,
the place, the advert words and the things you did, and put in general phrases. Then run the **swap
test**. Give two readers that copy and your real letter, plus a second, different job advert, and ask:
*Could this letter be sent for that other job, unchanged?* The general copy should fit anywhere.
Your real letter should not.

## The story

At the pharmacy, your neighbour pulls out two letters from the same week. They're from different
people, and the words are almost identical:

> I am writing to apply for the vacancy in your esteemed organisation. I am a hardworking and
> dedicated individual with excellent communication skills. I am confident I will be an asset to your
> company.

> Somebody's selling this, she says. It came to me, and it'll have gone to the petrol pump and the
> bank too. It doesn't say *pharmacy* anywhere.

A letter that fits every job tells the reader it wasn't written for theirs.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **cover letter**, **advert words**, **tailored**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-four-paragraphs-three-jobs.md).
- **two-reasons test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-cover-letter.md).
- **general**: true of any job or any place, so it tells the reader nothing about this one.
- **swap test**: a reader decides whether a letter could be sent, unchanged, for a different job.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Copy | What's in it | Swap test should say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| G, general (deliberate) | no job title, no place, no advert words, no things you did | *Yes, it could go anywhere.* |
| L, your letter | your real letter from [part 2.2](2.2-write-your-cover-letter.md) | *No, it's for this job.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

A gate cover letter can have a perfect frame and still be a letter to nobody. The phase 5 gate checks
the greeting, the sign-off and the contractions. A real reader checks whether the letter is for them.
This part makes sure yours passes both.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to write a cover letter](https://nationalcareers.service.gov.uk/careers-advice/covering-letter)
says to "write a new one for every job you apply for and make sure it's tailored to the company and
the specific role", to "match the keywords used by the employer in their job advert", and to "back up
any statements you make with facts".

**Step one: make copy G.** Copy your letter, and write *deliberate* at the top. Keep the frame: the
greeting, the four paragraphs and the sign-off. Then:

1. In ¶1, replace the job title and the place with *the vacancy in your organisation*.
2. In ¶2 and ¶3, replace every advert word and every thing you did with general phrases: *hardworking*,
   *excellent communication skills*, *an asset to your company*.
3. Leave ¶4 as it is.

**Step two: find a second advert.** A real one, for a different kind of job at a different place.

**Step three: the swap test.** Give each reader copy G *or* your letter L, never both, together with
the second advert. Ask:

> Could this letter be sent for this other job, without changing a word?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if L's reader says *yes*, meaning your real
letter could go to the other job unchanged. It's **GREEN** when L's reader says *no* and names at least
one word that stopped it, and G's reader says *yes*.

**Step four: the repair.** If L went red, the words that should have stopped the swap aren't there. Put
the job title and the place in ¶1, and one advert word and one thing you did in ¶2. Then try a new
reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a G that keeps the place name** out of habit. One
*Station Road* and the reader says *no*. Take every name out.

The second is **a reader who says *no* to G because of your name**. Your name at the bottom doesn't
count. Ask them to judge only the four paragraphs.

The third, in real applications, is **the half-tailored letter**. The first paragraph names the job,
and the rest is general. It passes a quick look and fails a careful reader. ¶2 is where the letter
proves it was written for them.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

General cover letters are easy to find and easy to copy, which is exactly why they don't work. Employers
who hire often have seen the same sentences many times.

Under pressure, with many applications to send, keep your real letter as a base and change three
things for each job: the job and place in ¶1, the advert words and matches in ¶2, and the reason in
¶3. That's tailored enough, and it's quick.

A formal reader doesn't reject a general letter for being general. They just move on to the next
envelope, and there are forty.
