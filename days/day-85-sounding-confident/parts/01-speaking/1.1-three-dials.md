## One-line answer

A listener hears *confident* from three things you can control. **Volume:** your last word is as
loud as your first, with no fading at the end. **Pace:** you slow down on the words that matter, such
as a name, a number or a day. **Pause:** where you need to think, you're silent instead of saying *um*.
None of them needs better English. They need the English you already have, said on purpose.

## The story

Your housing society has a meeting in the hall downstairs, and you've agreed to tell everyone when the
water tanks will be cleaned. You know exactly what to say. You stand up and say it the way you'd say it
to your neighbour:

> Um, so the tank cleaning is, um, on Saturday the fourteenth, so there'll be no water from, um, nine
> till about two …

Your voice drops at the end of every sentence. You speed up to get it over with. From the back of the
hall, someone calls *Sorry, which Saturday?* Then someone else asks *Till when?*

The man who speaks after you says less, in simpler English, and nobody asks him anything. He isn't
louder than you all the time. He's louder at the ends. He slows down on the dates. And when he loses
his place, he just stops for a second and then carries on.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **pause**: a short silence while speaking, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/01-speaking/1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md).
- **chunk**: a group of words said together as one piece, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/01-speaking/1.1-where-the-pauses-go.md).
- **the tune falls**: the voice going down at the end of a statement, from
  [day 24](../../../day-24-voice-up-and-down/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md).
- **three lengths of silence**: the short, middle and long pauses, from
  [day 57](../../../day-57-pausing-right-place/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-lengths-of-silence.md).
- **volume**: how loud your voice is.
- **pace**: how fast you speak.
- **filler**: a sound or word that fills a silence and carries no meaning, such as *um*, *uh*, *like*
  or *you know*.
- **fading**: getting quieter towards the end of a sentence.
- **key word**: the word the listener most needs: a name, a number, a day, a place.
- **dial**: here, one thing about your voice you can turn up or down on purpose.

| Dial | What a nervous speaker does | What a confident speaker does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Volume | starts loud, fades at the end | keeps the last word as loud as the first |
| Pace | speeds up, fastest on the numbers | slows down on the key words |
| Pause | fills every gap with *um* | stays silent, then goes on |

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Oral presentation](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/oral-presentation)
says: "Speak clearly and at the right volume." and "Pause from time to time and don't speak too
quickly. This allows the listener to understand your ideas."
[Speaking tips](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/speaking-tips) says:
"Slow down! It's not a race. Before you speak, think carefully about what to say and speak a little
slower than normal."
[High rising terminal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uptalk) describes statements that "end with a
rising pitch similar to that typically found in yes/no questions". It reports that some have claimed it
shows "a speaker's insecurities". It also reports research that "its meaning is highly situational".

**Dial 1: volume to the last word.** English statements usually end with the tune falling. A falling
tune isn't a fading voice. The pitch goes down, and the loudness stays. Push a little more breath into
the last word of each sentence:

```text
   pitch:   ─────────╲
   volume:  ▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮     ← same to the end        confident
   volume:  ▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▮▯▯▯     ← fading on the last words  nervous
            the tank cleaning is on SATURDAY the FOURTEENTH
```

**Dial 2: slow on the key word.** Find the one or two words in each sentence that the listener must
not miss. Say those slower and a little louder. The small words around them can stay at normal speed:

> The tank cleaning is on … **Saturday … the fourteenth.**
> There'll be no water from **nine** until **two**.

**Dial 3: pause, not um.** When you need to think, close your mouth and breathe. A silence of one breath
sounds like you're choosing your words. An *um* sounds like you've lost them. Pausing just *before* a
key word also makes the listener lean in for it.

**About the rising end.** If your voice goes up at the end of a statement, some listeners hear a
question, or hear you as unsure. The reference page above says this isn't always true, and that it
depends on the situation. So this is a choice, not a rule. In an interview or a complaint, let your
statements fall. Then nobody has to wonder.

**Name the trap.** The dials fight each other at first. When you slow down, you'll want to go quieter.
When you get louder, you'll want to speed up. Practise one dial at a time.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the fade**:

> The meeting is on Thursday at the community ha…

The listener hears *Thursday*, then a mumble. The fact they needed was in the last word, and they lost
it.

The second is **the race to the number**. Nervous speakers speed up exactly where they should slow
down, on the date, the price or the reference number.

The third is **the um chain**: *um, so, um, basically, um …*. Each filler is small. Five in one sentence
tell the listener you're not sure of what you're saying, even when you are.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers use fillers too. Everyone does. The difference is how many, and where. One *um* before
a hard sentence is normal. Fillers in front of every key word make the key words hard to find.

Under pressure, in an interview or at a counter with a queue behind you, all three dials go the wrong
way together: you get quieter, faster and more *um*. So practise the dials when you're calm. Then
they're habits before you need them.

A native listener doesn't say *you sound nervous*. They say *sorry, which Saturday?*, or they stop
listening. They notice a fade and a race more than an accent.
