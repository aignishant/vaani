## One-line answer

Write a real announcement of five sentences with at least three key words. Mark the key words and the
pauses. Record it three times, each take with one more dial turned. Then run the **far-room test**. A
listener stands in another room with the door open and writes down the key words as they hear them.
If they get every key word from your last take, the dials are working.

## The story

Before the next society meeting, you practise at home. Your son sits in the kitchen while you speak
from the bedroom, with the door open between you. Your first try, he writes *Saturday … something …
from nine*. On your third try, with the dates slow, the ends loud and a breath before each number, he
writes *Saturday the fourteenth, nine until two, fill buckets the night before*.

You didn't change a word. You changed how you said the words that mattered.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **volume**, **pace**, **pause**, **filler**, **fading**, **key word**, **dial**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-three-dials.md).
- **announcement**: a short piece of news or information given to a group.
- **marking**: writing small signs on your text to show where to slow down and where to pause.
- **far-room test**: a listener in another room writes down the key words they hear.

The marks you'll use:

| Mark | Means |
| --- | --- |
| **bold** or underline | a key word: slow and clear |
| `/` | a short pause, one breath |
| `//` | a longer pause, at the end of one idea |
| an arrow down at a full stop | keep the volume to the last word, and let the pitch fall |

## Why Vaani needs it

Marking a text and practising the dials on it is how you'll get ready for the presentations on days 89
and 90. It's also how you'll prepare the answers you know you'll need in the phase 5 gate interview. A
marked text turns a habit you'd like into a habit you have.

**Step one: write a real announcement.** Five sentences for a real group: your family, your building,
your class, your team at work. It needs at least three key words: a day, a time, a place, a number or a
name.

**Step two: mark it.** Bold every key word. Put `/` before each one and `//` at the end of each idea.
For example, a line might be marked like this:

> The cleaning is on / **Saturday** / **the fourteenth**. //

**Step three: three takes.**

| Take | Dials on | What to listen for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | volume | is the last word as loud as the first? |
| 2 | volume + pace | are the bold words slower than the rest? |
| 3 | volume + pace + pause | is every gap silent, with no *um*? |

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **marking everything bold**. If every word is key, none is. Two per
sentence at most.

The second is **a pause that's too short to hear**. Beginners stop for a moment so tiny it disappears.
A pause is one full breath in and out.

The third is **slowing the whole thing down**, so the key words are no slower than anything else. Only
the key words slow. Everything else can stay at your normal speed.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

People who speak to groups often mark their notes like this, with a few bold words and a slash for a
breath. The marks are a way of remembering how to say the text, not what to say.

Under pressure, the pauses go first, because silence feels long when you're nervous. A pause that
feels long to you usually sounds normal to the listener. Trust the mark.

In a big room, the people at the back decide whether you were clear. The far-room test puts your
listener at the back, on purpose.
