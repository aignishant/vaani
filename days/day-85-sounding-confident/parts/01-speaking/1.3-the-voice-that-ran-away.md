## One-line answer

Record your announcement with all three dials turned the wrong way on purpose: fast, fading, and full
of *um*. Call it take **N**. Then record your take **C** with the dials right. Play both to two
listeners and ask two things: *How sure did the speaker sound?* and *What were the key facts?* N should
sound unsure and lose facts. C should sound sure and keep them all.

## The story

At the society meeting, your turn comes after the secretary's. You've practised. Then the secretary
says *And please keep it short, we're running late*, and something in you speeds up. The first
sentence comes out fast, the second fades, and there's an *um* in front of the date.

You notice, stop, breathe, and say the date again, slowly. Afterwards a neighbour says *Good that you
said the date twice. I missed it the first time.*

Pressure turns all three dials the wrong way at once. This part lets you hear what that sounds like
while nothing depends on it.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **volume**, **pace**, **pause**, **filler**, **fading**, **key word**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-three-dials.md).
- **marking**, **far-room test**: from [part 1.2](1.2-one-announcement-three-takes.md).
- **sure-or-unsure test**: a listener says how sure the speaker sounded, and writes the key facts.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Take | Dials | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| N, nervous (deliberate) | fast, fading, *um* before key words | unsure; key facts missing |
| C, confident | loud to the end, slow on key words, silent pauses | sure; all key facts |

In the phase 5 gate interview, the pressure is real. If you've never heard your own voice run away,
you won't recognise it when it happens. This part puts the running-away voice on your phone, where you
can hear it calmly.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if either listener misses a key fact from C, or

N scores lower than C for at least one listener.

**Step four: the repair.** If C went red, ask the listener which fact they missed. Find it in your

listener.

**Step five: the rescue line.** Practise the thing to do when your voice runs away in real life: stop,
breathe, and say the key fact again, slowly: *Sorry, let me say that again. Saturday … the fourteenth.*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an N that's too good**. Beginners can't bring
themselves to fade on purpose. Turn it right down at the end, as if you're walking away from the
listener.

The second is **a listener who scores the English, not the voice**. Say: *Don't judge the words. Just
say how sure the voice sounded.*

The third, in real life, is **not noticing the run until it's over**. The first signal is usually
breathing: you stop taking breaths between sentences. When you notice you haven't breathed, pause.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Confident speakers get nervous too. The difference is that they have a rescue. A stop, a breath and a
key fact said again sounds careful, not nervous.

Under pressure, the first sentence is the hardest. Once it's out with a steady voice, the rest usually
follows. So the first sentence is the one to practise most.

A native listener forgives a nervous start and remembers a clear finish. If your last sentence is loud,
slow on the key word and followed by a real pause, that's what stays with them.
