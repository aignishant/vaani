## One-line answer

Before you write a verb about the past, ask two questions. **Is the time finished?** If there's a date,
*ago*, *last …*, or a job you've left, use the past simple: *I worked there from 2019 to 2022.* **Is it
still true now, or is the result here now?** If you're still doing it, or it's *since* or *for* up to
today, or you're giving news with no time, use the present perfect: *I have worked here since 2022.* /
*I have attached my CV.* On paper, the verb tells the reader whether you still have the job.

## The story

You're filling in an online job application. In the box marked *Current employment*, you type:

> I worked at Sharma Electronics for three years.

The next week, the person who calls you from the company says, *I see you've left Sharma Electronics.
Are you available to start at once?* You haven't left. You're still there, and you need to give a
month's notice. It takes the whole call to sort out.

Your sentence was correct English. It just said something you didn't mean. *I worked there for three
years* means those three years are over. You meant *I have worked there for three years*: three years
up to today, and still going.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **present perfect**: *have* or *has* with the third form of the verb, as in *I have worked* or *she
  has sent*, from
  [day 63](../../../day-63-weekend-and-year-plans/parts/02-writing/2.1-have-or-has-and-the-third-form.md).
- **past participle**: the third form of a verb, the one after *have*: *worked*, *sent*, *done*, from
  [day 63](../../../day-63-weekend-and-year-plans/parts/02-writing/2.1-have-or-has-and-the-third-form.md).
- **past simple**: the past form for a finished action, often ending *-ed*: *worked*, *sent*, *did*, from
  [day 41](../../../day-41-what-you-did-yesterday/parts/02-writing/2.1-add-ed-and-what-happens-to-the-spelling.md).
- **frame and detail**: a present-perfect sentence with no time, followed by past-simple details with a
  time, from [day 64](../../../day-64-have-you-ever-experiences/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-frame-and-the-detail.md).
- **finished time**: a time that is over: *in 2019*, *last year*, *two years ago*, *from 2019 to 2022*.
- **unfinished time**: a time that runs up to now: *since 2022*, *for three years* (and still), *this
  year*, *so far*.
- **result now**: a past action whose effect is here today, like an attached file or a received letter.

On [day 64](../../../day-64-have-you-ever-experiences/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-frame-and-the-detail.md)
you used the present perfect for experiences in your whole life. Today is about the two other places
it appears on work paper: things still going, and results now.

## Why Vaani needs it

Your CV, your cover letter, the phase 5 gate letter, and every status update on day 88 say what you did
and what you still do. One wrong tense can tell an employer you've left a job you still have, or that
a task is still open when it's done.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Present perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect)
says: "We also use the present perfect to talk about unfinished states, especially with *for*, *since*
and *how long*." It says: "If we say when something happened, or we feel that that part of our life is
finished, we use the past simple", with "We visited Russia for the first time in 1992." And it says:
"We can use the present perfect to talk about a past action that has a result in the present."

**The two questions, as a path:**

```text
   A verb about the past …
          │
          ├── Is there a finished time? (in 2019, last year, ago, from…to, a job I left)
          │        └── YES → PAST SIMPLE          I worked there from 2019 to 2022.
          │
          └── Is it still true now, or is the result here now?
                   ├── still going (since, for … up to now)  → PRESENT PERFECT
                   │                                          I have worked here since 2022.
                   └── news, result now, no time given      → PRESENT PERFECT
                                                              I have attached my CV.
```

**Signal words.** These are the words a reader's eye catches:

| Past simple goes with | Present perfect goes with |
| --- | --- |
| *in 2019*, *last month*, *two years ago*, *yesterday* | *since 2022*, *since I joined* |
| *from 2019 to 2022* | *for three years* (and still) |
| *when I was at college* | *so far*, *this year*, *already*, *just*, *yet* |

**For is the trap.** *For* goes with both, and the verb decides the meaning:

| Sentence | What the reader understands |
| --- | --- |
| *I worked at Sharma Electronics for three years.* | Those three years are over. You've left. |
| *I have worked at Sharma Electronics for three years.* | Three years up to today. You're still there. |

**Since takes the present perfect, not the present.** *Since 2022* counts up to now, so it needs *have*:
*I have worked here since 2022*. Not *I work here since 2022*, and not *I am working here since 2022*.

**Result now, in emails.** When the result is in front of the reader, use the present perfect with no
time: *I have attached my CV.* / *We have received your application.* / *I have completed the form.*
Once you add a finished time, switch: *I sent the form on Monday.*

**Formal writing, full forms.** In a formal letter, *I have*, not *I've*. It's the same verb, in full,
following this course's choice for formal pages.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **since with the present**:

> I am working here since 2022.

A reader understands it, but it marks the writer as a beginner at once, on the page where you want to
look most careful.

The second is **the finished time with *have***:

> I have completed my diploma in 2021.

The reader sees *in 2021* and expects *completed*.

The third is **the job you seemed to leave**:

> I worked in the accounts team for two years.

This is correct if you've left, and misleading if you haven't.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A fluent writer on a CV writes the current job in the present (*Manage the evening shift*) and in a
letter in the present perfect (*I have managed the evening shift since 2023*). Every job that's over is
in the past simple, with its dates.

Under pressure, filling in a form quickly, people reach for whichever past form comes first. Before you
submit, find every *for* and every *since* and check the verb in front of it.

A formal reader doesn't stop at a tense mistake. They carry on, with the wrong fact. That's why this
matters more than it looks: nobody will correct you. They'll just believe what you wrote.
