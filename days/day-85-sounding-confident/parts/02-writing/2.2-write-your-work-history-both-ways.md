## One-line answer

Write one formal paragraph about your own work or study history. It should have at least one thing
still going, one thing finished and one result now. Label the time behind every past verb. Then give
it to a reader with one question per job or course: *Is this person still doing it?* If every answer
is right, your tenses told the truth.

## The story

After the phone call, you rewrite the *Current employment* box, and then the email that goes with your
application:

> I have worked at Sharma Electronics since 2022, where I have managed the repair counter for the last
> year. Before that, I worked at a phone shop in Andheri from 2019 to 2021. I have attached my CV and a
> copy of my diploma.

Your sister reads it and says, *So you're still at Sharma, you used to be at the phone shop, and the
CV's in the email.* That's all three facts, from the verbs alone.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **present perfect**, **past simple**, **finished time**, **unfinished time**, **result now**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-finished-or-still-going.md).
- **work history**: the jobs, study and training you've done, and when.
- **time label**: a small note beside a verb saying *finished*, *still going* or *result now*.
- **still-doing-it test**: a reader says, for each thing in your paragraph, whether you're still doing
  it.

## The rule

**Step two: write the paragraph.** Four to six sentences, formal, no contractions. Include:

- at least one *still going* sentence with *since* or *for*, in the present perfect;

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **all past simple**, because it feels safest: *I worked at Sharma. I
managed the counter. I attached my CV.* The reader thinks you've left, and wonders where the CV went.

The second is **all present perfect**, because it feels formal: *I have worked at the phone shop from
2019 to 2021.* A date that's finished pulls the verb back to the past simple.

The third is **a finished time hidden in a phrase**: *I have learned Excel when I was at college.* *When
I was at college* is a finished time. It's *I learned*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often open with the present perfect and then switch to the past simple, exactly as on
day 64: *I have worked in retail for five years. In 2021, I moved to …*. The switch is where the story
starts.

Under pressure, writing on a phone, *since* and *for* are the words to watch. They're short, and the
verb in front of them carries the whole meaning.

A formal reader, such as an employer or an office, uses your verbs to fill in their own form: *current
employer*, *previous employer*. Whatever your verbs say, that's what goes in their boxes.
