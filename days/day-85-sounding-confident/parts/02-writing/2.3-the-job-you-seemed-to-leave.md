## One-line answer

Make a copy of your paragraph with every tense flipped on purpose. Put the still-going things in the
past simple, the finished things in the present perfect, and write *since* with the present. Give that
copy to one reader and your real paragraph to another, and run the still-doing-it test. The flipped
copy should make the reader get the facts wrong. Your real one should get every fact right.

## The story

At Sharma Electronics, your manager is writing a reference letter for a colleague who's moving to
another city. He shows you the draft:

> Priya has worked with us from 2020 to 2024. She managed our online orders since 2022.

You read it twice. Has Priya left? Is she still managing the orders? The dates say one thing and the
verbs say another. A new employer reading this letter would have to phone to find out, and most won't.

The grammar mistakes are small. The reader's confusion is big.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

- **present perfect**, **past simple**, **finished time**, **unfinished time**: from

- **flip**: here, change a verb to the other tense on purpose.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

## Why Vaani needs it

A cover letter is read once, and its facts go straight into the employer's notes. The phase 5 gate
letter has to say, in its verbs, what you do now and what you did before. This part shows you what a
reader does with a wrong tense. They don't correct it. They believe it.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Present perfect](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/present-perfect):
"unfinished states, especially with *for*, *since*"; "If we say when something happened … we use the
past simple".

**Step one: make copy F.** Copy your real paragraph, and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Change every still-going sentence to the past simple: *I have worked here since 2022* becomes *I
   worked here since 2022*, or *I am working here since 2022*.
2. Change every finished sentence to the present perfect: *I worked there from 2019 to 2021* becomes
   *I have worked there from 2019 to 2021*.
3. Change the result-now sentence to the past simple with no time: *I attached my CV.*

**Step two: the still-doing-it test, twice.** Give F to one reader and R to a different reader. For
each job or activity, ask:

> Is this person still doing it? Yes, no, or can't tell?

| Job or activity | F reader | R reader | True answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |
| `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if R's reader gets any fact wrong or says
*can't tell*. It's **GREEN** when R's reader gets every fact right, and F's reader gets at least one
wrong or says *can't tell*.

**Step three: the repair.** If R went red, find the verb behind the wrong answer. Put its time label
back above it, check it against the path in [part 2.1](2.1-finished-or-still-going.md), fix it, and
give R to a new reader.

**Step four: one real page.** Find one real thing you've written about your work or study: a form, an
application, a profile. Underline every *since* and *for* in it and check the verb in front.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who guesses from the dates**. If F says *from
2019 to 2021*, the reader may say *no* whatever the verb says. That's fine. The dates rescued the
meaning. The trouble is in sentences with *for* and no dates. Make sure F has at least one of those.

The second is **a reader who just says *can't tell* to everything**. Ask them to choose *yes* or *no*,
and say *can't tell* only when they really can't.

The third, in real writing, is **a mixed paragraph where only one verb is wrong**. That's the hardest to
catch, because everything around it is right. The underline-*since*-and-*for* check is built for it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Most readers can't tell you the rule. They just know what they understood. So never ask *is my grammar
right?* Ask *what did you understand?*, and compare it with what's true.

Under pressure, the present-with-*since* slips in when you're writing quickly from how you'd say it in
another language. Checking only your *since* and *for* sentences before sending takes less effort than
rereading the whole page.

An employer, a bank or an office uses your verbs to decide what to ask next. A wrong tense doesn't cost
you a mark. It costs you a phone call to set right, if they bother to make it.
