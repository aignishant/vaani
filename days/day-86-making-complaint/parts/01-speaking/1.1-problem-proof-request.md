## One-line answer

A spoken complaint that works has three moves: the **problem**, softly opened (*I'm afraid there's a
problem with …*); the **proof** (when you bought it, what's wrong, the receipt); and the **request**,
said plainly (*I'd like a replacement, please*). You're polite to the person, because they didn't cause
it. You're firm about the request, because you won't leave without it.

## The story

Your new pressure cooker's handle came loose the second time you used it. You take it back to the shop
with the box and the bill. At the counter, you're nervous, so you start like this:

> Sorry, sorry, I know you're busy … it's only a small thing … the cooker, it's maybe not working
> properly, I don't know …

The young man behind the counter looks at the cooker, looks at the queue, and says *Hmm, you'll have to
contact the company*. You say *Oh, OK*, and leave with the cooker.

Your aunt goes back the next day with the same cooker:

> Good morning. I'm afraid there's a problem with this cooker. I bought it here on Tuesday, here's the
> bill, and the handle has come loose. I'd like a replacement, please.

He goes into the back and brings out a new one.

She wasn't louder than you, and she wasn't ruder. She said what was wrong, showed that it was true, and
asked for one thing.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **the ladder (of requests)**: the steps from *Can you …?* up to *Would you mind …?* and *Do you think
  you could …?*, from
  [day 81](../../../day-81-formal-and-informal/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-request-ladder.md).
- **sorry**: the three sizes of apology, from
  [day 36](../../../day-36-sorry-and-thank-you/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-three-sizes-of-sorry.md).
- **key word**, **volume**: the word the listener most needs, and how loud you are, from
  [day 85](../../../day-85-sounding-confident/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-dials.md).
- **complaint**: telling someone that something they sold or did for you has gone wrong, and asking
  them to fix it.
- **polite**: kind in how you speak to the person.
- **firm**: clear and steady about what you want, without backing down.
- **proof**: facts that show the problem is real, such as a date, a bill or the broken thing.
- **refund**, **replacement**, **repair**: your money back, a new one, or the old one fixed.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Problem | names what's wrong, softly | *Good morning. I'm afraid there's a problem with this cooker.* |
| 2. Proof | shows it's true | *I bought it here on Tuesday. Here's the bill. The handle has come loose.* |
| 3. Request | says exactly what you want | *I'd like a replacement, please.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

This phase is about formal situations where something is at stake. On day 95 you'll be on the other
side, dealing with an angry customer. That's easier once you know what a good complaint sounds like
from the customer's side. And every office, bank and landlord conversation on day 94 might turn into a
complaint halfway through.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[I'm afraid](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/I%27m_afraid) gives the phrase as "Used to politely
introduce a disappointment or disagreement."
[How to write an effective complaint letter](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2015/09/how-write-effective-complaint-letter)
is about letters, but three of its tips hold for speaking too: "Describe the item or service you bought
and the problem"; "State exactly what you want done"; and "The person reading your letter probably
isn't responsible for the problem, but may be very helpful in resolving it."

**Move 1: the soft opening.** *I'm afraid* tells the listener that bad news is coming, politely. It
isn't fear. It's a cushion:

> I'm afraid there's a problem with my order.
> I'm afraid this isn't working.

One *sorry* at most, and only the small size: *Sorry to bother you*. Three *sorry*s turn a complaint into
an apology.

**Move 2: the proof.** Facts, not feelings. When, where, what's wrong. Hold up the bill or the item as
you say it. *It's rubbish* is a feeling. *The handle came loose the second time I used it* is proof.

**Move 3: the request.** One thing, named: a refund, a replacement, a repair, or someone who can decide.
Use *I'd like …, please*. It's polite and it isn't a question, so it's hard to answer with *no*:

> I'd like a refund, please.
> I'd like to speak to someone who can arrange a replacement.

**Polite to the person, firm about the thing.**

```text
          to the PERSON                about the THING
          ───────────────              ────────────────
 soft  →  Good morning.                                    
          I'm afraid …                                     
                                       the handle has come loose   ← proof
                                       I'd like a replacement.     ← firm
          Thank you.                                              
```

**Voice.** Let your statements fall at the end, with the volume held to the last word, as on
[day 85](../../../day-85-sounding-confident/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-dials.md). A request whose tune
rises at the end can sound like you're asking permission to complain.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the complaint that apologises itself away**:

> Sorry, sorry, it's only a small thing, maybe it's my mistake …

The listener hears that you're not sure there's a problem, so they're not sure either.

The second is **the complaint aimed at the person**: *You sold me a broken cooker.* The young man didn't
make the cooker. He's the person who can help, and now he's defending himself instead.

The third is **no request**. You describe the problem well and then stop, and the listener chooses the
cheapest answer: *Contact the company.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often open with a small, true, friendly line before the complaint: *Hi, I hope you can
help me.* It turns the listener into a helper before the problem arrives.

Under pressure, when you're angry, the proof and the request are the parts that go. Anger wants to
describe how you feel. Before you go to the counter, say the three moves once to yourself.

A shop worker hears complaints every day. They respond fastest to someone who is calm, has the bill,
and knows what they want. That person is also the easiest to say yes to.
