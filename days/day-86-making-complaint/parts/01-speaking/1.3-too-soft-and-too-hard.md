## One-line answer

Record your complaint three ways. **S** is too soft on purpose: apologies, maybes and no request. **H**
is too hard on purpose: blame, a raised voice and a demand. **P** is your real one: polite to the person
and firm about the thing. Play all three to two listeners and ask them to score each for *polite* and
for *firm*. S scores low on firm and H scores low on polite. P should score high on both.

## The story

Your neighbour tells you about two customers at the mobile shop where she works. One came in with a
cracked screen and said *So sorry, I don't know, maybe it just happened, is there anything, if
possible …* and left with nothing. The other came in and said *Your phones are rubbish. I want my money
back now.* The manager was called, and he went very quiet and very slow, and that customer also left
with nothing.

> The ones who get sorted, she says, just tell me what happened and what they want. Nicely. And then
> they don't go away.

Two ways to fail, one on each side of the one that works.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **polite**, **firm**, **proof**, **request**: from [part 1.1](1.1-problem-proof-request.md).
- **hold**: from [part 1.2](1.2-hold-the-request.md).
- **demand**: a request said as an order, with no *please* and no room for the other person.
- **blame**: saying the problem is the listener's fault.
- **two-score test**: a listener scores a take from 1 to 5 for *polite* and from 1 to 5 for *firm*.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Take | What's in it | Polite | Firm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S, soft (deliberate) | three *sorry*s, *maybe*, *if possible*, no named request | high | low |
| H, hard (deliberate) | *you* blamed, voice raised, *I want … now* | low | high, or loud at least |
| P, real | the three moves and a hold | high | high |

## Why Vaani needs it

Both wrong takes are what pressure does. Nerves push you to S, and anger pushes you to H. The phase 5
gate is an interview, not a complaint, but the same control shows there: a steady voice that's polite
and clear at once. Hearing both failures in your own voice is how you learn where the middle is.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to write an effective complaint letter](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2015/09/how-write-effective-complaint-letter):
"Don't write an angry, sarcastic, or threatening letter. The person reading your letter probably isn't
responsible for the problem, but may be very helpful in resolving it."; "State exactly what you want
done".
[I'm afraid](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/I%27m_afraid): "Used to politely introduce a disappointment
or disagreement."

**Step one: record S.** Say *deliberate* first. Apologise at least three times, use *maybe* and *if
possible*, and don't name what you want.

**Step two: record H.** Say *deliberate* first. Start with *you*: *You sold me …*. Raise your voice a
little, not a shout, and end with *I want my money back now.* No *please*.

**Step three: record P.** Your real three moves and one hold, from
[part 1.2](1.2-hold-the-request.md).

**Step four: the two-score test.** Two listeners, one at a time. Play S, H and P jumbled. After each,
ask:

> From one to five, how polite was that? And from one to five, how firm?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if either listener scores P at 3 or below on
*polite* or on *firm*. It's **GREEN** when both score P at 4 or 5 on both, and S or H scores low on at
least one side.

**Step five: the repair.** If P was low on *polite*, look for *you* at the start of a sentence, a
missing *please*, or a rising volume. If P was low on *firm*, look for *maybe*, an extra *sorry*, or a
request that isn't named. Fix the one thing, record P again, and try a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an H that's really a shout**. You don't need to shout.
A little louder, with *you* and *now*, is enough for a listener to hear the difference. Look after your
voice and your neighbours.

The second is **a listener who scores S as firm because it's long**. Ask: *Did the speaker say what they
wanted?* If not, it can't be firm.

The third, in real life, is **sliding from P into H at the second *no***. That's where the hold from
part 1.2 does its work. The same words, at the same volume.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Too soft is the quiet failure. The customer leaves without asking, and nobody remembers them. Too hard
is the loud one, and it's remembered for the wrong reason. Neither gets the cooker replaced.

Under pressure, when the shop is wrong and you know it, H feels fair. It usually costs you the help
you came for. Say the fact that makes you angry as a fact: *This is the second time this has
happened.* That's firm, and it's still polite.

A native listener decides in the first sentence whether they're dealing with a customer or a fight.
*Good morning, I'm afraid there's a problem* tells them it's a customer.
