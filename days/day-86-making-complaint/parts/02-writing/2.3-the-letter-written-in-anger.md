## One-line answer

Make a copy of your complaint that's written in anger, on purpose. Use capitals, exclamation marks,
blame, sarcasm and a threat, and leave out the action. Give that copy and your real letter to two
readers playing the company. Ask each to fill the act-on-it table, then answer one more question: *Do
you want to help this person?* The angry copy loses the facts and the goodwill. Your real letter should
keep both.

## The story

Your cousin at the call centre shows you a letter her team passed round that week, with the name
hidden:

> THIS IS THE WORST COMPANY I HAVE EVER DEALT WITH!!! Your so-called "customer care" is a joke. I have
> called FIVE TIMES. You people clearly don't care. I will be telling everyone I know.

> We all wanted to help her, my cousin says. We really did. But there's no order number, no date, and
> she never says what she wants. So it went into the *needs more information* pile, and she got a
> form reply asking for her order number. Which made her angrier.

The anger was fair. The letter just couldn't carry it anywhere.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **complaint letter**, **action**, **passive**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-the-four-paragraphs-of-a-complaint.md).
- **act-on-it test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-complaint.md).
- **sarcasm**: saying the opposite of what you mean in order to mock, like *your so-called customer
  care*.
- **goodwill**: the reader's wish to help you.
- **help test**: a reader says, from one to five, how much they want to help the writer.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Copy | What's in it | What a reader should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A, angry (deliberate) | capitals, *!!!*, blame, sarcasm, a threat; no reference number, no action | empty boxes; low goodwill |
| R, real | your letter from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-complaint.md) | four full boxes; goodwill 4 or 5 |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate formal email is checked for greeting, sign-off and contractions. A real complaint is
also checked by a reader who decides whether to help. This part lets you see, from a reader's side,
what anger does to a page.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to write an effective complaint letter](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2015/09/how-write-effective-complaint-letter):
"Don't write an angry, sarcastic, or threatening letter. The person reading your letter probably isn't
responsible for the problem, but may be very helpful in resolving it."
[A letter of complaint](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/upper-intermediate-b2-writing/letter-complaint):
"Focus on the most important facts."; "Use passives to be less direct and more formal".

**Step one: make copy A.** Copy your real letter, and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Put one sentence in capitals, and add *!!!* to two sentences.
2. Change a passive back to a blaming *you*: *I was told …* becomes *Your useless staff told me …*.
3. Add one piece of sarcasm and one threat.
4. Delete the reference number and ¶4, the action.

**Step two: the act-on-it and help tests.** Give A to one reader and R to a different reader. Each plays
the company, fills the four boxes, and answers one more question:

> From one to five, how much do you want to help this person?

at 4 or 5, and A's reader leaves at least one box empty or scores A lower.

**Step three: the repair.** If R went red on a box, that paragraph needs its fact. If R went red on
help, look for a leftover blaming *you*, an exclamation mark, or a sentence that sounds like a speech.
Fix it and give R to a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who likes A**. Some readers enjoy an angry
letter, the way you might enjoy a film. Remind them: *You work there. It's your desk it lands on.*

The second is **an R that's so calm it's lost its point**. Calm doesn't mean soft. *This is the second
time I have reported this problem* is calm and firm. Keep it.

The third, in real life, is **the angry second letter**. The first letter was good, nothing happened,
and the second one is written in capitals. The second letter should be the first one again, with one
line added: *I wrote to you on 10 September and have not received a reply.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Anger in a letter is read by someone who didn't cause the problem. They can't reply to your feelings.
They can only reply to your facts and your request.

Under pressure, after the fifth phone call, you'll want to write A. Write it, if it helps, on a separate
page. Then write R and send R.

A formal reader remembers how a letter made them feel, but they act on what it asked for. Give them both
a reason to help and something to do.
