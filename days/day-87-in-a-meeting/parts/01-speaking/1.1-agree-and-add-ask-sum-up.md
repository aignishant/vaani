## One-line answer

In a meeting, three short moves let you take part without making a speech. **Agree and add**: *I agree,
and I'd add that …*. **Ask**: *Sorry, can I just check, when you say X, do you mean Y?* **Sum up**: *So,
just to sum up, we've agreed that … . Is that right?* Each one is a sentence or two, and each one moves
the meeting forward instead of just filling it.

## The story

The supermarket where you work has a team meeting about the Diwali rota. The manager talks, then the
senior cashier talks, then two people argue about Sundays. You agree with half of what's said, you don't
understand one thing, and you have an idea. You say nothing, because you can't find a way in that isn't
a whole speech.

At the end, the manager says *OK, so that's sorted*, and everyone goes back to the tills. The next day,
three people have three different ideas of who's working on Sunday.

A colleague who joined after you says only three things in the next meeting. *I agree with Sunita, and
I'd add that the new staff shouldn't all be on the same shift.* *Sorry, can I check, does "late shift"
mean until ten or until closing?* And at the end: *So, just to sum up, Ravi and I are on Sunday, and the
new staff are split. Is that right?* The manager says *Yes, exactly*, and writes it down.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **agree**, **disagree softly**: showing you heard someone, then giving your view, from
  [day 62](../../../day-62-agreeing-disagreeing/parts/01-speaking/1.1-agree-half-agree-disagree-softly.md).
- **repair**: asking someone to say again what you missed, from
  [day 37](../../../day-37-i-didnt-catch-that/parts/01-speaking/1.1-four-ways-to-say-you-missed-it.md).
- **going round the table**: each person introducing themselves in turn at the start of a meeting,
  from [day 82](../../../day-82-introducing-yourself-at-work/parts/01-speaking/1.2-three-introductions-three-lengths.md).
- **meeting**: a planned talk between several people, usually to decide things.
- **clarify**: make something clear by asking what exactly it means.
- **sum up**: say the main points again, briefly, at the end. It's also called *summarise*.
- **decision**: what the group has agreed to do.
- **move**: here, one short thing you say that does one job in the meeting.

| Move | Its job | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agree and add | join in without repeating | *I agree with Sunita, and I'd add that …* / *That's a good point. Also, …* |
| Ask | make one thing clear for everyone | *Sorry, can I just check, when you say "late shift", do you mean until closing?* |
| Sum up | turn the talk into a decision | *So, just to sum up, we've agreed that … . Is that right?* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate is an interview, and some interviews happen as a panel, with several people round a
table. The moves are the same. Tomorrow's short update, and the presentations on days 89 and 90, all
happen in meetings. And at work, the person who sums up is often the person whose version becomes the
record.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Managing meetings](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/business/magazine/managing-meetings)
says: "Don't assume that everyone understands each other, especially when the meeting language isn't
everyone's first language. Ask for clarification, summarise each time a point is made, check that the
participants agree and ensure that decisions are made." It also says "action points – tasks to be done
and who will do them" should be recorded.

**Agree and add.** Don't just say *I agree*. Name who you agree with and add one thing. The *add* is your
contribution:

> I agree with Sunita, and I'd add that the new staff shouldn't all be on one shift.

If you half agree, use the soft *but* from day 62: *I see what you mean, but I'm not sure Sunday works
for everyone.*

**Ask.** Ask about one word or one number, not *everything*. Start small, *Sorry, can I just check …*,
and then offer your guess, so the answer can be *yes* or *no*:

> Sorry, can I just check, is that this Sunday or next Sunday?

Asking helps everyone. The page above says not to assume people understand each other.

**Sum up.** Near the end, or after a long argument, say what was decided and who does what. Then ask *Is
that right?* so people can correct you now, not tomorrow:

> So, just to sum up, Ravi and I are on Sunday, and the new staff are split between the two shifts. Is
> that right?

**When to come in.** Wait for a pause, or for someone's voice to fall at the end of a point. Then say the
first word a little louder than the rest: *Sorry …*, *So …*, *I agree …*. That word is your way in.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **silence**. You agree, you're unsure, you have an idea, and you say none
of it. Afterwards, the decision isn't the one you'd have made, and it's too late.

The second is **the speech**. When you finally come in, everything you've been holding comes out at once.
People stop listening halfway. One move, one or two sentences, then stop.

The third is **the sum-up with no names**: *So we'll sort out the rota.* Who sorts it? By when? A sum-up
without names doesn't decide anything.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

In many meetings, the person running it sums up. If they don't, anyone can, and a fluent colleague often
does: *Can I just sum up where we are?* Usually nobody minds.

Under pressure, in a fast meeting where the others are fluent speakers, the *ask* is the move that helps
most, because you're probably not the only one who didn't follow. The page above makes the same point.

A native listener hears *Sorry, can I just check* as careful, not as a weakness. What they notice is the
person who nodded, then did the wrong thing.
