## One-line answer

Hold a small real meeting with two or three people about one real decision. Record it with everyone's
permission. Your job is to use each move at least once: agree and add, ask, and sum up at the end. Then
run the **sum-up check**. Everyone says whether your sum-up matched what they think was decided. If they
all say yes, your sum-up did its job.

## The story

Your family needs to decide who's taking your grandmother to her eye appointment next week, and who's
cooking while they're out. Usually this happens in the kitchen in pieces, and someone always ends up
cross.

This time you say, *Let's sit down for a moment and sort it out properly.* Your brother suggests he
drives. You say *I agree, and I'd add that someone should go in with her, because the forms are long.*
Your mother says *the afternoon*, and you ask *Sorry, can I check, the two o'clock or the four o'clock
slot?* At the end you say *So, just to sum up, Arun drives, I go in with her, and Ma cooks. Four
o'clock on Tuesday. Is that right?*

Everyone says *yes*. For once, nobody is cross on Tuesday.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **meeting**, **clarify**, **sum up**, **decision**, **move**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-agree-and-add-ask-sum-up.md).
- **permission**: someone saying *yes* to something, here to being recorded.
- **decision to make**: the one question the meeting has to answer.
- **sum-up check**: after your sum-up, each person says whether it matches what they think was decided.

## Why Vaani needs it

A move you've only said alone into your phone is a line, not a habit. In a meeting, you have to find the
moment, come in, say it and stop, while other people are talking. This part is the first time you do
that for real, with a decision that matters to the people in the room.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **running the meeting instead of joining it**. Leading is harder, and
it's a different skill. For today, let someone else lead and practise coming in.

The second is **an ask that's really a disagreement**: *Sorry, can I check, why would we do it that
way?* That's a challenge dressed as a question. An ask is about what something *means*, not whether
it's right.

The third is **a sum-up with no *Is that right?*** Without the question, people nod to be polite, and
the mistakes come out later.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

At work, meetings often run over and end in a rush. The sum-up is the easiest thing to skip, and when
it's skipped, people leave with different ideas. Offering to sum up is one of the most useful things a
new person can do.

Under pressure, in a meeting where you're the newest or the least fluent, come in early. The first move
is the hardest. After one *I agree, and …*, the others come more easily.

A native listener often answers *Is that right?* with a small correction: *Nearly, it's Wednesday, not
Tuesday.* That's the sum-up working. Say *Thanks, Wednesday*, and you're done.
