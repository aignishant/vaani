## One-line answer

Record two sum-ups of your real meeting. **V** is vague on purpose: *So yeah, we'll sort it out, it's
all fine.* **S** is specific: who does what, and when, ending with *Is that right?* Play each to a
different listener who wasn't at the meeting. Ask them to write down who does what, and when. V leaves
them with nothing to write, and S should give them all of it.

## The story

At the supermarket, the manager ends the rota meeting with *OK, so that's sorted, great, thanks
everyone.* On Sunday morning, two cashiers turn up for one till and nobody turns up for the other.
Each of them says *But we agreed in the meeting.* They both believe it.

The meeting did make a decision. The sum-up just didn't say it out loud, so everybody left with their
own version.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **sum up**, **decision**: from [part 1.1](1.1-agree-and-add-ask-sum-up.md).
- **sum-up check**: from [part 1.2](1.2-one-real-meeting-three-moves.md).
- **vague**: not clear or exact. It could mean many things.
- **specific**: exact, with names, days and numbers.
- **stranger test**: someone who wasn't at the meeting hears only the sum-up and writes down who does
  what, and when.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Take | What's in it | What the stranger should write |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V, vague (deliberate) | *we'll sort it*, *it's fine*, *the usual*, no names, no days | nothing usable |
| S, specific | the real decision, names, days, *Is that right?* | every name and every day |

## Why Vaani needs it

At work, your sum-up is often what someone else writes down. If it's vague, the record is vague, and the
mistake belongs to whoever spoke last. In the phase 5 gate interview, answers are judged the same way:
a vague answer leaves the interviewer with nothing to write.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Managing meetings](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/business/magazine/managing-meetings):
"summarise each time a point is made, check that the participants agree and ensure that decisions are
made", with "action points – tasks to be done and who will do them".

**Step one: record V.** Say *deliberate* first. Sum up your meeting from part 1.2 with no names and no
days: *So yeah, we'll sort it out, it's all fine, same as usual.*

**Step two: record S.** Your real sum-up: each decision, the name of the person who does each thing, the
day or date, and *Is that right?*

**Step three: the stranger test.** Find two people who weren't at the meeting. Play V to one and S to
the other. Ask each:

> Write down who's doing what, and when.

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if S's listener misses or gets wrong any name,
task or day that was really decided. It's **GREEN** when S's listener gets every one, and V's listener
can't fill at least one column.

**Step four: the repair.** If S went red, find what they missed. Usually a name was replaced by *we* or
*someone*, or a day by *soon*. Put the real word back, record S again, and try a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a V that sneaks a name in** out of habit. Then the
stranger can fill a box, and the difference is smaller. Keep V completely vague.

The second is **an S that repeats the whole meeting**. A sum-up is shorter than the meeting: only the
decisions, the names and the days. If S takes longer to say than the argument did, cut it.

The third, in real meetings, is **the sum-up with *we***: *We'll call the supplier.* Which of *we*? A
task with *we* in it has no owner. Use a name.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Meetings often end in a hurry, with people standing up. That's when a specific sum-up is worth most.
One sentence, *So, Ravi on Sunday, me on Saturday, Sunita tells the new staff by Friday*, can save a
week of confusion.

Under pressure, when everyone's leaving, you might feel you're holding them up. Say it anyway, quickly.
A sum-up that takes one breath costs everyone one breath.

A native listener remembers the last thing said in a meeting. Make it the decision, with names.
