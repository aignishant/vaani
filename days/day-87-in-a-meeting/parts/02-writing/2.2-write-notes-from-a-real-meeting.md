## One-line answer

Take notes at a real meeting or group discussion, using only three things while it happens: decisions,
names and dates. Straight afterwards, turn them into full notes with the header, the numbered items, the
actions table and the next meeting. Then run the **absent-reader test**. Give the notes to someone who
wasn't there and ask them three questions they should be able to answer from the page alone.

## The story

At the next cricket club meeting, you take notes differently. On the left side of your page you write
only *D* for a decision, a name, or a date, as they come. After everyone leaves, you sit in the pavilion
and turn the scribbles into a page with a header, three numbered items and an actions table.

You send it to the secretary, who wasn't there again. She replies with one line: *Perfect. I'll chase M.
Rao about the umpires on Friday.* She knew who to chase, about what, and by when, all from your page.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **meeting notes**, **header**, **apologies**, **agenda**, **action**, **owner**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-notes-for-the-person-who-was-not-there.md).
- **imperative**: the verb form that tells someone to do something, from
  [day 32](../../../day-32-giving-instructions/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-imperative-on-paper.md).
- **live notes**: the quick marks you make while the meeting is happening.
- **absent-reader test**: someone who wasn't at the meeting reads the notes and answers questions from
  them alone.

The two stages:

| Stage | When | What you write |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live notes | during the meeting | only *D* + a decision, names, dates |
| Full notes | straight after | header, numbered items, actions table, next meeting |

## Why Vaani needs it

A page you only design at home isn't the same as one you make while people are talking over each other.
This part makes you do the real thing once. The live-notes habit will help again on day 88, when you
turn your own week into a status update.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minutes): "a record of what was *done* at the meeting, not what
was *said*".
[Action item](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_item): each action with an owner and a planned
completion date.
[Managing meetings](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/business/magazine/managing-meetings):
"Ask someone to take notes of what is being discussed and ensure that action points – tasks to be done
and who will do them – are recorded".

**Step one: find a real meeting.** A work or college meeting, a building committee, a family decision,
or the meeting you held for your speaking practice. Tell the group you're taking notes.

**Step two: live notes.** Three marks only:

- *D:* and the decision, in as few words as you can.
- A name, when someone agrees to do something.
- A date or day, whenever one is said.

If a decision isn't clear, ask. Asking is better than guessing on paper.

**Step three: full notes, straight after.** Write the header, the numbered items (decisions only), the
actions table (verb, one owner, a date) and the next meeting. No contractions if it's going to people
you don't know well. Full sentences in the items, imperatives in the *What* column.

**Step four: the absent-reader test.** Give the full notes to one person who wasn't there. Ask three
questions, and don't let them ask you anything:

> Who is doing the first action, and by when?
> What was decided about the second item?
> When and where is the next meeting?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **waiting a day to write the full notes**. By then the live marks don't
make sense, and you guess. Write them up straight after, while the meeting is still in your head.

The second is **an action written as a sentence with the owner hidden**: *It was agreed that the balls
would be bought before the tournament.* That's a correct passive, and the owner has vanished. In the
actions table, use a name.

The third is **a *not decided* left out** because it feels like a failure. The absent reader needs to
know that the umpires question is still open, or they'll think it's been dealt with.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Good note-takers often ask a question during the meeting just to fill their table: *Sorry, who's taking
that one?* or *By when?* It sounds like a small interruption, and it saves a lot of confusion later.

Under pressure, in a fast meeting, you'll miss things. Put a question mark in your live notes, and check
with someone before sending. A note with *(to confirm)* is better than a wrong one.

A reader who wasn't there trusts the notes completely. They have nothing else. Whatever your page says
becomes what happened.
