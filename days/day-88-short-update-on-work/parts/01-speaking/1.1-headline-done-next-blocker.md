## One-line answer

A short spoken update has four pieces, in this order: a **headline** (*on track*, or *one problem*),
what's **done**, what's **next**, and any **blocker**, meaning anything stopping you, with what you need.
*The stock count's on track. I've finished aisles one to six. Tomorrow I'll do seven to ten. One thing:
the scanner in aisle eight is broken, so I'll need the spare one.* That's four sentences, and the
listener knows everything.

## The story

Every morning at the courier depot, the supervisor stands by the loading bay and asks each person,
*Where are we?*

Your colleague says: *Yesterday I came in, first I checked the vans, then there was that problem with
the gate, then I had lunch, then Suresh called about the Andheri parcels, and then …* The supervisor is
already looking at the next person. At the very end, your colleague adds, *Oh, and van three has a flat
tyre.* Nobody hears it. At ten, van three can't go out.

The next person says: *Andheri route's on track. Forty parcels out yesterday, all delivered. Today it's
the Bandra run. One problem: I need a second person for the heavy boxes.* The supervisor says *Take
Farhan* and moves on.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **headline**: the most important thing, said first, from
  [day 55](../../../day-55-passing-on-news/parts/01-speaking/1.1-did-you-hear-then-the-headline.md).
- **sum up**: say the main points again, briefly, from
  [day 87](../../../day-87-in-a-meeting/parts/01-speaking/1.1-agree-and-add-ask-sum-up.md).
- **key word**: the word a listener most needs, said slower, from
  [day 85](../../../day-85-sounding-confident/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-dials.md).
- **update**: a short report on how your work is going.
- **on track**: going as planned, and likely to finish on time.
- **blocker**: something stopping your work, which you can't fix alone.
- **the ask**: what you need from the listener to remove a blocker.

| Piece | The question under it | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline | Is it OK? | *The stock count's on track.* / *We've got one problem.* |
| Done | What's finished? | *I've finished aisles one to six.* |
| Next | What's coming? | *Tomorrow I'll do seven to ten.* |
| Blocker + ask | Is anything stopping you? | *The scanner's broken, so I'll need the spare one.* / *Nothing's blocking me.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

At work, this is the most common thing you'll say in a meeting, often every day. In the phase 5 gate
interview, *Tell me about your current role* wants the same shape: what you do, what you've achieved,
what's next. And tomorrow's presentation opening is a headline, too.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Stand-up meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-up_meeting) gives the three questions that
teams have traditionally answered in a daily stand-up. The first is "What did I do yesterday …?", the
second "What will I do today …?", and the third "Do I see any impediment …?". An impediment is a
blocker. The article says the stand-up "usually does not include full discussions", and that people may
say "'Let's talk about this more after the meeting'".
[Traffic light rating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_light_rating_system) says "project
managers often use a RAG rating to indicate how on track or at risk is the project". RAG is Red, Amber,
Green.

**The headline first.** One short sentence that tells the listener whether to worry. You can say it in
plain words (*on track*, *a bit behind*, *stuck*) or in the colours (*green*, *amber*, *red*) if your
workplace uses them.

**Done: numbers, not activities.** *I've finished aisles one to six* is a result. *I was busy with the
count* is an activity. Give one number if you can. *I've finished …* is the natural spoken form for
what's done by now.

**Next: one sentence.** *Today I'll …* / *Tomorrow I'm going to …*.

**The blocker: say it, with the ask.** If nothing's blocking you, say so: *Nothing's blocking me.* If
something is, say what it is, and in the same breath what you need: *So I'll need the spare scanner.* A
blocker with no ask is just a complaint.

**Short.** Four sentences is a good update. If someone asks a follow-up that needs a long answer, use the
stand-up line: *Can we talk about that after the meeting?*

**Voice.** The headline and the blocker are your key words. Slow down on them, and keep your volume to
the end, as on [day 85](../../../day-85-sounding-confident/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-dials.md).

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the diary**: *First I did this, then that, then …*. Every activity in
order, no result, and the blocker at the end, if at all.

The second is **the buried blocker**: the problem is mentioned, quickly, as the listener is turning
away. It needed to be the loudest sentence.

The third is **the blocker with no ask**: *The scanner's broken.* The supervisor says *Oh no* and moves
on. Say what you need.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

In many workplaces, updates go round a circle, fast. The person who's clear gets help, and the person
who's long gets a nod. A fluent speaker prepares their four sentences on the way in.

Under pressure, when things are going badly, people either hide the blocker or tell the whole story of
it. Neither helps. Say it plainly as the headline: *I'm stuck on the Bandra route. I need a second
driver.*

A native listener, such as a supervisor, decides what to do from your headline and your ask. Everything
else is detail they may never need.
