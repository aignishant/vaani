## One-line answer

Take three real pieces of work from your week, and give each one a four-sentence update: headline,
done, next, blocker. Make at least one of them a *not on track* update. Record all three without notes.
Then run the **four-box test**. A listener hears each update once and fills four boxes: *OK or not?*,
*done*, *next*, *needs*. If they fill every box, your update was complete.

## The story

Your sister is organising your cousin's wedding, and she's asked you to handle three things: the tent,
the cook and the invitations. Every evening she phones and says, *Update?*

The first evening you talk for a long time and she says *So … is the tent booked or not?* The second
evening you say, for each thing, one headline, one *done*, one *next* and one *need*. On the cook, you
say *We're a bit behind. He hasn't confirmed the menu. I'll call him tomorrow morning. If he doesn't
answer, I need you to call his wife, because she knows you.*

Your sister writes four words on her hand and says *Done. Next?*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **update**, **headline**, **on track**, **blocker**, **the ask**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-headline-done-next-blocker.md).
- **behind**: later than planned.
- **four-box test**: a listener hears an update once and fills four boxes: *OK or not?*, *done*, *next*,
  *needs*.

## Why Vaani needs it

A good update about going well is easy. A good update about going badly is the one that matters at
work, and it's the one people avoid. This part makes you practise both with real work, so the shape is
ready whichever news you have.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Stand-up meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-up_meeting): what was done, what will be done,
and "any impediment"; "the stand-up usually does not include full discussions".
[Traffic light rating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_light_rating_system): the RAG

**Step three: listen for the headline.** Is each headline the first sentence? Is the *not on track* one

they fill the boxes without hearing it again.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the soft headline** on the bad news: *It's mostly fine, just a few
small things.* The listener hears *fine*. If it's behind, say *behind*.

The second is **the ask to nobody**: *Someone needs to call the cook.* Who? Name the person, even if
it's the listener: *I need you to call …*.

The third is **the missing *next***. You say what's done and what's wrong, and stop. The listener
doesn't know if you're still working on it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A *behind* update on Monday is a problem your manager can still fix. The same news on Friday is a
problem they have to explain to someone else. Early and plain beats late and soft.

Under pressure, with a bad update, you'll want to start with the reasons: *Because the cook didn't …*.
Start with the headline instead. The reasons can come if they ask.

A native listener fills the four boxes in their head whether you give them the pieces or not. If you
don't, they guess, usually *it's fine*.
