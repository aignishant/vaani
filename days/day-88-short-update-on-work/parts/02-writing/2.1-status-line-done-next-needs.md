## One-line answer

A status update email tells the reader, in its first line, whether the work is **on track**. Then come
three short labelled lists: **Done** (in the present perfect: *I have finished …*), **Next** (*I will
…*), and **Needs** (any blocker, with a named person and a date). The subject line names the work and
the period. Someone who reads only the subject and the first line should already know whether to worry.

## The story

You've been asked to help your building's committee get the lift repaired. Every Friday, the chairman
wants an update by email. Your first one is three long paragraphs about phone calls, a visit from a
mechanic, a quote that was too high, a second quote, and a question about whether the committee can
pay in two parts. It's all there, somewhere.

The chairman replies: *Thanks. So is the lift going to be fixed this month or not? And what do you need
from me?* The answer to both was in the third paragraph, in the middle of a sentence.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **frame**, **reason line**, **closing line**: from
  [day 82](../../../day-82-introducing-yourself-at-work/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-formal-frame-top-to-bottom.md).
- **present perfect**, **result now**: *have* + the third form, for something finished whose result is
  here now, from
  [day 85](../../../day-85-sounding-confident/parts/02-writing/2.1-finished-or-still-going.md).
- **action**, **owner**: a task with one person responsible, from
  [day 87](../../../day-87-in-a-meeting/parts/02-writing/2.1-notes-for-the-person-who-was-not-there.md).
- **status update**: a short email saying how a piece of work is going.
- **status line**: the first line of the update, saying whether the work is on track.
- **on track / at risk / off track**: going as planned; might be late; will be late unless something
  changes. Some workplaces call these *green*, *amber* and *red*.
- **blocker**: something stopping the work that you can't fix alone.
- **bullet**: a short line in a list, starting with a mark such as a dash.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate asks for a formal email. A status update is the formal email most people at work send
most often, sometimes every week. It uses the frame from day 82, the tenses from day 85 and the actions
from day 87, all on one page.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Traffic light rating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_light_rating_system): "project
managers often use a RAG rating to indicate how on track or at risk is the project, its deliverables or
tasks", where RAG is "Red, Amber, Green". It also notes that the letters R, A and G are shown beside the
colours, for readers who are colour-blind.
[Stand-up meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-up_meeting) lists the classic three questions:
what was done, what will be done, and "any impediment".

**The email, on the screen:**

```text
Subject: Lift repair – update for week ending 26 September – AT RISK

Dear Mr Kapoor,

Status: at risk. The repair can still happen in October, but only if the
committee approves the second quote by 2 October.

Done
- I have received two quotes: Rs 1,20,000 and Rs 95,000.
- The mechanic has confirmed that the motor, not the cable, is faulty.

Next
- I will send both quotes to all committee members on Monday.
- I will book the repair as soon as a quote is approved.

Needs
- A committee decision on the Rs 95,000 quote, by 2 October (you).

I will send the next update on 3 October.

Yours sincerely,
Anita Sharma
Flat 12
```

**The subject line.** Name the work, the period, and, if your readers use it, the status in capitals as
a label: *Lift repair – update for week ending 26 September – AT RISK*. That's the one place capitals
are a label, not shouting.

**The status line.** First line after the greeting, starting *Status:*. One word, then one sentence
saying why. Don't use a colour on its own. Write the word, so it survives a black-and-white printout and
a reader who can't tell red from green.

**Done: the present perfect.** Finished things with a result now: *I have received*, *The mechanic has
confirmed*. If you give a date, switch to the past simple: *The mechanic visited on 22 September.*

**Next: *will*.** *I will send …*, *I will book …*. One owner per line. If it's someone else, name them.

**Needs: the ask.** What you need, from whom, and by when. If nothing, write *Needs: nothing this
week.* Never leave the heading out. An empty *Needs* is information too.

**The close.** Say when the next update comes. Then the formal sign-off. For a colleague you write to
every week, *Kind regards,* is also common, as on day 82.

**Grammar in the lists.** Each bullet is a full sentence with a full stop, or each is a short phrase
without one. Choose one style and keep it for the whole email.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the story email**: three paragraphs in time order, with the status and
the ask somewhere in the middle. The reader has to dig for the two things they needed.

The second is **the missing status line**. The email starts with *Please find below the update*, and the
reader must read everything to find out whether to worry.

The third is **a green status with a red need**: *Status: on track.* … *Needs: approval by tomorrow or
we lose the slot.* If a need can make the work late, the status is *at risk*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Busy readers often decide from the subject line whether to open an update at all. An update marked *ON
TRACK* may be read next week, and one marked *AT RISK* is likely to be read today. That's why the
status belongs in the subject.

Under pressure, when the news is bad, people soften the status line: *mostly on track*. There's no
*mostly*. If it might be late, it's *at risk*, and saying so early is what gives the reader time to help.

A formal reader reads the status line and the *Needs* list, and often nothing else. Put everything they
must act on in those two places.

## Check yourself

- **Write:** `TODO(me)` Think of one real piece of work you're doing: at work, for a course, at home, for
  a group. Write only its subject line and status line, the word *on track*, *at risk* or *off track* and
  one sentence saying why, and its *Needs* list, with a named person and a date, or *nothing this week*.
- **Say out loud:** Say your status line, then say what would have to happen to move it one step
