## One-line answer

Write a complete status update email for one real piece of work: the subject line, the status line,
*Done*, *Next* and *Needs*, inside the formal frame. Then run the **two-line test**. A reader sees only
the subject line and the first line, covers the rest, and tells you whether the work is OK and whether
they need to do anything. Then they read the whole email and check their answer.

## The story

On the second Friday, you write the lift update in the new shape. The chairman replies the same morning:
*Approved the 95. Tell the mechanic.* That's six words. He read the status line and the *Needs* list,
did the one thing you asked, and went back to his day.

You didn't write more than last week. You wrote the same facts in the order he needed them.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **status update**, **status line**, **on track / at risk / off track**, **blocker**, **bullet**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-status-line-done-next-needs.md).
- **present perfect**: *have* + the third form, for something done whose result is here now, from
  [day 85](../../../day-85-sounding-confident/parts/02-writing/2.1-finished-or-still-going.md).
- **period**: here, the stretch of time the update covers, such as *week ending 26 September*.
- **two-line test**: a reader sees only the subject and the first line, then says whether the work is OK
  and whether they need to act.

## Why Vaani needs it

A status update is short, so every piece shows. Writing a real one, for a real reader, is the fastest
way to find out which piece you leave out. It's also a formal email, so it's gate practice: the
greeting, the sign-off and no contractions.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Traffic light rating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_light_rating_system): the rating
shows "how on track or at risk" work is.
[Stand-up meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-up_meeting): done, next, and "any impediment".

**Step one: choose real work and a real reader.** A manager, a teacher, a committee, a family member
paying for something. Someone who actually wants to know how it's going.

**Step two: write the facts.** Before the email, list them:

**Step three: write the email.** The formal frame, with the status line first after the greeting. *Done*
in the present perfect, *Next* with *will*, *Needs* with a name and a date. No contractions. One style
for all the bullets.

**Step four: check the tenses.** Underline every verb under *Done*. Each should be *have* or *has* with
the third form. If you've added a date, it should be the past simple instead. Under *Next*, each verb
should have *will*.

**Step five: the two-line test.** Fold or cover the email so only the subject line and the first line
after the greeting show. Ask one reader:

> Is this work OK? And do you need to do anything?

Then let them read the whole email and ask:

> Was your first answer right?

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a *Done* list full of activities**: *Worked on the quotes. Talked to
the mechanic.* That's busy, not done. What's the result? *I have received two quotes.*

The second is **the *Needs* with no name**: *Approval needed.* From whom? The reader may assume it's
someone else.

The third is **the update with no next date**. The reader doesn't know whether to expect another, and
starts asking you.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Weekly updates get easier after the first one. Keep the last one, copy it, and change each line. The
shape stays and only the facts move. Readers like that too, because they know where to look.

Under pressure, in a bad week, you'll want to skip the update. That's the week it matters most. A short
*at risk* update with a clear *Needs* is worth more than a long one next week.

A formal reader who gets the same shape every week starts to trust it. After a few weeks, they read only
the status and *Needs* lines, and that's the point.
