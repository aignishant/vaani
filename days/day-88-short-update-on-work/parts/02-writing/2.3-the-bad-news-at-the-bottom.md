## One-line answer

Make a copy of your status update that hides the bad news on purpose. Give it a cheerful *on track*
status line, a long *Done* list, and the blocker in the last sentence of a paragraph at the bottom. Then
run the two-line test on that copy and on your real one, with two readers. The hidden copy should make
the reader say *fine, nothing to do*. Your real update should make them see the problem at once.

## The story

The lift repair drags into a third week. The quote was approved, but the mechanic now says a part must
come from another city, and it could take a month. You don't want to be the person with bad news again.
So your Friday email starts *Good progress this week!* and lists everything that went well. The part is
mentioned in the last line: *There may be some delay with a part.*

On Monday, the chairman tells the residents the lift will be working by the weekend. On the weekend, it
isn't. He's the one who has to explain, and he asks you why he didn't know.

He did know. It was in the email, at the bottom.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **status update**, **status line**, **blocker**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-status-line-done-next-needs.md).
- **two-line test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-status-update.md).
- **hidden**: here, present on the page, but where a reader won't look.
- **false green**: a status line that says *on track* when the work is really at risk.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed. Here these are the course's check
  words, not the status colours.

| Copy | What's in it | What the two-line reader should say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H, hidden (deliberate) | *on track*, a long *Done*, the blocker last and vague | *Fine. Nothing to do.* |
| T, true | your real update, with the true status and a named *Needs* | the real status, and what they must do |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate is checked by a reader. So is every email you send at work. This part shows you, with
your own update, the difference between writing the bad news down and making sure it's read.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Traffic light rating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traffic_light_rating_system): the rating
indicates "how on track or at risk" the work is.
[Stand-up meeting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-up_meeting): "any impediment" is one of the
three things reported.

**Step one: make copy H.** Copy your update from part 2.2, and write *deliberate* at the top. If your
real update is already *on track*, invent one believable blocker for this copy only, and label it
*invented for the exercise*. Then:

1. Change the status line to *Status: on track. Good progress this week.*
2. Double the *Done* list with small things.
3. Delete the *Needs* heading. Put the blocker in the last sentence of the email, softly: *There may be
   some delay with …*.

**Step two: make T match.** If you invented a blocker for H, add the same blocker to a copy of your real
update, done properly: *Status: at risk* and the blocker under *Needs* with a name and a date. Label it
*practice copy*.

**Step three: the two-line test, twice.** Give H to one reader and T to a different reader. Each sees
only the subject line and the first line, then answers:

> Is this work OK? And do you need to do anything?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if T's reader, from the first two lines alone,
says the work is fine or that they needn't act, when there's a blocker. It's **GREEN** when T's reader
gets the status and the need right from two lines, and H's reader gets at least one wrong.

**Step four: the repair.** If T went red, the status line is too soft or the subject line is missing the
status. Fix those two lines only, and try a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an H reader who reads everything first**. The whole
point is the first two lines. Cover the rest before you hand it over.

The second is **a T with an honest status and no ask**: *Status: at risk.* The reader now worries, but
doesn't know what to do. The *Needs* line turns worry into action.

The third, in real updates, is **the status that never changes**: *on track* every week, until the week
it's suddenly *off track*. If it has been at risk for a while, say so while there's still time.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Hiding bad news at the bottom usually isn't dishonesty. It's hope: *maybe it'll be fine by next week*.
But the reader can't help with a problem they haven't seen.

Under pressure, when you've already given bad news the week before, a second *at risk* feels
impossible. Write it anyway, with the ask. The second bad update, read in time, is far better than the
first surprise.

A formal reader who is surprised by bad news asks two questions: *what happened?* and *why didn't I
know?* A true status line means they only ever have to ask the first.
