## One-line answer

Open a short talk with four lines, before you say anything about the topic itself. Say **who you
are**, **what you'll talk about**, **why it matters to them**, and **the map**: *I'll cover three
things.* *Good morning, everyone. I'm Ravi, from accounts. I'm going to talk about the new way to claim
expenses. It'll save you a trip to my desk every month. I'll cover three things: what's changed, how to
fill in the form, and the new deadline.* Then the audience knows where they're going, and they can
follow you there.

## The story

Your manager asks you to explain the new expense-claim form at the Monday team meeting, in front of
fifteen people. You've prepared the three points well. You stand up, and the first thing that comes
out is:

> Sorry, sorry, my English is not so good … OK, so, the form … it's on the shared drive, and the first
> box is …

People are still sitting down, and still finishing their tea. By the time they're listening, you're
on the second box, and they don't know what the form is for, why they should care or how much more
there is. Two people check their phones.

A colleague who presents the next week starts differently. She waits until the room is quiet. She
says her name, the topic, why it matters to them, and *three things*. Everyone looks up at *three
things*, because now they know when it'll end.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **key word**, **pause**, **volume**: from
  [day 85](../../../day-85-sounding-confident/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-dials.md).
- **the short size**: name and role, for going round a table, from
  [day 82](../../../day-82-introducing-yourself-at-work/parts/01-speaking/1.2-three-introductions-three-lengths.md).
- **what it's for**: saying the purpose before the details, from
  [day 70](../../../day-70-explaining-how-it-works/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-its-for-what-its-like-how-it-works.md).
- **presentation**: a short talk to a group, planned in advance. It's also just called *a talk*.
- **audience**: the people listening to a talk.
- **opening**: the first few lines of a talk, before the first point.
- **the map**: a line near the start that says how many points are coming and what they are.
- **signpost**: a short phrase that tells the audience where they are in a talk, such as *First* or *To
  sum up*.

| Line | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Who | lets them know who's talking | *Good morning, everyone. I'm Ravi, from accounts.* |
| 2. What | names the topic in one line | *I'm going to talk about the new expense form.* |
| 3. Why | says why it matters to *them* | *It'll save you a trip to my desk every month.* |
| 4. Map | says how many points, and names them | *I'll cover three things: what's changed, how to fill it in, and the deadline.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Tomorrow you'll explain a chart or a picture to an audience, and on day 99 you'll give a full talk with
an opening, three points and a close. Both start with these four lines. In the phase 5 gate interview,
a clear first sentence plays the same part: it tells the listener what's coming.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Oral presentation](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/oral-presentation)
lists introduction phrases: "I'm going to talk about ...", "I'd like to talk about ...", "The main focus
of this presentation is ...". For ordering, it lists "First of all, ...", "Secondly, ...", "Finally,
...". It also says: "Look at the people who are listening to you." and "Pause from time to time and
don't speak too quickly."

**Wait for quiet.** Stand, look at the audience and wait until the room is still before the first word.
One breath of silence is the start of the talk. It isn't a gap.

**Line 1: who.** The short size from day 82: name and role. If everyone knows you, just *Good morning,
everyone.*

**Line 2: what.** *I'm going to talk about …* or *I'd like to talk about …*. One topic, in one line.

**Line 3: why it matters to them.** Say what they'll gain, or what goes wrong if they don't listen. Use
*you*: *It'll save you …*, *You'll need this for …*. This is the line that makes them put their phones
down.

**Line 4: the map.** Say the number, then the names: *I'll cover three things: …*. Three is easy to
remember. Two or four are fine too. Stress the number.

**Voice.** The opening is where nerves are loudest. Use the three dials from
[day 85](../../../day-85-sounding-confident/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-dials.md): loud to the last word,
slow on the topic and the number, and a pause after the map before your first point.

**Name the trap.** Never open with an apology for your English. It tells the audience to listen for
mistakes, and they'll find them. If you need a moment, just smile and breathe. Silence is better than
*sorry*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **starting on point one**: *OK, so, the first box on the form is …*. The
audience is still arriving in their heads.

The second is **the apology opening**: *Sorry, my English is not good, and I'm very nervous.* It spends
your first line on the wrong thing.

The third is **the map with no number**: *I'll talk about the form and some things about it.* Without a
number, nobody knows how much is left, so nobody can pace their listening.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent presenters often learn their opening word for word, even when they speak the rest from notes.
The first four lines are the ones most affected by nerves, so they're the ones worth having ready.

Under pressure, when the person before you overran and the room is restless, the *why* line matters
even more. Make it about them and make it short.

A native audience decides quickly whether to listen. The *why* and the *map* are what they decide on.
