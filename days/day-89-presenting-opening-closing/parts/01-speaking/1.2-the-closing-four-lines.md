## One-line answer

Close a talk with four lines after your last point. First, a **signal** that you're ending: *To sum
up, …*. Second, a **recap** of the map in one breath. Third, **the one thing** you want them to do or
remember. Fourth, **thanks and questions**: *Thank you. Are there any questions?* Then stop, and look at
the audience. The close is what people remember, so it can't be *So … yeah … that's it.*

## The story

Back at the Monday meeting, you reach the end of your third point about the expense form. You don't
know how to finish. So you say:

> … and that's the deadline. So … yeah. That's it, I think. OK.

You sit down. There's a pause, and then the manager says *Thanks, Ravi. Right, next item.* Nobody asks
a question. That afternoon, two people come to your desk and ask what the deadline was.

Your colleague ends her talk the next week like this:

> So, to sum up: the new form, the three boxes, and the fifth of every month. If you remember one thing,
> it's the fifth. Thank you. Are there any questions?

Three hands go up.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **presentation**, **audience**, **the map**, **signpost**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-the-opening-four-lines.md).
- **sum up**: say the main points again, briefly, at the end, from
  [day 87](../../../day-87-in-a-meeting/parts/01-speaking/1.1-agree-and-add-ask-sum-up.md).
- **closing**: the last few lines of a talk, after the last point.
- **recap**: the map, said again at the end, in one breath.
- **the one thing**: the single action or fact you most want the audience to take away.
- **trail off**: stop talking gradually, with no clear end.

| Line | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Signal | says the end is here | *To sum up, …* / *So, to finish, …* / *In conclusion, …* |
| 2. Recap | repeats the map | *the new form, the three boxes, and the fifth of every month.* |
| 3. The one thing | what to do or remember | *If you remember one thing, it's the fifth.* |
| 4. Thanks + questions | ends, and opens the floor | *Thank you. Are there any questions?* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Tomorrow's chart explanation ends with a close, and day 99's full talk is judged on its opening, three
points and close. In the phase 5 gate interview, your answers need the same clean ending. A final
sentence that lands, then silence, is how a listener knows you've finished.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Oral presentation](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/speaking-exams/oral-presentation)
lists conclusion phrases, "To sum up, ..." and "In conclusion, ...", and ordering phrases ending with
"Finally, ..." and "Lastly, ...".

**Line 1: the signal.** *To sum up* or *So, to finish*. The audience hears it and starts listening again,
even if they drifted in the middle. Say it a little slower, with a pause after it.

**Line 2: the recap.** The map from your opening, said again, fast and in one breath. It's the same three
names in the same order. The repetition is on purpose.

**Line 3: the one thing.** One action or one fact: *If you remember one thing, it's …*, or *So please …
by Friday.* This is the sentence to slow down on most.

**Line 4: thanks and questions.** *Thank you. Are there any questions?* Then stop talking and look at the
audience. Let the silence sit for one or two breaths before you decide there are none. People need a
moment to find a question.

**The tune.** Let your voice fall on the last word of each closing line, with the volume held, as on
day 85. A close that rises sounds as if it isn't finished.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the trail-off**: *So … yeah … that's it, I think.* The talk just stops,
and the audience isn't sure it has.

The second is **a new point in the close**: *Oh, and one more thing I forgot …*. The close is for things
already said. If it's important, it belongs in the middle.

The third is **the rushed questions line**: *Thanks-any-questions-no-OK*, and you sit down before anyone
can breathe. The questions line needs a real pause after it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent presenters often plan the close before the middle. If you know where you're going to land, it's
easier to get there.

Under pressure, when you're told *we're running out of time*, don't cut the close. Cut a detail from
your last point. The close is the part people remember and the part they act on.

A native audience often doesn't ask questions straight away. That silence isn't failure. Wait a moment,
then, if you like, start one yourself: *One question people often ask is …*.
