## One-line answer

Give a real short talk of three points to a small audience of two or three people. Open with the four
opening lines and close with the four closing lines. Keep the middle short, with each point one or two
sentences. Record it. Then run the **map test**: ask each listener to tell you the three points and the
one thing. If they can, your opening and close did their job.

## The story

At home, your father wants to understand the new way to book his pension appointment online. You could
just show him. Instead, you stand in the kitchen and say:

> Papa, I'm going to explain the new booking page. It'll mean no more queue at the office. There are
> three things: finding the page, choosing a date, and the text message you get after.

Then the three points. Then:

> So, to sum up: the page, the date, the message. If you remember one thing, keep the message: it's
> your proof. Any questions?

Your father says, *The message is the proof. Got it.* He books the next one himself.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **opening**, **the map**, **signpost**, **audience**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-the-opening-four-lines.md).
- **closing**, **recap**, **the one thing**: from [part 1.2](1.2-the-closing-four-lines.md).
- **the middle**: the points themselves, between the opening and the close.
- **map test**: after a talk, each listener says the points and the one thing.

The whole talk is three pieces:

```text
  OPENING (4 lines)       MIDDLE (3 points)              CLOSING (4 lines)
  who · what · why · map → First … / Second … / Finally … → sum up · recap · one thing · questions?
```

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a map that doesn't match the middle**. You promise three things and
give four, or the names change. The audience notices. Keep the same names in the map, the signposts and
the recap.

The second is **reading the opening from the card**. The opening is where eye contact matters most.
Learn it.

The third is **skipping the signposts in the middle**. Without *Second* and *Finally*, the listener
can't tell where one point ends. The map only works if the middle follows it out loud.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A good talk often feels too repetitive to the speaker: the map, the signposts, the recap. It doesn't
feel repetitive to the audience. They hear the talk once, and the repetition is what lets them follow.

Under pressure, if you lose your place in the middle, go back to the map: *So that was the first thing.
The second thing is …*. The map works as a rescue as well as a promise.

A native audience remembers the one thing far more often than any detail. Choose it carefully.
