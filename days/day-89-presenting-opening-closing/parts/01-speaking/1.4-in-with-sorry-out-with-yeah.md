## One-line answer

Record your talk twice more. **B** is broken on purpose: it opens with an apology and goes straight to
point one, and it ends with *So … yeah … that's it*. **G** is your real talk: four opening lines and
four closing lines. Play each to a different listener and run the map test. B's listener should lose
the map or the one thing. G's listener should get them all.

## The story

At a parents' meeting at your son's school, three parents speak about the sports day. The first says
*Sorry, I'm not a good speaker*, talks for a while, and sits down mid-sentence. The second says *Good
evening. I'm Meera. I'm going to talk about volunteers. We need twelve, and here's what they'll do.
Three jobs: gates, water, first aid.* At the end: *So: gates, water, first aid. If you can help, put
your name on the sheet by the door. Thank you. Any questions?*

At home, someone asks what the meeting was about. You remember all three of Meera's jobs and the
sheet by the door. You can't remember what the first parent said at all.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

| Take | Opening | Closing | What the listener should manage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B, broken (deliberate) | *Sorry, my English …*, then point one | *So … yeah … that's it* | the points, maybe; the one thing, no |
| G, good | who · what · why · map | sum up · recap · one thing · questions | every point and the one thing |

The middle, meaning your three points, is the same in both takes. Only the two ends change.

## Why Vaani needs it

A talk's middle can be excellent and still be forgotten if the two ends are missing. The phase 5 gate
interview and day 99's full talk are both judged by listeners who hear them once. This part lets you
hear what the ends are worth by taking them away.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a B that keeps its signposts** out of habit. Then the
middle still carries the listener, and the difference is small. Take the signposts out.

The second is **a B listener who has already heard G**. They'll remember G. Use different listeners.

The third, in a real talk, is **the half-close**: *So, to sum up … that's it.* The signal is there, but
the recap and the one thing aren't. The listener heard *the end is coming*, and then nothing came.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

An apology at the start often comes from kindness: you don't want to waste their time. But the kind
thing is the *why* line. It tells them their time won't be wasted.

Under pressure, the close is what nervous speakers cut, because they want to sit down. Keep the one
thing, even if you drop everything else from the close.

A native audience forgives a shaky middle when the opening told them where they were going and the close
told them what to keep. It rarely forgives the other way round.
