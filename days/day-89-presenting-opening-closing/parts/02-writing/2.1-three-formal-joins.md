## One-line answer

*However*, *therefore* and *although* are the formal cousins of *but*, *so* and *but* again. Each one
has its own punctuation, and the punctuation is what a reader sees. **However** starts a new sentence
and takes a comma after it: *The first offer is cheaper. However, it has no repair service.*
**Therefore** does the same for a result: *… . Therefore, we recommend the second offer.* **Although**
stays inside one sentence, with a comma between the two halves: *Although the first offer is cheaper,
it has no repair service.* Never *although* and *but* together.

## The story

Your office manager asks you to compare two offers for a new photocopier and write a short
recommendation. You've learned *but* and *so*, and you use them:

> The first one is cheaper but it has no repair service so we should choose the second one but it is
> more expensive.

It's one long sentence, and it reads like speech. You swap in the formal words you've seen in letters:

> The first one is cheaper, however it has no repair service, therefore we should choose the second one.
> Although it is more expensive, but it includes repairs.

It looks more formal. Your manager reads it twice, and then asks you what you're recommending.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **joining word**: a word that joins two ideas: *and*, *but*, *so*, *because*, from
  [day 39](../../../day-39-steady-pace/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-four-joining-words.md).
- **sentence**: a group of words with a subject and a verb that makes sense alone, starting with a
  capital and ending with a full stop, from
  [day 3](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-sentence-is.md).
- **formal**: careful writing for someone you don't know well, from
  [day 81](../../../day-81-formal-and-informal/parts/02-writing/2.1-five-switches.md).
- **clause**: a group of words with its own subject and verb. A sentence can have one clause or two.
- **contrast**: two facts that pull in different directions, such as *cheaper* and *no repairs*.
- **result**: what follows from a fact, such as *so we recommend …*.
- **comma splice**: two full sentences joined by only a comma. It's a mistake.
- **semicolon**: the mark *;*, a stop between two closely linked sentences that's stronger than a comma
  and weaker than a full stop.

| Formal word | Informal cousin | Job | Where it goes | Punctuation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *however* | *but* | contrast | at the start of the second sentence | full stop before, comma after |
| *therefore* | *so* | result | at the start of the second sentence (or after *and*) | full stop before, comma after |
| *although* | *but* | contrast | at the start of the first clause, or the middle | comma between the two clauses; no *but* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate letter is formal, and a formal reader notices *but* and *so* at the start of every

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[however](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/however) gives the sense "Nevertheless; yet, still; in spite
of that". It says that at the start of a sentence it is "widely accepted if followed immediately by a
comma", and that joining two sentences with a comma before *however* is "traditionally considered an
error". Its examples include "He told me not to do it. However, I did it anyway." and "She wanted to go;
however, she decided against it."
[therefore](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/therefore) gives "Consequently, by or in consequence of that
or this cause; referring to something previously stated".
[Contrasting ideas: 'although', 'despite' and others](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/contrasting-ideas-although-despite-others)
says "After *although* and *even though*, we use a subject and a verb", with "I enjoyed the course,
although I would have liked more grammar practice."
[Comma splice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma_splice) defines it as "the use of a comma to join two
independent clauses". It says comma splices "often arise when writers use conjunctive adverbs (such as
furthermore, however, or moreover)", and that the usual corrections are a semicolon or separate
sentences.

**However: two sentences.** Finish the first fact with a full stop. Start the second with *However* and
a comma:

> The first offer is cheaper. However, it has no repair service.

If the two sentences are very close, a semicolon can replace the full stop: *The first offer is cheaper;
however, it has no repair service.* The full stop is always safe.

**Therefore: two sentences, for a result.** The same pattern:

> The second offer includes repairs for three years. Therefore, we recommend it.

Inside one sentence, *therefore* can follow *and*: *It includes repairs and is therefore better value.*

**Although: one sentence, two clauses.** *Although* goes in front of the fact you're admitting. A comma
separates the two clauses when *although* comes first:

> Although the second offer is more expensive, it includes repairs.
> The second offer includes repairs, although it is more expensive.

**The picture:**

```text
  HOWEVER / THEREFORE           [ Fact one. ]  [ However, fact two. ]
                                             ▲            ▲
                                       full stop      comma

  ALTHOUGH                      [ Although fact one, fact two. ]
                                                   ▲
                                                 comma  (and no "but")
```

**Name the trap.** *Although* already does the job of *but*. Adding *but* to the same sentence joins it
twice. *Although it is expensive, but it includes repairs* has one join too many. Delete *but*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the comma splice with *however***:

> The first offer is cheaper, however it has no repair service.

A reader sees one sentence that should be two. Many readers won't know the name for it, but they'll read
it as careless.

The second is ***although* with *but***:

> Although it is more expensive, but it includes repairs.

The third is ***therefore* in the middle with only commas**, running three facts into one: *It is
cheaper, therefore we chose it, however it broke.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent formal writers don't put *however* in every sentence. One or two in a paragraph, at the real
turns, is plenty. Too many make the writing sound like it keeps changing its mind.

Under pressure, writing quickly, people keep the formal word and drop the full stop. Before you send
anything formal, find every *however* and *therefore* and look at the mark just before it. It should be
a full stop or a semicolon, not a comma.

A formal reader, such as a manager, an examiner or an office, is more likely to forgive a plain *but*
than a comma splice. If you're unsure, two short sentences are always correct.
