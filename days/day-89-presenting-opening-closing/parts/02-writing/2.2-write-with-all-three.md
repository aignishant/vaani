## One-line answer

Write a short formal recommendation, one paragraph, about a real choice. Use *however*, *therefore* and
*although* once each. Then mark every join with its punctuation and check it against the picture in part
2.1. Finally, run the **read-aloud test**. A reader reads your paragraph aloud once. Wherever they
stumble, run on or go back, there's a join to fix.

## The story

You rewrite the photocopier recommendation:

> We compared two offers for a new photocopier. The first is cheaper. However, it has no repair service,
> and our current machine breaks down every few months. Although the second offer costs more, it
> includes repairs for three years. Therefore, we recommend the second offer.

You ask a colleague to read it aloud. She reads it straight through, pausing in all the right places,
and at the end she says *So, the second one.* Your manager signs it the same afternoon.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **however**, **therefore**, **although**, **clause**, **contrast**, **result**, **comma splice**,
  **semicolon**: from [part 2.1](2.1-three-formal-joins.md).
- **recommendation**: a short piece of writing that says which option you think is best, and why.
- **join**: here, the place where two ideas meet, with its word and its punctuation.
- **read-aloud test**: a reader reads your writing aloud once. You mark every place they stumble or go
  back.

## Why Vaani needs it

Using the three words correctly in exercises is one thing. Using them in a real paragraph, where they
have to carry a real argument, is what the phase 5 gate letter will ask of you. The read-aloud test also
works on any formal page you write from now on.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **three formal words in three sentences in a row**. *However … Therefore
… Although …* sounds like a grammar exercise. Put ordinary sentences between them.

The second is ***however* in the wrong place for its meaning**. *However* turns *against* the sentence
before it. If the next sentence agrees, you need *also* or *in addition*, not *however*.

The third is ***therefore* with no reason before it**. It points back to "something previously stated",
as the dictionary says. If the sentence before isn't the reason, the *therefore* points at nothing.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

In a real recommendation, the *therefore* sentence is often the only one a busy reader looks at. Make it
the last sentence, and make it say exactly what you recommend.

Under pressure, people write the paragraph and never read it aloud. The read-aloud test takes one
reading, and it catches the joins your eye skips.

A formal reader hears your writing in their head as they read. A comma splice makes that voice run on.
A full stop gives it a breath.
