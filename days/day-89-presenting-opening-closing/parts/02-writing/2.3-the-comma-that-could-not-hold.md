## One-line answer

Make a copy of your paragraph with the three joins broken on purpose. Put a comma before *however*, a
comma before *therefore*, and *but* after the *although* clause. Give that copy and your real paragraph
to two readers. Each one reads aloud, and then says in one sentence what you're recommending. The broken
copy should make its reader stumble or misread. Your real paragraph should be read straight through and
understood.

## The story

Your colleague shows you a report from another branch:

> The new rota is fairer, however some staff prefer the old one, therefore we have kept both, although
> the new one is better, but it is harder to plan.

She reads it aloud and runs out of breath halfway. *So did they keep the new one or not?* Neither of
you can say. Each fact is fine on its own. The joins have glued them into one sentence, and it points in
four directions.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate letter will be read by someone who can't ask you what you meant. A comma splice, or a
sentence with *although* and *but*, is visible on the page. It's also something you can learn to see in
your own writing, and this part is where you practise seeing it.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Comma splice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comma_splice): "the use of a comma to join two independent
clauses"; they "often arise when writers use conjunctive adverbs (such as furthermore, however, or
moreover)".
[however](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/however): joining with a comma before *however* is
"traditionally considered an error".
[Contrasting ideas: 'although', 'despite' and others](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/contrasting-ideas-although-despite-others):
"After *although* and *even though*, we use a subject and a verb."

**Step one: make copy J.** Copy your paragraph, and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Change the full stop before *However* to a comma, and make the *H* small.
2. Change the full stop before *Therefore* to a comma, and make the *T* small.
3. Add *but* after the comma in the *although* sentence.

**Step two: the read-aloud and one-sentence tests.** Give J to one reader and R to a different reader.
Each reads their copy aloud once. Mark every stumble. Then ask:

> In one sentence, what is the writer recommending?

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if R's reader stumbles at a join, or gives the
wrong recommendation. It's **GREEN** when R's reader reads straight through and gives the right
recommendation, and J's reader stumbles at least once or gives a vaguer answer.

**Step three: the repair.** If R went red, look at the mark before the join where they stumbled. Make it a
full stop, or split the sentence. Give R to a new reader.

**Step four: one real page.** Take one real formal thing you've written in English: an email, a form, an
application. Find every *however*, *therefore* and *although* in it and check the punctuation.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who reads J smoothly anyway**. Fluent readers
sometimes fix the punctuation in their heads. Ask them afterwards: *Did any sentence feel too long?*
Their answer counts as a stumble.

The second is **an R with a comma splice you didn't make on purpose**. That's the real finding. Fix it,
and write it in your error ledger.

The third, in real writing, is ***however* after *but***: *but however, it is expensive.* It's one
contrast word too many, the same trap as *although … but*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Native writers make comma splices too. The reference page says they "often arise" with words like
*however*, so every *however* is the first place to look.

Under pressure, you'll join sentences with commas because it feels faster. It isn't faster for the
reader. They have to find the stops you didn't put in.

A formal reader rarely says *comma splice*. They just say the writing is hard to follow, and ask you
what you meant, which is exactly what the one-sentence test measures.
