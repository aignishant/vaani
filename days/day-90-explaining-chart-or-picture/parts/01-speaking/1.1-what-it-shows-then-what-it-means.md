## One-line answer

Explain a chart or a picture in four moves. Say **what it shows** (*This chart shows our electricity
use, month by month, this year*). Give **the big picture** (*Overall, it went up*). Point to **one or
two things to notice**, with their numbers (*As you can see, it jumped from 90 units in March to 210 in
May*). Then say **what it means** (*So the new air cooler costs us about a hundred and twenty units a
month*). A listener who hears only numbers remembers nothing. A listener who hears the big picture
first remembers the numbers that prove it.

## The story

At a family meeting about money, you show everyone the graph on the back of the electricity bill. You
start at the left and read every bar:

> January, ninety-five. February, eighty-eight. March, ninety. April, a hundred and forty. May, two
> hundred and ten …

By June, your uncle has stopped listening. Your mother asks, *So is it going up or down?*

Your cousin takes the bill and says:

> This shows how much electricity we've used each month this year. Overall, it's more than doubled.
> Look here: it was about ninety until March, then it shot up in April and May. That's when we got the
> air cooler. So the cooler is costing us more than the fridge and the TV together.

Everyone nods. Your uncle says, *Then we use it only at night.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **opening**, **the map**: the first lines of a talk, and the line that says how many points are
  coming, from
  [day 89](../../../day-89-presenting-opening-closing/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-opening-four-lines.md).
- **numbers out loud**: saying numbers so they're understood, from
  [day 10](../../../day-10-numbers-times-dates/parts/01-speaking/1.1-numbers-out-loud.md).
- **describing what you see**: saying what's happening in a picture, from
  [day 27](../../../day-27-happening-right-now/parts/01-speaking/1.1-say-what-you-can-see-happening.md).
- **chart**: a picture that shows numbers, such as bars, a line or slices of a circle. A *graph* is one
  kind of chart.
- **trend**: the general direction of numbers over time: up, down or level.
- **overview**: the big picture, said in one sentence, before any detail.
- **so what**: what the numbers mean for the listener.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. What it shows | names the chart: what, and when | *This chart shows our electricity use, month by month, this year.* |
| 2. The big picture | one sentence, the overall trend | *Overall, it's more than doubled.* |
| 3. One or two things to notice | points, with numbers | *As you can see, it jumped from about 90 units in March to 210 in May.* |
| 4. What it means | the *so what* | *So the cooler is costing us more than the fridge and TV together.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

At work, charts turn up in meetings and updates: sales, stock, complaints, attendance. On day 99 you'll
give a full talk with three points, and a chart can be one of them. In the phase 5 gate interview, *Tell
me about a result you achieved* is a chart without the picture: a big picture, one number, and what it
meant.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Describing charts](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/describing-charts)
is about writing, but its order is the one a listener needs too. It says "Start by saying what the
charts show". It says the next part "should provide an overview of the key features of the
information", and that you should "describe the patterns or trends in more detail" but "only select the
most important ones". Its model begins "Overall, smartphone ownership increased during the six-year
period."

**Move 1: what it shows.** What is measured, and over what time. *This chart shows …* / *This is a
picture of …*. Say it before you point at anything.

**Move 2: the big picture.** One sentence, starting *Overall, …*: up, down, level, or *up then down*.
Say it slowly. It's the sentence everything else hangs on.

**Move 3: one or two things to notice.** Not every number, just the ones that prove the big picture, or
the one surprise. Use *As you can see* or *Look here* while you point. Say the numbers slowly and
clearly, and say the unit: *units*, *rupees*, *per cent*, *people*.

**Move 4: what it means.** *So …*, *This means …*, *That's why …*. This is what the listener takes
away. Without it, they have facts and no reason to care.

**A picture works the same way.** *This is a photo of our street during the flood* (what it shows).
*You can see the water came up to the second step* (the thing to notice). *That's why we're asking for
a drain* (what it means). A picture has no *overall* trend, so the big picture is the scene in one line:
*The whole road is under water.*

**Say numbers so they land.** Say *per cent* after each percentage. Say years in two halves, as on
[day 10](../../../day-10-numbers-times-dates/parts/01-speaking/1.2-times-and-dates-out-loud.md):
*twenty twenty-five*. For a big number, round it and say so: *about two hundred*. Pause just before the key
number, as on day 85.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **reading every number, left to right**: *January, ninety-five.
February, eighty-eight …*. The listener gets numbers, and no picture.

The second is **no *what it shows***: *As you can see, it goes up here.* What goes up? The listener
hasn't seen the title.

The third is **no *so what***. You give the big picture and the numbers perfectly and stop. The listener
thinks, *and?*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

At work, the person explaining a chart usually knows it too well. They skip move 1 because it's obvious
to them. It isn't obvious to anyone else. Always say what the chart shows, even when it has a title.

Under pressure, in a meeting where someone interrupts with *so what's the point?*, jump straight to move
4. Then go back for the numbers if they want them.

A native listener remembers the big picture and the *so what*, and one number at most. Choose that
number carefully.
