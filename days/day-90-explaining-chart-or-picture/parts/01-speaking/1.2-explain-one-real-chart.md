## One-line answer

Take one real chart and explain it to a listener who **can't see it**. Sit back to back, or talk on the
phone. You use the four moves, and they sketch the chart on paper from your words alone. Then compare
their sketch with the real chart. If the shape and the one or two key numbers match, your explanation
worked.

## The story

Your friend, who's studying in another city, calls to ask whether the fees at your old college have
gone up. You have the college's fee chart on your screen. She doesn't.

You say: *This shows the fees for the first year, for the last five years. Overall, they've gone up
every year, but slowly, except for one jump. They were about forty thousand in 2021, stayed around
there for two years, then jumped to fifty-five thousand in 2023. They've gone up by only a thousand or
two a year since. So the jump was the new building, and it's not going to happen again.*

She says, *So a step, then flat-ish.* That's exactly the shape.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **chart**, **trend**, **overview**, **so what**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-what-it-shows-then-what-it-means.md).
- **back to back**: two people sitting with their backs together, so one can't see what the other is
  looking at.
- **sketch**: a quick rough drawing.
- **shape**: the overall line of a chart: rising, falling, a step, a peak, flat.
- **back-to-back test**: a listener who can't see the chart sketches it from your words, and you compare
  the sketch with the real one.

## Why Vaani needs it

Explaining a chart to people who can see it lets you point. Explaining it on a call, or to someone at the
back of the room, means the words have to carry the shape. If a listener can draw your chart, your
explanation will survive any room. That's the level day 99's full talk needs.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a big picture with no shape word**: *It changes a lot.* Changes how?
Use a shape word: *goes up*, *drops*, *jumps*, *stays flat*, *peaks and then falls*.

The second is **numbers without units**: *from forty to fifty-five*. Forty what? Say *thousand rupees*,
*per cent*, *units*.

The third is **the listener sketching as you read numbers**, getting the dots right and the story wrong.
If you gave the big picture first, their pen knows where it's going before the numbers arrive.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

In online meetings, half the people may not have the chart open, or may be looking at the wrong slide.
Always say what the chart shows before *as you can see*. Some of them can't.

Under pressure, when a listener says *sorry, I'm lost*, don't go back to the first number. Go back to the
big picture: *Overall, it went up, with one big jump in 2023.*

A native listener often repeats your big picture back to you in their own words: *So, a step, then
flat.* That's the sign it worked. If they repeat a number instead, the shape didn't land.
