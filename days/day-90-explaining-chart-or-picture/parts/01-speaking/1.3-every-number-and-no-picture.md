## One-line answer

Record your chart explanation twice. **N** is broken on purpose: every number read out left to right,
with no *what it shows*, no *overall* and no *so what*. **E** is your real four-move explanation. Run
the back-to-back test with two new listeners, one for each. N's listener should get dots with no shape,
or no meaning. E's listener should get the shape, the key number and the *so what*.

## The story

At work, the monthly meeting has a slide of complaint numbers. The person presenting reads it aloud:
*Week one, twelve. Week two, fifteen. Week three, eleven. Week four, thirty-one.* Then the next slide.

Afterwards, nobody can tell you whether complaints are a problem. You find the slide later and see it at
once: steady, then a huge spike in week four, the week the new delivery company started. That's the
whole story, and it was never said.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

A talk is judged by what the listener can say afterwards. The phase 5 gate interview and day 99's talk
both depend on a listener who hears you once and remembers the point. This part lets you hear, in your
own voice, what happens when the point is left out and only the numbers are left.

"Start by saying what the charts show"; "The second paragraph should provide an overview of the key

**Step one: record N.** Say *deliberate* first. Read every value on your chart from left to right, with
its label. Don't say what the chart is, and don't say *overall* or *so*.

**Step two: record E.** Your four-move explanation from part 1.2.

**Step three: the back-to-back test, twice.** Two new listeners who haven't seen the chart. N to one, E
to the other. Each sketches while listening, then answers:

vague. Fix it, record E again, and try a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an N listener who guesses the shape from the numbers**.
With only four or five numbers, they might. Use a chart with more values for N if you can, or read them
at an even pace so no number stands out.

The second is **an E that reads all the numbers after the big picture**. Only one or two numbers, the
ones that prove the shape.

The third, in real presentations, is **the chart that speaks for itself**. The presenter says *As you can
see …* and waits. Nobody sees what they see. Say it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Reading every number feels thorough and safe, because you can't be wrong. But the listener wanted
judgment: which number matters, and why. That's what move 4 gives them.

Under pressure, with a chart you didn't make, you might not know the *so what*. Say what you can see and
ask: *The big jump is in week four. Does anyone know what happened that week?* That's still move 4.

A native listener asks *so what?* in their head after every number. Answer it before they have to ask
out loud.
