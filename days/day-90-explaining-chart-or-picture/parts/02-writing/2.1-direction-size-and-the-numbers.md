## One-line answer

To describe numbers in words, write three things in each sentence. The **direction**: *rose*, *fell*,
*peaked*, *levelled off*. The **size**: *sharply*, *steadily*, *slightly*. The **numbers**, with the
right small word: *from* the start, *to* the end, *by* the amount of change. *Sales rose steadily from
200 to 260*. *They rose by 60*. Open with one **Overall** sentence. A reader should be able to draw the
line from your words.

## The story

Your aunt runs a small tailoring shop and is applying to a bank for a loan. The form asks for "a short
description of your sales over the last year", in words. She gives you her notebook of monthly numbers
and asks you to write it.

You write:

> Sales were 210 in January, 205 in February, 215 in March, 300 in April, 420 in May, 430 in June …

It's all correct, and it's every number. The bank officer phones her and asks, *So is the business
growing?* She says *yes, of course*, and he says *It doesn't say so anywhere.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **date**: written in full, so nobody can misread it, from
  [day 10](../../../day-10-numbers-times-dates/parts/02-writing/2.1-writing-the-date.md).
- **past simple**: the past form for a finished time, often ending *-ed*, from
  [day 41](../../../day-41-what-you-did-yesterday/parts/02-writing/2.1-add-ed-and-what-happens-to-the-spelling.md).
- **irregular verb**: a verb whose past doesn't end *-ed*, like *rise → rose* and *fall → fell*, from
  [day 42](../../../day-42-three-sounds-of-ed/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-verbs-that-refuse-ed.md).
- **trend**: the general direction of numbers over time: up, down or level.
- **overview**: the big picture in one sentence, often starting *Overall*.
- **adverb**: here, a word that says *how much* or *how fast* something changed: *sharply*, *steadily*,
  *slightly*.
- **per cent**: out of a hundred, written *%* or *per cent*.

**Direction words:**

| Direction | Verb (past) | Noun |
| --- | --- | --- |
| up | *rose*, *increased*, *went up* | *a rise*, *an increase* |
| down | *fell*, *decreased*, *went down* | *a fall*, *a decrease* |
| highest point | *peaked (at …)* | *a peak* |
| stopped changing | *levelled off*, *stayed the same* | — |

**Size words:**

| Size | Adverb (after the verb) | Adjective (before the noun) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| big and fast | *sharply* | *a sharp rise* |
| even, over time | *steadily* | *a steady fall* |
| small | *slightly* | *a slight increase* |

## Why Vaani needs it

Status updates, reports and applications at work are full of numbers: sales, costs, attendance,
complaints. On day 97 you'll write instructions and rules on paper, where numbers must be exact. And the
formal letters of this phase often need one sentence of numbers that a reader can trust.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Describing charts](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/b1-writing/describing-charts)
says "Start by saying what the charts show", then "provide an overview of the key features", and "only
select the most important ones". It says to keep one style of "% or per cent", and to "Be careful to use
the correct tenses to describe the time periods shown". Its model uses "Overall, smartphone ownership
increased during the six-year period", "rose steadily, starting at around 35% in 2011 and reaching about
77% by 2016", and "from 46% to 84%".
[by](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/by) gives the sense "Indicates the amount of change, difference or
discrepancy", with "Our stock is up by ten percent."
[peak](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/peak): "To reach a highest degree or maximum."
[level off](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/level_off): "To get to a stable level; to cease climbing,
descending, or oscillating."

**Sentence 1: what it shows.** *This description covers the shop's monthly sales from January to
December 2025.*

**Sentence 2: overall.** One sentence, the whole shape: *Overall, sales more than doubled during the
year.*

**Then the key changes, with direction, size and numbers:**

> Sales stayed at about 210 a month until March.
> They then rose sharply, from 215 in March to 420 in May.
> After that, they levelled off at around 430.

**The three small words.** They change the meaning, so a reader who draws the line will draw what they
say:

```text
  rose FROM 215   → where it started
  rose TO 420     → where it ended
  rose BY 205     → how much it changed (420 − 215)
  peaked AT 450   → the highest point
```

**Tense.** For a finished period, use the past simple: *rose*, *fell*, *peaked*. *Rise* and *fall* are
irregular: *rose*, *fell*, never *rised* or *falled*. For a period that runs up to today, you can use the
present perfect: *Sales have risen since March.*

**Numbers.** Keep one style for percentages, either *%* or *per cent*, all the way through. Round numbers
honestly and say so: *about*, *around*, *just over*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **every number and no overview**, like the loan form in the story. It's
correct, and the reader still can't see the shape.

The second is ***by* and *to* swapped**:

> Sales rose by 420 in May.

The writer meant *to 420*. The reader now thinks sales went up by 420, to more than 600. It's one small
word, and the number is wrong by hundreds.

The third is **the irregular past**: *Sales rised in April* or *They falled in June*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A busy reader, such as a bank officer, a manager or an examiner, often reads the *Overall* sentence and
the biggest change, and little else. If those two sentences are right, the rest is support.

Under pressure, when you're not sure of a trend word, use the plainest one: *went up*, *went down*,
*stayed the same*. Plain and correct beats fancy and wrong.

A formal reader checks your numbers against the source. Every *from*, *to* and *by* is a promise they
can check. Make sure each one is true.
