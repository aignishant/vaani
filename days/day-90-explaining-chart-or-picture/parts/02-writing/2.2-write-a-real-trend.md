## One-line answer

Take a real set of numbers over time and write one paragraph about them, of five or six sentences.
Say what it shows, give the overall trend, then describe the two or three key changes with direction,
size and numbers. Then run the **sketch test**. A reader draws the line from your paragraph alone, and
you compare it with the real numbers.

## The story

You rewrite your aunt's sales description:

> This description covers the shop's monthly sales from January to December 2025. Overall, sales more
> than doubled during the year. They stayed at about 210 a month until March. They then rose sharply,
> from 215 in March to 420 in May, when the shop began taking school uniform orders. After that, sales
> levelled off at around 430 for the rest of the year.

Your cousin, who's never seen the notebook, reads it and draws a line: flat, then a steep climb, then
flat again, higher. It's the same shape as the notebook. The loan is approved the following week.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **trend**, **overview**, **adverb**, **per cent**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-direction-size-and-the-numbers.md).
- **key change**: a point where the line changes direction or speed.
- **data**: the numbers you're describing.
- **sketch test**: a reader draws the line from your paragraph, and you compare it with the real numbers.

## Why Vaani needs it

A trend paragraph is a small, exact piece of formal writing that's easy to check. If you can write one
that a stranger can draw, you can put numbers into any report, status update or application, and they'll
be read the way you meant.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a reason you're guessing**: *Sales rose because people liked the new
designs.* If you don't know, leave the reason out. A trend paragraph is a record of numbers, not a
guess.

The second is **too many key changes**. Five or six, and the paragraph turns back into a list. Choose the
two or three that make the shape.

The third is **mixed number styles**: *35%*, then *forty per cent*, then *0.5*. Choose one style for
percentages and one for other numbers, and keep them.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Reports at work often put a chart and a paragraph side by side. The paragraph is what gets copied into
emails and read on phones, where the chart may not show. Write it as if the chart isn't there.

Under pressure, people copy numbers straight from a table into sentences. Check each *by* by
subtracting, every time. It's the easiest place for a number to go wrong without anyone noticing.

A formal reader trusts a paragraph that uses *about* and *around* honestly more than one that gives
exact numbers it can't back up.
