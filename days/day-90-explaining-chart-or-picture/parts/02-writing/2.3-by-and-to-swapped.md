## One-line answer

Make a copy of your trend paragraph and break it on purpose. Delete the *Overall* sentence, swap one *to*
for *by*, and turn one irregular past into a wrong *-ed* form. Give that copy to one reader and your real
paragraph to another, and run the sketch test with both. The broken copy should produce a wrong line.
Your real paragraph should produce the right one.

## The story

Your aunt's neighbour writes his own sales description for the same bank:

> Sales were 150 in January. In April they rised by 300. In June they falled slightly.

The bank officer reads *rised by 300* as a jump from 150 to 450. It was actually a rise *to* 300. The
application goes to the next stage on figures the business never had. When the bank asks for the
books, the figures don't match, and the neighbour has to explain a mistake he didn't know he'd made.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

A formal page with a wrong number is worse than one with no number. A reader who trusts you will act on
it. This part shows you, with your own numbers, how one small word moves a line on someone else's
sketch.

2. Change one *to* into *by*, keeping the same number: *rose to 420* becomes *rose by 420*.
3. Change one *rose* or *fell* into *rised* or *falled*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who notices *rised* and stops drawing** to
correct your English. Ask them to draw first and comment after. The spelling error is a visible
mistake. The *by* error is an invisible one. That's the point.

The second is **a W where the swapped *by* happens to be right**, because the start was zero. Choose a
number where *by* and *to* give different heights.

The third, in real writing, is **the swap with percentages**. *Prices rose by 10%* means they're a tenth
higher than before. *Prices rose to 10%* only makes sense if the thing measured is itself a percentage,
like an interest rate. Read every *by* and *to* next to a *%* twice.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Many readers won't catch a swapped *by*. They'll just believe it. That's why the check is yours, not
theirs: subtract, and compare.

Under pressure, when you're writing numbers from memory, say *about* and round. A rounded true number is
safer than an exact wrong one.

A formal reader who finds one wrong number starts checking all of them. A reader who finds none trusts
the page. That trust is what the small words earn you.
