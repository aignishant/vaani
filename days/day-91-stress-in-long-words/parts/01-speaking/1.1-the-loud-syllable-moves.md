## One-line answer

In a family of long words, the loud syllable moves when the ending changes: **PHO**tograph,
pho**TO**grapher, pho**TO**graphy, photo**GRA**phic. The endings tell you where it goes. Before *-ic*,
*-tion* and *-sion*, the loud syllable is usually the one just before the ending: photo**GRA**phic. With
*-phy*, *-cy*, *-ty*, *-gy* and *-al*, it's usually three from the end: pho**TO**graphy, de**MO**cracy.
And when the loud syllable moves, the vowels move too. The syllable that loses the stress goes weak.

## The story

You go to the college office to ask about the evening course in photography. You say:

> I want to join the PHO-to-gra-phy course.

The clerk frowns. *Photograph course? We don't do photographs here. The studio is next door.* You try
again, louder, with the same stress, and get the same answer. A student in the queue says, *I think
they mean pho-TO-graphy*. The clerk says *Oh! Room 4*.

You said every sound correctly. You put the loud part where it goes in *photograph*, and the clerk heard
*photograph*.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **syllable**: one beat of a word. *Pho-to-graph* has three.
- **stress**, **the loud syllable**: the syllable said louder, longer and higher, from
  [day 7](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-loud-syllable.md).
- **schwa**, **the lazy vowel**: the short, weak *uh* sound in syllables with no stress, from
  [day 30](../../../day-30-the-schwa/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-lazy-vowel.md).
- **stress for emphasis**: pushing a whole word to show what you mean, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/01-speaking/1.1-push-the-word-you-mean.md). Today's
  stress is inside one word, and it's fixed, not a choice.
- **word family**: words built from the same root, such as *photograph*, *photography*, *photographic*.
- **ending**: the last part added to a word, such as *-ic*, *-y* or *-er*. It's also called a *suffix*.
- **full vowel**, **weak vowel**: a vowel said clearly, or a vowel said as schwa.

## Why Vaani needs it

Formal and work English is full of long words from one family: *economy* and *economic*, *photograph*
and *photographer*, *politics* and *political*. In an interview, a presentation or a meeting, a long
word with the stress in the wrong place is often heard as a different word, or not recognised at all.
On days 98 and 99 you'll explain a technical thing and give a full talk, both full of long words.

**The pages behind the rule, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Stress and vowel reduction in English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_and_vowel_reduction_in_English)
uses this very family. In *photograph* and *photographic*, "the first *o* is pronounced with a full
vowel" and "the second *o* with a reduced vowel ([schwa])". In *photography* and *photographer*, "where
the stress moves to the second syllable, the first syllable now contains schwa while the second syllable
contains a full vowel".
[The Rules of Word Stress, Part 2](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/a/the-rules-of-word-stress-part-2/4777534.html)
says "Words ending in –ic, –tion and –sion usually have their stress on the second-to-last syllable", and
for *-al*, *-gy*, *-cy*, *-ty* and *-phy*, "the stress is on the third-to-last syllable", with
*photography* and *democracy* among its examples.

**Count from the end.** The endings pull the stress to a fixed place, counted backwards:

```text
  ending          count back      example
  -ic -tion -sion   2 from end     photo·GRA·phic      demo·CRA·tic
  -phy -cy -ty      3 from end     pho·TO·gra·phy      de·MO·cra·cy
  -gy -al
```

**The vowels move with it.** In *PHOtograph*, the first *o* is a full *oh* and the second is a weak
schwa. In *phoTOgraphy*, it's the other way round: the first *o* goes weak (*fuh*) and the second is
full. So moving the stress changes two sounds, not one. That's why the wrong stress can sound like a
different word.

**Name the trap.** Your first language may give every syllable the same weight, or always stress the
same place. English does neither. Also, *usually* means usually: some words break these patterns. When
in doubt, open the dictionary page and listen.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the family stress carried over**: *PHOtography*, said with the stress
from *photograph*. The listener hears *photograph* and a stray ending.

The second is **every syllable equal**: *pho-to-gra-phy*, four even beats. Nothing is loud and nothing
goes weak, and the word doesn't sound like any word the listener knows.

The third is **the right loud syllable with full vowels everywhere**: *foh-TO-gra-fee*. It's better, but
it's still hard to recognise. The first syllable needs to go weak.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

The stress pattern is part of what makes a long word recognisable, as the clerk in the story showed.
Get the loud syllable right and a listener can often forgive a vowel. Get it wrong and even perfect
sounds may not help.

Under pressure, in an interview, long words are where people speed up and flatten out. Slow down on the
one long word in a sentence, and make its loud syllable clearly louder.

A native listener who doesn't recognise a word often asks you to repeat it. If you repeat it the same
way, it won't help. Move the stress, not the volume.
