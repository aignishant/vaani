## One-line answer

Find three long-word families from your own work, study or life, such as *economy, economic* or
*electric, electricity*. Guess where the loud syllable goes in each word using the endings, then check
every guess on a dictionary page with audio. Record each family, tapping the loud syllable. Then run the
**which-beat test**. A listener hears each word once and taps the syllable they heard as loud.

## The story

At your new job in a pharmacy, the long words arrive every day: *medicine, medical, medication*;
*prescribe, prescription*. You've been saying them all with the same stress, and customers keep saying
*sorry?*

You write the three families on the back of a receipt, guess the loud syllable in each, and look them up
on your phone during the lunch break. Two of your guesses were wrong. By the evening shift, you're saying
*MED-i-cine, MED-i-cal, med-i-CA-tion*, and the *sorry?*s have stopped. (The pages
[medicine](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/medicine), [medical](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/medical)
and [medication](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/medication), opened on 2026-09-24, give /ˈmɛd.ɪ.sɪn/,
/ˈmɛdɪkl̩/ and /mɛdɪˈkeɪʃən/.)

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[The Rules of Word Stress, Part 2](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/a/the-rules-of-word-stress-part-2/4777534.html):
*-ic*, *-tion*, *-sion* "usually have their stress on the second-to-last syllable"; *-al*, *-gy*, *-cy*,
*-ty*, *-phy*: "the stress is on the third-to-last syllable".
[Stress and vowel reduction in English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_and_vowel_reduction_in_English):
when the stress moves, one syllable's vowel goes to schwa and another's becomes full.

**Step one: collect three families.** Long words you really say or hear: at work, in your course, in the
news. Each family needs at least two words with different endings. If you're stuck, look for pairs like
*economy/economic*, *politics/political*, *electric/electricity*, *nation/national* in things you read
this week, but choose your own.

**Step two: guess.** For each word, count the syllables, find the ending, and mark your guess for the
loud syllable in capitals.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **trusting the guess**. The ending rule says *usually*.
[Catholic](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Catholic) (/ˈkæθəlɪk/) and
[Arabic](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Arabic) (/ˈæɹəbɪk/) put the loud syllable first, not just before
*-ic*. Both pages were opened on 2026-09-24. The *Arabic* page also lists an Indian English pronunciation
with the stress on the second syllable, so accents differ too. Always check, and listen.

The second is **a tap without a loud voice**. Your hand taps the right beat, and your voice stays flat.
The tap is there to pull your voice with it. If it doesn't, exaggerate at first.

The third is **the family in isolation only**. Words said alone often sound right, then flatten out in a
sentence. Always finish with the word in a real sentence.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

People who work with long words every day, such as nurses, accountants and engineers, often learn them
by ear from colleagues. If you're new, listen for how colleagues say the words you use, and check any you
hear two ways.

Under pressure, the longest word in your sentence is the one that flattens first. Slow it down a little,
and let the loud syllable be clearly louder.

A native listener doesn't usually correct your stress. They just take longer to understand, or say
*sorry?* When *sorry?* comes after a long word, the stress is the first thing to check.
