## One-line answer

Write a real formal notice for a real place, of five to seven sentences. Use the passive for the rules,
one report sentence and, if there is one, a piece of careful news, and keep one active line with a
contact. Check every passive for *be* and the third form. Then run the **what-and-who test**. A reader
says what they must do and who they'd ask. If they can answer both, the notice works.

## The story

You rewrite the clinic notice:

> **Changes to appointments from 1 October**
> Patients are requested to arrive before their appointment time. Late arrivals may be asked to book a
> new appointment. Payment is now accepted by card only. The card machine was installed on 20 September
> and has been tested. For any questions, please speak to the reception desk.

The doctor pins it up. A patient reads it, takes out a card, and asks you, *Can I book the next one here
too?* She knew what to do and who to ask.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **passive**, **active**, **doer**, **notice**, **modal**, **past participle**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-three-formal-jobs-for-the-passive.md).
- **heading**: the short title at the top of a notice, saying what it's about.
- **contact line**: one active sentence saying who to ask.
- **what-and-who test**: a reader says what the notice asks them to do, and who they'd ask about it.

The notice's shape:

| Line | Voice | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heading | — | *Changes to appointments from 1 October* |
| Rules | passive (*are requested*, *must be*, *is accepted*) | *Payment is now accepted by card only.* |
| Report | passive, past (*was installed*) | *The card machine was installed on 20 September.* |
| Careful news, if any | passive (*has been*, *will be*) | *The Saturday clinic has been cancelled.* |
| Contact | active, with a name or a place | *Please speak to the reception desk.* |

## Why Vaani needs it

A notice is one of the shortest formal texts there is, and one of the most read. Writing one for a real
place, with real readers, is practice for the rules and instructions on days 96 and 97, and for any
formal page where the passive does its job without hiding anyone.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **all active, pointing at the reader**: *You must pay by card. You must
come early. We will cancel you.* Every sentence is correct, and the whole notice feels like a telling-off.

The second is **all passive, with nobody in it**: *It is requested that payment be made by card. Late
arrival will be dealt with.* Dealt with by whom? How? The reader can't answer the test's second
question.

The third is **a report sentence in the present when it happened once**: *The machine is installed on
20 September.* A single finished event with a date takes *was*.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Good notices are short, and they're read standing up. The passive keeps the rules short, because it
drops the *we* and the *you*. In a full sentence, always keep the *be*.

Under pressure, when a rule has upset people, the pull is to write everything in the passive so no one
can be blamed. Resist it for the contact line. Readers are calmer when they know who to ask.

A formal reader, such as a patient, a resident or a customer, reads the heading and the first rule, then
looks for a name. Give them all three.
