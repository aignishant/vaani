## One-line answer

Make a copy of your notice that breaks the passive on purpose in two ways. Put in **form errors**: one
passive missing *be*, and one with the wrong verb form after *be*. And **hide** everyone: turn the
contact line into a passive with no doer, and add *It has been decided that …*. Give that copy and your
real notice to two readers. Ask them to circle anything that looks wrong, and to run the what-and-who
test. The broken copy should get circles and no *who*. Your real notice should get neither.

## The story

A notice goes up in your building's lift:

> It has been decided that the terrace will be closed. Keys must returned. Any problems will be dealt
> with.

Nobody knows who decided, why, until when, or who to give the keys to. Two residents knock on the
secretary's door. He didn't write it. The next day a second notice appears underneath, handwritten: *By
WHO??*

The first notice was grammatical in places and wrong in one, but that isn't what upset people. It had no
one in it.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **passive**, **doer**, **past participle**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-three-formal-jobs-for-the-passive.md).
- **contact line**, **what-and-who test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-notice.md).
- **form error**: a passive built wrongly, with *be* missing or the wrong verb form after it.
- **hidden doer**: a passive that leaves out a doer the reader needs to know.
- **RED / GREEN**: a check that failed, and a check that passed.

## Why Vaani needs it

A formal page can be grammatical and still fail its reader, or have one small form error that makes the
writer look careless. This part trains your eye for both at once. Both matter in the formal letters and
notices of this phase, and in the gate.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Passives](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/passives): "We make the
passive using the verb *be* + past participle"; "The past participle stays the same."
[English passive voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_passive_voice): *Mistakes have been made*
as a criticised example of avoiding responsibility.

**Step one: make copy H.** Copy your notice, and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. In one passive, delete *be*: *must be made* becomes *must made*.
2. In another, use the wrong verb form after *be*: *is accepted* becomes *is accept*.
3. Add a first line: *It has been decided that …* with your change.
4. Change the contact line to *Any questions will be dealt with.*

**Step two: the circle and what-and-who tests.** Give H to one reader and N to a different reader. Ask
each to circle anything that looks wrong, then ask:

> What does this ask you to do? And if you had a question, who would you ask?

**Step three: the repair.** If N went red on a circle, check that passive: is *be* there, and is the third

or a role. Give N to a new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who circles the passive itself** because it
sounds formal to them. *Is accepted* isn't an error. Ask them: *Is it wrong, or just unusual?*

The second is **an N that's all active after the repair**. You don't need to remove every passive. Keep
the rules and the report in the passive, and make only the contact line active.

The third, in real notices, is **the doer that's needed but missing in the middle**: *Keys must be
returned.* Returned to whom? Sometimes the passive is right and it still needs a place: *Keys must be
returned to the security office.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Notices that hide everyone usually aren't trying to. The writer wanted to sound formal and reached for
*It has been decided*. The fix is almost always one active sentence with a name, a role or a place.

Under pressure, writing bad news, the passive feels like protection. For the news itself, it can be kind:
*The terrace will be closed.* For the reason and the contact, name someone: *The committee closed it
after the railing was damaged. Please contact the secretary in Flat 3.*

A formal reader forgives a notice for being short. They don't forgive one they can't act on. The
what-and-who test asks the two questions they'll ask.
