## One-line answer

To get a better deal from someone you'll work with, don't just ask for less. Say what you like, name
the gap with a reason, and then **trade**: *If I …, could you …?* You give something small that costs
you little, and you get something you want. Then say the whole deal back, so both of you heard the
same thing.

## The story

Your kitchen cupboards need new doors. A carpenter comes, looks, measures, and says *Twelve thousand,
done in two weeks*.

You've got ten thousand, so you say:

> Twelve thousand is too much. Can you do ten?

He shakes his head. *Wood is expensive now.* You say *OK, twelve*, and feel bad about it all week.

Your neighbour has the same carpenter the next month. She says:

> I like your work, and I'd like you to do it. My budget is ten thousand, though. If I pay half today,
> could you do it for ten and a half?

He thinks, and says *Eleven, and I'll add the handles*. She says:

> So that's eleven thousand, with the handles, half today, finished by the twentieth. Great.

She didn't argue about the price of wood. She gave him something he wanted, money today, and asked for
something back.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **negotiate**: to talk about a price until both sides agree, from
  [day 34](../../../day-34-money-and-prices/parts/01-speaking/1.2-asking-for-a-better-price.md). Day 34
  was a stall and one question. Today it's a deal with someone you'll work with, and more than one
  thing on the table.
- **the ladder (of requests)**: the steps from *Can you …?* up to *Could you …?* and *Would you mind
  …?*, from [day 81](../../../day-81-formal-and-informal/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-request-ladder.md).
- **deal**: what two sides agree: the price, and everything that comes with it.
- **the gap**: the distance between what they asked for and what you can give.
- **trade**: giving something so that you get something back.
- **the if-trade**: a sentence of the shape *If I …, could you …?*
- **say-back**: repeating the whole deal at the end, so nothing is left unclear.

A deal has more in it than the price. There's **when** you pay, **when** it's finished, and **what's
included**. Each one is something you can offer or ask for.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[6 language must-haves to negotiate in English](https://englishonline.britishcouncil.org/blog/articles/6-language-must-haves-to-negotiate-in-english/)
gives a proposal in exactly this shape: "Of course, if you wanted to buy 2,000, we could reduce the
price to £4.50 per gnome." And it closes with a summary: "So, we're in agreement. £3.75 per gnome is
broadly reasonable for both of us".
[Negotiating](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/business/business/magazine/negotiating)
advises "Ask questions and listen" and "Look at creative options".

**Four moves.**

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Warm | tells them you want to do the deal | *I like your work, and I'd like you to do it.* |
| 2. Gap + reason | says the problem is the number, not them | *My budget is ten thousand, though.* |
| 3. If-trade | gives something, asks for something | *If I pay half today, could you do it for ten and a half?* |
| 4. Say-back | puts the whole deal into one sentence | *So that's eleven, with the handles, finished by the twentieth.* |

**The if-trade.** The *if* part is what you give. The *could you* part is what you get. Things you can
give that cost you little:

> If I pay half now, could you …?
> If I don't need it until next month, could you …?
> If I buy the paint myself, could you …?

**When the price won't move, trade on something else.** Ask for more in the deal, not less money:
*Could you include the handles?* *Could you finish by the twentieth?*

**The say-back.** Start with *So*, and name every part: the number, what's included, the date. Let the
tune fall at the end. It's a statement, not a question.

**Name the trap.** In some places a deal is closed with a nod or a handshake and nothing said. In a
second language that's risky. You may have heard *eleven* where they said *eleven and a half*. The
say-back catches it while you're still both there.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the number thrown back**:

> Too much. Ten.

The other person hears a fight. Now one of you has to lose.

The second is **the free gift**. You offer *I can pay half now* and never ask for anything back. They
say *Thank you*, and the price stays at twelve.

The third is **no say-back**. You leave thinking *eleven with handles*. He thinks *eleven, handles
extra*. The argument happens on the last day, when it's hardest to fix.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often go quiet after the if-trade. They've asked, and now they wait. That silence feels
long in a second language, but filling it with *or maybe eleven is also OK* gives the deal away.

Under pressure, when the other person is busy or impatient, people skip the warm move. Don't. One line
of *I'd like you to do this* costs nothing, and it makes a *no* less likely.

A seller hears *too much* all day. They hear *if I …, could you …?* much less often. It's easier to
say yes to, because they get something too.
