## One-line answer

Record your deal twice. In take G, give things away on purpose: offer to pay early and take the plain
version, and never ask for anything back. In take T, trade every one of them with an *if*. Play both to
two listeners and ask each one to write down the final deal. Take G ends with less for you, or with a
deal nobody can write down. Take T should end with a deal both listeners write down the same way.

## The story

Your friend is buying a second-hand scooter from a man two streets away. The price is forty-five
thousand. She's nervous and wants him to like her, so she keeps offering things:

> I can pay cash, no problem. And I can come and take it today. And I don't need the helmet, it's OK.

He smiles. *Good. Forty-five, then.*

Every one of those things was worth something to him. Cash, today, and keeping his helmet. She gave all
three away, and the price never moved.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **if-trade**, **say-back**: from [part 1.1](1.1-give-to-get-the-if-trade.md).
- **deal card**: from [part 1.2](1.2-one-real-deal-two-rounds.md).
- **concession**: something you give up in a deal.
- **free concession**: something you give up and get nothing back for.
- **write-the-deal test**: a listener hears a recording and writes down the final deal: the number,
  what's included, and the date.

| Take | What's in it | What a listener should write |
| --- | --- | --- |
| G, giving (deliberate) | two or three free concessions, no *if*, no say-back | a worse deal, or a deal they can't write clearly |
| T, trading | every concession inside an if-trade, with a say-back | the same clear deal, both listeners |

## Why Vaani needs it

A free concession sounds kind, so it's hard to hear as a mistake. It is one, though. Real deals on day
94 with a landlord or an office, and the salary question an interview may bring, go wrong this way.
The write-the-deal test turns it into something you can hear.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take T that still gives one thing away**. It often
comes at the very end: *Oh, and I can collect it myself*. Listen for any *I can …* that has no *if*
before it.

The second is **a say-back with a question tune**: *So, eleven thousand?* The other person hears a
new offer, not a summary, and may answer *Eleven and a half*. Let the tune fall.

The third, in real life, is **feeling rude when you trade**. An if-trade isn't rude. It tells the other
person you value what they're giving, too.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A free concession feels like being nice. A traded one is being nice and being clear. Most sellers
respect the second more.

Under pressure, a free concession often comes out to fill a silence. If you notice one leaving your
mouth, you can still attach a trade: *I can collect it today. If I do, could you include the helmet?*

A listener who's watching you, not the numbers, remembers the say-back more than anything else. It's
the sentence that becomes the deal.
