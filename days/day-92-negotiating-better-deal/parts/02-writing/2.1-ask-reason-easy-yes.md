## One-line answer

A request email that gets a yes puts the **ask** in the subject line and the first paragraph, gives
the **reason** in a few plain sentences, and then makes **yes easy**: it says exactly what would
happen, from when, and what you'll do so the reader loses nothing. It ends with one polite line and
the right sign-off, and it has no contractions.

## The story

Your father has hospital appointments every Tuesday and Thursday morning for two months. You need to
start work at eleven on those days, not nine. You write to your manager:

> Subject: Hello
>
> Dear Ms Rao, I hope you are well. As you know, my father has not been well for some time. The
> doctors have tried several things. Last month they suggested a new treatment, and it has started.
> It is at the city hospital, which is far from our house ...

Your manager reads four lines on her phone, sees nothing she has to do, and closes it. Two days later
you get a reply: *Sorry to hear about your father. Let me know if you need anything.*

You did need something. It was in the fifth paragraph.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **subject line**: the short line at the top of an email saying what it's about, from
  [day 29](../../../day-29-weather-and-small-talk/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-five-parts-of-a-friendly-email.md).
- **frame (of an email)**: the formal greeting, the reason line, the paragraphs, the closing line and
  the matching sign-off, from
  [day 82](../../../day-82-introducing-yourself-at-work/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-formal-frame-top-to-bottom.md).
- **contraction**: a short form such as *I'll*, *can't*, *that's*, from
  [day 25](../../../day-25-on-the-phone/parts/02-writing/2.1-a-message-is-not-a-letter.md).
- **request email**: an email whose job is to get someone to say yes to one thing.
- **the ask**: the one thing you want the reader to agree to.
- **easy yes**: the details that let a reader agree without having to work anything out or lose
  anything.

A reader who's busy decides from the subject line and the first paragraph whether an email needs
anything from them. If the ask isn't there, they may never reach it.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate asks for a formal email with the correct greeting, sign-off and no contractions.
Most real formal emails you'll send are asking for something. On day 93 you'll write the other side,
the email that says *no*, and on day 94 the free-text boxes on a form often need a request in one
paragraph.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[An email request](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/c1-writing/email-request)
says: "Don't go into too much personal detail when explaining the problem, as this is a formal
situation with a person you don't know well." And: "To make polite requests use the phrase *I would be
grateful if you could …*". Its model opens with "I am writing to request your help following a change
in my circumstances."

**The shape on the page.**

```text
 Subject: Request: 11 a.m. start on Tuesdays and Thursdays, 3 March to 30 April   ← the ask

 Dear Ms Rao,

 ¶1  THE ASK       I am writing to ask whether I could start work at 11 a.m. on
                   Tuesdays and Thursdays from 3 March to 30 April.

 ¶2  THE REASON    My father has hospital treatment on those mornings, and I
                   need to take him. (two or three sentences, no more)

 ¶3  EASY YES      I would make up the hours by working until 7 p.m. on those
                   days. Anil has agreed to cover the 9 a.m. calls.

 ¶4  CLOSE         I would be grateful if you could let me know by Friday.
                   Thank you for considering this.

 Yours sincerely,
 <your name>
```

**¶1, the ask.** One sentence. Use *I am writing to ask whether I could …* or *I am writing to request
…*. Put the dates and numbers in it.

**¶2, the reason.** Enough to make the ask fair, and no more. The reader doesn't need the whole story.

**¶3, easy yes.** Answer the reader's first worry before they have it: *Who does the work? What does it
cost? When does it end?* This is the paragraph that turns *maybe* into *yes*.

**¶4, the close.** *I would be grateful if you could …* plus a date for the answer. Then *Thank you for
considering this.*

**No contractions.** Write *I would*, *I am*, *do not*. That's this course's choice for formal emails,
and the phase 5 gate checks it.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the story first**: three paragraphs of background and the ask at the
bottom, as in the story. The reader stops before they get there.

The second is **the ask with no details**:

> Please can I come late some days.

Which days? How late? Until when? The reader has to write back and ask, and many won't.

The third is **the subject line that says nothing**: *Hello*, *Request*, *Urgent*. The subject line
should work as the ask on its own.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often write ¶3 first in their head. They ask themselves *What will make this hard to
say yes to?* and fix that before they send.

Under pressure, when the request is emotional, the reason grows and the ask shrinks. Write the ask
first, then add only the reason it needs.

A manager or an office reads many requests. The ones that get a quick yes are the ones where the yes
is already worked out.
