## One-line answer

Write one real request email in four paragraphs: the ask, the reason, the easy yes and the close.
First list the reader's worries and answer each in ¶3. Then give it to a reader who plays the person
you're writing to, and ask them for two things only: *What am I being asked? What would stop you saying
yes?* Fix what they find, and send it if the request is real and live.

## The story

Your friend works at a small office. She wants Fridays from home for the next three months, because
her mother moved in after an operation. She wrote the email twice.

The first version asked and gave the reason. Her manager wrote back: *Who will open the office on
Fridays?* Then: *What about the Friday team call?* Then: *Can you still come in if a client visits?*
Three emails, and a week went by.

The second time, she sat down first and wrote the three worries in her notebook. She answered each one
in the email before she sent it. The reply came the same day. It said *Yes, that's fine.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **request email**, **the ask**, **easy yes**: from [part 2.1](2.1-ask-reason-easy-yes.md).
- **worry list**: the questions your reader will have before they can say yes.
- **ask-and-block test**: a reader answers two questions: *What am I being asked?* and *What would stop
  me saying yes?*

The worry list is how you write ¶3. You're guessing what the reader will think, and answering it
before they have to ask.

## Why Vaani needs it

Common worries are: who does it instead, what it costs, how long it lasts, what happens if it goes
wrong, and whether everyone else will want the same.

**Step two: write the email.** Use the shape from [part 2.1](2.1-ask-reason-easy-yes.md):

- a subject line that states the ask with dates or numbers
- *Dear* + name, or *Dear Sir or Madam*, with a comma
- ¶1 the ask, ¶2 the reason, ¶3 the answers to the worry list, ¶4 the close with *I would be grateful
  if you could …* and a date
- *Yours sincerely,* with a name, *Yours faithfully,* after *Dear Sir or Madam*
- no contractions

**Step three: the ask-and-block test.** Give the email to one reader. Tell them who they are playing.
Ask them to read it once and answer:

| What am I being asked? | `TODO(me)` |
| What would stop me saying yes? | `TODO(me)` |

**Step four: fix and send.** If their ask is wrong or missing detail, fix ¶1. If they name a block,
answer it in ¶3. If the request is real and live, send it, and write down what came back.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a worry list that's really about you**: *I'm worried he'll say no.*
That's not the reader's worry. Ask *What does he lose if he says yes?*

The second is **¶3 that promises too much**, like *I will work every weekend*. Don't offer what you
can't keep. A promise broken later costs more than a *no* now.

The third is **a reader who's too polite** and says *It's perfect*. Ask them again: *If you had to say
no, what would your reason be?*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often send a request the same day they think of it, but they read it once as the
reader first. That one read is the ask-and-block test done in their head.

Under pressure, the easy-yes paragraph is the one that gets dropped, because it takes thought. It's
also the paragraph that decides the answer.

A formal reader doesn't mind a long email if the ask is in the first line. They mind a short one that
makes them write back with questions.
