## One-line answer

Make a copy of your request with the ask moved to the end of ¶3 on purpose, a subject line that says
*Request*, and ¶1 turned into background. Show two readers only the subject line and ¶1, one reader for
each copy. Ask each: *What is this person asking you for?* The buried copy's reader can't say. Your real
email's reader should say the ask, with its dates, straight away.

## The story

Your uncle is a school principal. He tells you how he reads parents' emails on a busy morning:

> Subject line, first paragraph. If I can see what they want, I deal with it now. If the first
> paragraph is the story of the child's summer, I mark it *later*. Later is sometimes next week.

He shows you one he marked *later*. The ask, *Could my daughter sit the test on Monday instead?*, was
the last line of the third paragraph. The test had been on Friday. By the time he read it, it was too
late to say yes.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the ask**, **easy yes**: from [part 2.1](2.1-ask-reason-easy-yes.md).
- **ask-and-block test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-request.md).
- **buried**: put deep inside a text, where a reader may not reach it.
- **first-look test**: a reader sees only the subject line and ¶1, and says what the writer is asking
  for.

| Copy | What's in it | What a reader should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B, buried (deliberate) | subject *Request*; ¶1 background; the ask as the last line of ¶3 | nothing, or a guess |
| R, real | your email from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-request.md) | the ask, with its dates or numbers |

## Why Vaani needs it

A buried ask looks fine when you read your own email, because you know what you meant. Only a reader
who doesn't know can show you. The first-look test lets you see what a busy formal reader sees, before
the phase 5 gate email has to work on its own.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[An email request](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/skills/writing/c1-writing/email-request)
puts the reason for writing first in its list of parts, and its model opens with the reason for
writing: "I am writing to request your help following a change in my circumstances."

**Step one: make copy B.** Copy your real email and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Change the subject line to *Request*.
2. Replace ¶1 with two sentences of background.
3. Move the ask to the last line of ¶3.

**Step two: the first-look test.** Cover everything below ¶1. Show B to one reader and R to a different
reader. Ask: *What is this person asking you for?*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who reads past ¶1**. Cover the rest of the
page with your hand or a sheet of paper. The test is only fair if they see what a busy reader sees.

The second is **an R whose ¶1 is polite but empty**: *I hope this email finds you well. I am writing
about my schedule.* That names a topic, not an ask. The ask is a sentence the reader can answer with
*yes*.

The third, in real life, is **a second email that buries the ask again**, after the first got no
answer. Put the ask in the first line: *I am writing again to ask whether …*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Formal readers read from the top, and many stop early. A request that puts the story first works like
a delivery with the address on the back of the box.

Under pressure, when you feel you must explain before you ask, write the explanation after the ask.
The reader will still read it, and now they know why they're reading.

Look at your own inbox. You see who it's from and the subject line before anything else, and you
decide from that what to open first. Your reader does the same.
