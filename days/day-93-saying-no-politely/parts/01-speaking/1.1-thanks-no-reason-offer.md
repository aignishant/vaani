## One-line answer

A polite *no* has four short moves: **thanks** for asking, a clear **no** (*I'm afraid I can't*), one
short **reason**, and, if you want to, an **offer** of something smaller. The *no* is said plainly,
with the tune falling. Kind words go around it, not in place of it.

## The story

A colleague stops you on Thursday evening:

> Can you do my Saturday shift? My sister's getting engaged.

You've promised your mother you'll take her to the doctor on Saturday. But you don't want to seem
unkind, so you say:

> Oh … Saturday … hmm, I'll see, maybe, let me check …

She hears *maybe*, which to her sounds like *probably yes*. She tells the manager it's sorted. On
Friday night she messages *Thanks so much for tomorrow!* Now you have to say no anyway, later, and it's
much worse.

What you could have said on Thursday:

> Congratulations to your sister! I'm afraid I can't this Saturday. I'm taking my mum to the doctor.
> I could do your Wednesday next week, if that helps?

That's four sentences. She's disappointed for a moment, and then she asks someone else.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **I'm afraid**: a soft opening for bad news, from
  [day 86](../../../day-86-making-complaint/parts/01-speaking/1.1-problem-proof-request.md). It isn't
  fear.
- **intonation**: the tune of a sentence, where the voice goes up and where it goes down, from
  [day 24](../../../day-24-voice-up-and-down/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-voice-goes-down-and-up.md).
- **decline**: to say no to an offer, an invitation or a request, politely.
- **the no line**: the sentence that carries the *no* itself: *I'm afraid I can't.*
- **offer (after a no)**: a smaller thing you can say yes to, such as another day or part of the job.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Thanks | tells them the asking was fine | *Thanks for asking.* / *That's kind of you.* |
| 2. No | the clear answer | *I'm afraid I can't.* / *I'm afraid I won't be able to.* |
| 3. Reason | one short, true reason | *I've got something on that day.* |
| 4. Offer (if you mean it) | keeps the door open | *I could do Wednesday instead, if that helps?* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[I'm afraid](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/I%27m_afraid) gives the phrase as "Used to politely
introduce a disappointment or disagreement."
[How to turn down an invitation](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/sites/podcasts/files/LearnEnglish-Howto-turn-down-an-invitation.pdf)
is described as how to "say 'no' to a formal invitation", and its model line is "I'm unable to attend
because I have a prior engagement."

**The no line is short and plain.** Pick one and keep it:

> I'm afraid I can't.
> I'm afraid I won't be able to.
> Sorry, I can't make it this time.

**The tune falls on the *no*.** Let *can't* be loud and clear, and let your voice come down at the end.
If it rises, *I can't?*, it sounds like a question, and the other person may try again.

**One reason, not five.** A short, true reason is kind. A long one sounds like an excuse, and it gives
the other person something to argue with: *Oh, the doctor? Can't your brother take her?*

**The offer is optional.** Only offer what you'll really do. An offer you don't mean is a *maybe* in
disguise. If you don't want to offer anything, end on the reason, or on *Hope it goes well*.

**Name the trap.** In many families and workplaces, saying *no* straight out feels rude, so people say
*I'll try* or *let me see*. In English, those often mean *yes, probably* to the listener. Being clear
is the polite thing to do here, because it lets them ask someone else in time.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the maybe that means no**:

> Hmm, I'll see, maybe, I'll try …

The listener hears a probable yes and plans around it.

The second is **the no with no thanks**: *No. I'm busy.* It's clear, but it sounds cold, and the
listener remembers the coldness.

The third is **the reason that grows**: three sentences about your mother, the doctor, the bus, the
last appointment. The *no* gets lost in it, and the listener starts to solve your problem for you.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often say the *no* in the first two sentences, even when they're sorry. The kindness
is in the thanks and the tone, not in delaying.

Under pressure, when the person asking is senior or family, the *no* shrinks. Decide before you speak:
is it yes, or no? Then say that word first in your head.

Most people who ask a favour have a second person in mind. A quick, clear *no* lets them go to that
person. A slow *maybe* costs them the time to do it.
