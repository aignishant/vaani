## One-line answer

Take your medium-size *no* from part 1.2 and record it three ways: M, a soft *maybe* on purpose; C, a
cold bare *no* on purpose; and P, the four moves. Play each to a different listener. Ask two questions:
*Was that yes, no or maybe?* and *How do you feel about the speaker, from one to five?* M should come
back as *maybe*, and C as low. P should come back as a clear *no* that still scores warm.

## The story

A small clinic's receptionist told your cousin a story about her first month.

A patient asked whether he could come at seven, after closing. She didn't want to say no, so she said
*Hmm, I'll try, maybe*. He came at seven. The doctor had gone. He shouted at her in front of the whole waiting room.

The next week, another patient asked the same thing. This time she said *No*, and nothing else. He
complained to the doctor that she was rude.

The third time, she said:

> Thanks for asking. I'm afraid we close at six. I could book you at half past five on Tuesday?

He took Tuesday.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **the no line**: from [part 1.1](1.1-thanks-no-reason-offer.md).
- **push**: from [part 1.2](1.2-three-real-nos.md).
- **clear-and-warm test**: a listener says whether they heard *yes*, *no* or *maybe*, and scores how
  they feel about the speaker from one (cold) to five (warm).

| Take | What's in it | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| M, maybe (deliberate) | *hmm*, *I'll see*, *maybe*, *I'll try*; no no line | *maybe* or *yes* |
| C, cold (deliberate) | *No.* and nothing else | *no*, but warmth 1 or 2 |
| P, polite | thanks, the no line, one reason, an offer if you mean it | *no*, warmth 4 or 5 |

## Why Vaani needs it

A *no* that sounds like *maybe* feels polite to the speaker and gets heard as a promise. You can't hear
that from inside your own mouth. The clear-and-warm test lets someone else hear it for you.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a P with *I'll try* still in it**. *I'm afraid I can't,
but I'll try* is a *maybe*. Pick one.

The second is **a listener who knows you well** and hears what you mean, not what you say. Use
listeners who don't know the answer you intended.

The third, in real life, is **the late no**: *maybe* now, *no* the night before. The receptionist's
first patient got that, and it was the worst of all three.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A native listener takes *I'll try* as a soft *yes* and *I'm afraid I can't* as a firm *no*. That
difference decides whether they make other plans.

Under pressure, people drift into M because it feels safe. It only feels safe until the day comes.

A cold *no* is remembered for a long time. A warm one is forgotten quickly, because nothing went wrong.
