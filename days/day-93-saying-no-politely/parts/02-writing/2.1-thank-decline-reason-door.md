## One-line answer

A written *no* has four short paragraphs: **thank** them for the offer or invitation, **decline** in one
clear sentence in the first paragraph (*I have decided not to accept*, *I am unable to attend*), give
one short **reason** or none, and leave the **door** open if you want to. The decline sentence must be
impossible to miss, and the letter has no contractions.

## The story

Your friend had two job offers in the same week. She took the second one and wrote to the first
company:

> Dear Mr Sharma,
>
> Thank you so much for the offer. I really enjoyed meeting the team, and the office is lovely. The
> role sounds very interesting, and I learned a lot in the interview. I am very grateful for your
> time. I hope we can stay in touch in future.
>
> Best regards,
> Meena

Mr Sharma read it twice and replied: *Wonderful! We will send the contract on Monday.*

There was no *no* anywhere in the email. Every sentence was kind, and none of them said it.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **frame (of an email)**: the formal greeting, the reason line, the paragraphs, the closing line and
  the matching sign-off, from
  [day 82](../../../day-82-introducing-yourself-at-work/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-formal-frame-top-to-bottom.md).
- **contraction**: a short form such as *I'll*, *can't*, *that's*, from
  [day 25](../../../day-25-on-the-phone/parts/02-writing/2.1-a-message-is-not-a-letter.md).
- **decline**: to say no to an offer, an invitation or a request, politely.
- **the decline sentence**: the one sentence that says *no* in words a reader cannot misread.
- **leave the door open**: say you'd be glad to hear from them again, if that's true.
- **prior engagement**: a plan you made before the invitation came, a formal and common reason.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate asks for a formal email with the correct greeting and sign-off and no contractions.
Declining is one of the formal emails you'll really send: a job, an invitation, a meeting, a
request. On day 95 you'll write a reply to a complaint, and part of it may be a polite *no*.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[How to turn down an invitation](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/sites/podcasts/files/LearnEnglish-Howto-turn-down-an-invitation.pdf)
gives the formal decline "I'm unable to attend because I have a prior engagement." Written formally, as
this course writes formal pages, that's *I am unable to attend*.
[Politely declining a job offer: What to say and how to say it](https://www.uc.edu/blog/decline-job-offer.html)
gives, as a sample, "After careful consideration, I have decided to pursue another opportunity that
aligns more closely with my career goals." It also says "You don't owe an exhaustive explanation, and
it's better to keep your response professional and succinct."

**The shape on the page.**

```text
 Subject: Job offer — Office Assistant — decision

 Dear Mr Sharma,

 ¶1  THANK + DECLINE   Thank you for offering me the position of Office Assistant.
                       After careful thought, I have decided not to accept it.

 ¶2  REASON (short)    I have accepted another role that is closer to my home.

 ¶3  DOOR (optional)   I enjoyed meeting the team, and I hope we may work
                       together in the future.

 ¶4  CLOSE             Thank you again for your time.

 Yours sincerely,
 Meena Kulkarni
```

**The decline sentence goes in ¶1**, straight after the thanks. It uses a verb that can only mean no:

> I have decided not to accept the offer.
> I am unable to attend on 12 March.
> I am afraid I am not able to take on this work.

**The reason is short or absent.** One sentence at most. You don't have to give one, and a long one
reads as an excuse.

**The door is optional.** Only write it if you mean it.

**No contractions.** Write *I am*, *I have*, *do not*. The subject line can name the decision, but it
doesn't have to.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **all kindness, no decline**, as in the story. Every sentence is warm,
and the reader fills the gap with *yes*.

The second is **the decline hidden after *but***:

> The role sounds wonderful and I would love to be part of the team, but unfortunately at this time
> it might perhaps not be possible for me.

*Might perhaps not be possible* can be read as *ask me again*.

The third is **the angry reason**: *The salary you offered was too low.* It may be true, but it closes
a door you might want later. *I have accepted another offer* is enough.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers put the *no* early because they know the reader is waiting for one word, *yes* or *no*,
and reads everything else only after they've found it.

Under pressure, when you feel guilty, the decline slides down the page. Write it second, right after
*thank you*, and don't move it.

A formal reader isn't hurt by a clear *no*. They're annoyed by one they had to work out, or one they
found out about too late.
