## One-line answer

Make a copy of your decline with the decline sentence taken out on purpose and warm words left in its
place, and a softened *no* moved to the end. Give that copy and your real decline to two readers who
play the person you're writing to. Ask each one: *Is this a yes, a no, or a maybe? Underline the words
that told you.* The copy should read as *yes* or *maybe*. Your real decline should read as *no*, with
the decline sentence underlined.

## The story

Your cousin works in the office of a wedding hall. She keeps a list of who is coming to each function.
She shows you an email she got:

> Thank you so much for the kind invitation. It is such a special occasion, and we are so happy for
> the family. We will certainly be thinking of you all on the day. With warm wishes ...

> I put them down as coming, she says. Two plates, two chairs. They were telling me they weren't
> coming. *Thinking of you on the day* means they'll be somewhere else. I only understood when
> they didn't arrive.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **decline**, **the decline sentence**: from [part 2.1](2.1-thank-decline-reason-door.md).
- **softener**, **no-sentence check**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-decline.md).
- **yes-no-maybe test**: a reader says whether a message is a yes, a no or a maybe, and underlines the
  words that told them.

| Copy | What's in it | What a reader should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H, hidden (deliberate) | no decline sentence; warm words in its place; a softened hint at the end | *yes* or *maybe*; underlines warm words |
| R, real | your decline from [part 2.2](2.2-write-a-real-decline.md) | *no*; underlines the decline sentence |

## Why Vaani needs it

A hidden *no* reads as kind to the writer and as *yes* to the reader. Only a reader who doesn't know
what you meant can show you which one it is. That matters for every formal email you send after this
phase, and for the gate email too.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Politely declining a job offer: What to say and how to say it](https://www.uc.edu/blog/decline-job-offer.html)
puts a clear decision straight after the thanks: "After careful consideration, I have decided to
pursue another opportunity".

**Step one: make copy H.** Copy your real decline and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Delete the decline sentence.
2. Put two warm sentences in its place: *It sounds wonderful. We are so grateful.*
3. Add a softened hint at the end: *It may be difficult for us this time.*

**Step two: the yes-no-maybe test.** Give H to one reader and R to another. Tell them who they are
playing. Each reads once, answers, and underlines.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an H that still says no somewhere**, often in the
subject line: *Re: Invitation — regrets*. Take it out of the subject too, or the test isn't fair.

The second is **a reader who reads your face**. Hand over the page and look away, or send it to them.

The third, in real life, is **the reply that shows they misread**: *Wonderful, see you there!* When that
comes back, write again straight away, with the decline sentence first: *I am sorry, I think my email
was not clear. I am unable to attend.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Warm words without a decline sentence are a polite way of saying *no* in some families and places. In
a formal email in English, many readers will simply take them as *yes*.

Under pressure, people delete the decline sentence at the last moment because it looks harsh on the
screen. Read it aloud once. It usually sounds kinder than it looks.

A formal reader sorts replies into *yes* and *no* piles. Make sure yours lands in the right one on the
first read.
