## One-line answer

Take one of your three conversations and record it twice. In take L, leave on purpose after *It will
be done*: no next-step questions and no say-back. In take N, lock the next step and say it back. Play
each to a different listener and ask four questions: *What happens next? By when? Who, or what number?
If it doesn't happen, what would you say when you call?* L's listener can't answer. N's listener should
answer all four.

## The story

Your neighbour went to the municipal office about a broken streetlight outside her house. The man at
the desk said *Yes, yes, we'll send someone*. She thanked him and went home.

Two weeks later, nothing had happened. She phoned. *Which complaint? When did you come? Who did you
speak to?* She didn't know. She had to start again, from the beginning.

Her husband went the next time. At the end, he asked *What's the complaint number, and when will
someone come?* He wrote them on his hand. When nobody came, one call with that number was enough.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **lock the next step**, **reference number**: from [part 1.1](1.1-who-what-need-next.md).
- **office card**: from [part 1.2](1.2-three-offices-one-shape.md).
- **chase**: phoning or writing again when something you were promised hasn't happened.
- **four-question test**: a listener answers *What happens next? By when? Who or what number? What
  would you say when you chase?*

| Take | How it ends | What a listener should be able to answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L, leaving (deliberate) | *OK, thank you*, after *It will be done* | little or nothing |
| N, next step | three next-step questions and a say-back | all four questions |

## Why Vaani needs it

A conversation that ends without a next step sounds fine. Nobody made a mistake you can hear. The cost
only shows weeks later. This test brings that cost forward, so you can hear it today.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take N where the friend gives a vague date and you
accept it**. *Next week* is not a date. Ask *Which day next week?*

The second is **a say-back that's too fast**. The number comes out as one sound. Slow down on the
number. That's the part the listener must write.

The third, in real life, is **the chase that starts with the story**. When you chase, start with the
reference: *I'm calling about complaint 55120, from the fourth of March.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Most offices can only find you with a number. A conversation with no number in it can't be traced.

Under pressure, the next step feels like a small thing to ask. It's the thing that saves you the
second visit.

A clerk who is asked *When should I hear from you?* often gives a more careful answer than one who
isn't asked. The question itself makes the promise more real.
