## One-line answer

Before you write anything on a form, read it all the way to the signature. Then follow its
instructions exactly: **block capitals** where it asks for them, **dates** in the order its boxes
show, **N/A** in a box that doesn't apply to you instead of leaving it empty, a **tick or cross** as it
says, full sentences in the **free-text box**, and your **signature** only after you've read the
declaration above it.

## The story

Your cousin applied for an evening course at the government college. The form was two pages. She
filled it quickly at the counter, in her normal handwriting, and handed it in.

A week later it came back by post with a note: *Incomplete. Please resubmit.* Four things were
circled. Her surname was in small joined-up letters in a box that said BLOCK CAPITALS. Her date of
birth was written 07/12 in boxes marked DD/MM/YYYY, so nobody could tell the year, or even be sure
whether 07 was the day. The box *Previous qualifications* was empty, because she had none to list.
And the box *Why do you want to join this course?* said only *For job*.

The course had filled by the time she sent it again.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **address lines** and **the date on paper**: from
  [day 2](../../../day-02-long-vowels/parts/02-writing/2.1-your-address-line-by-line.md) and
  [day 10](../../../day-10-numbers-times-dates/parts/02-writing/2.1-writing-the-date.md).
- **form**: a printed or online page with boxes for you to fill in.
- **application**: a form, or a letter, asking for something: a place, a job, a licence, a service.
- **block capitals**: every letter written as a separate capital: RAO, not Rao.
- **N/A**: short for *not applicable*, written in a box that doesn't apply to you.
- **free-text box**: a larger box where you write sentences, not one word or number.
- **declaration**: the sentences above the signature that say what you are promising is true.
- **signature**: your name written in your own usual way, to show you agree.

## Why Vaani needs it

Every office conversation you have ends in a form. The landlord's agreement, the bank's address change
and the college's application all have boxes. On day 97 you'll write instructions for other people to
follow, and a form is instructions that someone else wrote for you.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[block capitals](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/block_capitals) gives "Text written entirely in capital
letters" and the example "Complete the form in block capitals."
[n/a](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/n/a) gives "not applicable" as its first meaning.
[A to Z style guide](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/),
a government writing guide, says: "DO NOT USE BLOCK CAPITALS FOR LARGE AMOUNTS OF TEXT AS IT'S QUITE
HARD TO READ."

**First, read the whole form.** To the last line. Look for the instruction at the top: *Use black ink*,
*Use BLOCK CAPITALS*, *Tick one box*.

**Name and address boxes.** If it says block capitals, every letter is a separate capital, one per box
if there are boxes. It's there because joined-up handwriting is hard to read.

```text
 SURNAME        | R | A | O |   |   |   |   |      ← one capital per box
 FIRST NAME     | A | N | J | A | L | I |   |
 DATE OF BIRTH  | 0 | 7 | / | 1 | 2 | / | 1 | 9 | 9 | 8 |     DD / MM / YYYY
 PREVIOUS QUALIFICATIONS   N/A                        ← not empty
```

**Dates.** Follow the letters printed on the boxes. DD is the day, MM the month and YYYY the four-digit
year. If the form says MM/DD, the month comes first. Never guess the order.

**Boxes that don't apply.** Write N/A. An empty box looks like you missed it. N/A shows you read it.

**Ticks and crosses.** Use whatever mark the form asks for, and put it inside the box.

**The free-text box.** Write full sentences, in ordinary capitals and small letters. Block capitals are
for names, not for long answers. Answer the exact question, and give one or two real facts.

**The declaration and the signature.** Read the declaration before you sign. Sign in your usual way.
If there's a separate box for your name, print it there in capitals.

**Name the trap.** Not every form uses the same order.
[List of date formats by country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_date_formats_by_country),
opened on 2026-09-24, says: "In India, the dd-mm-yyyy is the predominant short form of the numeric date
usage." It lists the United States under month-day-year, and warns that "the abbreviated date
'01/11/06' can be interpreted as '1 November 2006' for DMY, 'January 11, 2006' for MDY". So *07/12* is
the seventh of December on one form and the twelfth of July on another. Read the letters on the boxes.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **filling before reading**. You write your name in small letters, then
see *BLOCK CAPITALS* at the top.

The second is **the empty box**. The clerk can't tell whether it doesn't apply or you forgot, so the
form comes back.

The third is **the one-word free-text answer**: *For job.* The question asked *why*, and a reader
deciding between applicants learns nothing from it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent adults often ask for two copies of a paper form, and fill the first one in pencil or as a
practice. It's normal, and offices usually don't mind.

Under pressure, at a counter with a queue, you'll want to fill it fast. Read the top and the
declaration first. Those two take the least time and cause the most returns.

A clerk checks forms quickly, one box at a time. A complete, clear form is processed. A form with a
question in it waits, sometimes at the bottom of a pile.
