## One-line answer

Get one real form, free: a paper form from a bank, a clinic, a college or an office, or one you print
or copy by hand from an official page. Read it all first and list its instructions. Then fill it in,
including one free-text box in full sentences. Finally, give it to a reader who plays the clerk and
checks it box by box, and fix anything they stop at.

## The story

Your aunt needed to fill in a form for a senior citizen's bus pass. She asked for two copies at the
counter. At home, she read the whole thing and wrote the instructions on a scrap of paper: *black ink,
capitals for name, photo glued not stapled, proof of age attached, sign in two places*.

She filled the first copy slowly. Then she asked her grandson to check it *like the man at the counter
would*. He found one thing, a second signature box on the back she'd missed.

She copied everything onto the clean form. It was accepted the first time.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **form**, **block capitals**, **N/A**, **free-text box**, **declaration**, **signature**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-read-it-all-then-fill.md).
- **instruction list**: every rule the form gives, written out before you start.
- **attachment**: a paper or file sent with the form, such as a photo or a copy of an ID card.
- **clerk check**: a reader plays the clerk and checks each box, stopping at anything they'd send back.

## Why Vaani needs it

A form is formal writing where every box is checked. It's the smallest piece of formal writing there
is, and the one most often sent back. Getting one right the first time is what this phase is for.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **an instruction list that stops at page one**. The second signature,
the attachment, the photo size: they're usually on the last page.

The second is **the free-text box copied from somebody else**. A clerk or a college reads many of the
same sentences. Your two real facts are what make it yours.

The third is **a reader who's too quick**. Ask them to put a finger on each box as they check it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent adults keep a photo of every form they hand in. If it's lost, they can fill it again quickly,
and they can prove what they wrote.

Under pressure, the attachments are what get forgotten. Tick them off your instruction list as you
put each one in the envelope.

An online form may stop you at an empty box. A paper form never does. It lets you make the mistake,
and then it comes back to you.
