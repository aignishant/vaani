## One-line answer

When a customer is angry, **let them finish** without cutting in. Then **say their problem back** in
one sentence, with their facts in it, and say you understand why they're upset. Say **sorry for the
specific thing**, not for everything. Then say **what you'll do and by when**, and check it works for
them. Keep your voice lower and slower than theirs.

## The story

Your cousin works at the counter of a small phone repair shop. A woman comes in, red in the face:

> You said it would be ready today! I took a half-day off work for this! And now you're telling me
> Thursday?

Your cousin starts talking while she's still going:

> Madam, madam, calm down, it's not my fault, the part didn't come, it's the supplier …

She gets louder. Other customers stare.

The owner comes over and waits until she stops. Then he says, quietly:

> So you were told it would be ready today, and you took time off work to collect it, and it isn't
> ready. I understand why you're upset. I'm sorry we didn't call you. The part arrives tomorrow
> morning. I'll fix it first thing and bring it to your office by one o'clock. Would that work?

She breathes out. *Yes. OK. Thank you.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **complaint**: telling someone that something they sold or did for you has gone wrong, and asking
  them to fix it, from
  [day 86](../../../day-86-making-complaint/parts/01-speaking/1.1-problem-proof-request.md). Today
  you're on the other side of the counter.
- **volume**, **pace**: how loud you speak and how fast, from
  [day 85](../../../day-85-sounding-confident/parts/01-speaking/1.1-three-dials.md).
- **acknowledge**: say clearly that you've heard the problem and you understand it matters.
- **say-it-back**: repeating the customer's problem in one sentence, with their facts in it.
- **specific sorry**: an apology for the exact thing that went wrong: *I'm sorry we didn't call you.*
- **the promise**: what you will do and by when.

| Move | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Let them finish | shows you're listening | silence, a nod, *mm* |
| 2. Say it back | shows you heard their facts | *So you were told it would be ready today, and it isn't.* |
| 3. Acknowledge | shows the feeling is fair | *I understand why you're upset.* |
| 4. Specific sorry | owns one real thing | *I'm sorry we didn't call you.* |
| 5. The promise + check | a way forward | *I'll … by … Would that work for you?* |

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate is an interview role-play, and a common interview question asks about a difficult
customer or a difficult situation. You'll answer it better if you've actually done it. And at any job
with customers, this conversation comes in the first week.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[A response to a complaint](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/c1/response-complaint)
is about written replies, but three of its tips hold for speaking too: "Acknowledge the complaint and
show you understand the problem.", "Choose vocabulary that is more objective and reduces negative
emotions where possible", and "Offer some kind of solution or compensation if appropriate."

**Move 1: let them finish.** An angry person has a speech ready. If you cut in, they start it again,
louder. Wait until they stop.

**Move 2: say it back.** Use their facts, not your excuses. Start with *So*:

> So you were told Tuesday, and it didn't arrive.
> So the bill says two thousand, and you were quoted fifteen hundred.

**Move 3: acknowledge.** One line: *I understand why you're upset.* / *I can see why that's frustrating.*

**Move 4: a specific sorry.** Say sorry for the one thing that really went wrong, even if it wasn't you
personally: *I'm sorry we didn't call.* A general *sorry for everything* sounds empty.

**Move 5: the promise, then check.** What you'll do, and when. Only promise what you can do. Then ask:

> Would that work for you?

**Your voice.** Lower and slower than theirs. Not quiet. Just steady. If they speed up, you slow down.

**Name the trap.** Three things feel natural and make it worse: *Calm down*, *It's not my fault*, and
*It's company policy*. Each one tells the customer their anger isn't fair.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the excuse first**:

> The supplier didn't send the part, so …

It's true, but the customer hears *it's not our problem*.

The second is **cutting in**. You speak while they're still speaking, and they go back to the
beginning.

The third is **the promise you can't keep**: *It'll be ready in an hour*, when you don't know. A second
broken promise is worse than the first.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent staff often say the customer's name in the say-back, if they know it. It turns *a customer*
back into a person.

Under pressure, when the customer is shouting, your voice will want to rise to match theirs. Slow the
first word down on purpose. The rest follows it.

An angry customer usually wants two things: to be heard, and a way forward. Moves 2 and 3 are the
first. Move 5 is the second. Most of the anger goes when they get both.
