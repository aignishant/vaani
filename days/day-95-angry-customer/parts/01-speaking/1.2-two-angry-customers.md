## One-line answer

Ask a friend to play two angry customers with two real problems. The first one has a fix you can
offer. The second one wants something you can't give. Record both. For the first, use the five moves
and make a promise. For the second, use the five moves and a clear *no* to what they asked for, with
something you *can* do instead.

## The story

Your friend works at the front desk of a small hotel. In one evening she had two angry guests.

The first said his room was cold. She said it back, apologised for the heater, and moved him to another
room. He was happy in five sentences.

The second wanted his money back for the whole stay, because the lift had been broken for a day. She
couldn't give that. She said it back, said she understood, and said sorry for the lift. Then:

> I'm afraid I can't refund the whole stay. What I can do is take tonight off your bill, and move
> you to a room on the first floor.

He wasn't happy, but he stopped shouting. He took the first-floor room.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **say-it-back**, **acknowledge**, **specific sorry**, **the promise**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-let-them-finish-say-it-back-say-what-next.md).
- **role-play**: acting out a real conversation, with someone else playing the other person, from
  [day 86](../../../day-86-making-complaint/parts/01-speaking/1.2-hold-the-request.md).
- **the can't**: the thing a customer asks for that you can't give.
- **the can**: the thing you can give instead.

Some angry customers ask for something you can give. Then it's easy. Others ask for more than you can
give. Then the five moves still work, but move 5 has two halves: *I'm afraid I can't …* and *What I can
do is …*.

## Why Vaani needs it

An interview may ask *Tell me about a time you dealt with a difficult customer*. A recorded role-play
with a *can't* in it gives you a true story for that answer. It also rehearses a firm, polite *no* under
pressure.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **giving in to the *can't***. You say *OK, full refund* because they're
shouting. Then the manager says no, and the customer is angrier than before.

The second is **the *can't* with no *can***: *I'm afraid I can't refund it.* Full stop. The customer
hears a door closing and pushes on it.

The third is **losing the say-back when you're interrupted**. When they cut in, let them. Then start
the say-back again: *So …*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent staff often offer the *can* as a choice: *I can do this, or this. Which would you prefer?* A
choice gives the customer some control back.

Under pressure, people forget the check question. *Would that work for you?* is the sentence that turns
a customer from someone shouting into someone deciding.

Many angry customers know you can't give them everything. They're testing whether you'll take them
seriously. The say-back is how they know you did.
