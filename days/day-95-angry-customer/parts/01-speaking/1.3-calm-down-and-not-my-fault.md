## One-line answer

Take customer 1 from part 1.2 and record the answer twice. In take D, defend on purpose: cut in, say
*Calm down*, *It's not my fault*, *It's company policy*, and let your voice rise. In take H, use the five
moves. Play each to a different listener, who imagines being that customer. Ask: *From one to five, how
angry would you be now?* and *What happens next?* D should leave them angrier and not knowing what
happens. H should leave them calmer, knowing the next step.

## The story

Your aunt phoned her internet company because the connection had been down for three days. The young
man on the phone said:

> Madam, please calm down. It's not my department. It's policy, we can only send a technician after
> seventy-two hours.

She wasn't calm after that. She was furious. She asked for his manager.

The manager said:

> So your internet has been down since Monday, and you work from home. I understand why you're upset,
> and I'm sorry you've waited this long. I've booked a technician for tomorrow, between nine and twelve.
> Is someone home then?

Same company. Same rule. She ended the call calm.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **say-it-back**, **specific sorry**, **the promise**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-let-them-finish-say-it-back-say-what-next.md).
- **defensive**: protecting yourself instead of solving the problem.
- **anger-and-next test**: a listener, as the customer, scores their anger from one (calm) to five
  (furious) and says what happens next.

| Take | What's in it | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D, defensive (deliberate) | cutting in, *calm down*, *not my fault*, *policy*, rising voice | anger 4 or 5; next step unknown |
| H, the five moves | let them finish, say-back, acknowledge, specific sorry, promise and check | anger 1 or 2; next step known |

## Why Vaani needs it

Defensive lines feel natural when someone shouts at you. You can't hear from inside what they do to a
customer. The test lets a listener tell you, before a real customer does.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[A response to a complaint](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/c1/response-complaint)
advises "Choose vocabulary that is more objective and reduces negative emotions where possible". *Calm
down* does the opposite. It tells the customer their feeling is the problem.

**Step one: take D.** Write *deliberate* in your notebook. Ask your friend to play customer 1 again.
This time:

1. Cut in before they finish.
2. Say *Please calm down.*
3. Say *It's not my fault*, and blame someone else.
4. Say *It's company policy.*
5. Let your voice rise and speed up with theirs.

**Step two: take H.** The same customer, the five moves, your voice low and slow.

**Step three: the anger-and-next test.** Play D to one listener and H to another. Tell each: *You are
this customer.* Then ask:

| H | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

**The check, and how it goes RED.** This part is **RED** if H's listener scores 3 or more, or can't say
what happens next. It's **GREEN** when H's listener scores 1 or 2 and names the next step, and D's
listener scores higher.

**Step four: the repair.** If H went red on anger, listen for an excuse that slipped in before the
say-back, or a voice that rose. If it went red on the next step, your promise had no *when*. Record H
again and try a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an H with a hidden excuse**: *So your phone isn't
ready, but the supplier …*. The *but* undoes the say-back. Save the reason for later, if they ask.

The second is **an H that apologises for everything**: *I'm so sorry, so sorry, really very sorry.*
That sounds nervous, not caring. Say one specific sorry.

The third, in real life, is **the rule said as a wall**. If there is a real rule, put it inside the
*can*: *The rule is seventy-two hours, so I've booked the first slot after that, tomorrow at nine.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Most customers who shout at you aren't angry with you. They're angry with the thing that went wrong,
and you're the person in front of them. Knowing that makes the low, slow voice easier.

Under pressure, *calm down* comes out by itself. If you hear yourself say it, go straight to the
say-back: *Sorry. So you were told …*

A customer remembers the last thing you said more than the first. Make it the promise and the check.
