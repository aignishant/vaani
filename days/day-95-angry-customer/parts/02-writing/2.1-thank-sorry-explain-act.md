## One-line answer

A reply to a complaint has four paragraphs. **Thank** the customer and name their complaint with its
facts. Say **sorry** for the specific thing that went wrong. **Explain** in one or two plain sentences,
without blaming anyone. Then say what you'll **do** and by when, and how they can reach you. The tone is
formal and calm, with no contractions.

## The story

Your friend runs a small business from home, selling home-made pickles by post. A customer wrote to
complain: her jar arrived broken, the oil had soaked the box, and she'd paid for fast delivery.

Your friend was upset and wrote back quickly:

> Dear Madam, we pack all our jars very carefully and this has never happened before. The courier
> must have dropped it. We are not responsible for courier damage. Regards.

The customer posted the reply online, next to a photo of the oily box. Three regular customers asked
if they should still order.

Her sister rewrote it the next day:

> Dear Mrs Fernandes,
>
> Thank you for letting us know that your order of 12 March arrived broken. I am very sorry that your
> jar was damaged and your parcel was spoiled. ...

The customer replied with a thank-you, and ordered again.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **complaint letter**: a formal letter or email telling a company something went wrong and asking them
  to put it right, from
  [day 86](../../../day-86-making-complaint/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-four-paragraphs-of-a-complaint.md).
  Today you're writing the answer.
- **frame (of an email)**: the formal greeting, the reason line, the paragraphs, the closing line and
  the matching sign-off, from
  [day 82](../../../day-82-introducing-yourself-at-work/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-formal-frame-top-to-bottom.md).
- **reply to a complaint**: the formal letter or email a business sends back.
- **acknowledge**: show clearly that you've read and understood the complaint.
- **excuse**: a reason given to move the fault away from you, from
  [day 36](../../../day-36-sorry-and-thank-you/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-each-note-must-carry.md). An
  explanation says what happened. An excuse says it wasn't your fault.
- **compensation**: something you give to make up for the problem, such as a refund or a replacement.

## Why Vaani needs it

The phase 5 gate asks for a formal email with the correct greeting and sign-off and no contractions. A
reply to a complaint is the hardest formal email to keep calm. At any job with customers, you'll write
one sooner or later.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[A response to a complaint](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/c1/response-complaint)
gives these tips: "Use a formal register.", "Acknowledge the complaint and show you understand the
problem.", "Apologise and explain what steps will be taken to ensure it doesn't happen again." and
"Offer some kind of solution or compensation if appropriate." Its model says "On behalf of the company
I would like to apologise for the length of time it took for you to receive your final quote".

**The shape on the page.**

```text
 Subject: Your order of 12 March — broken jar

 Dear Mrs Fernandes,

 ¶1  THANK + NAME IT   Thank you for letting us know that your order of 12 March
                       arrived with a broken jar.

 ¶2  SPECIFIC SORRY    I am very sorry that your jar was damaged and your parcel
                       was spoiled.

 ¶3  EXPLAIN (short)   The jar was packed in one layer of paper, which was not
                       enough for the journey. From this week, every jar is
                       packed in a box inside the box.

 ¶4  ACT + CONTACT     I have sent a new jar today by fast delivery, at no cost,
                       and refunded your delivery charge. If you have any
                       questions, please reply to this email.

 Yours sincerely,
 Ritu Shah
```

**¶1 names their facts.** The date, the order, what went wrong. The customer knows you read it.

**¶2 is one specific sorry.** Say sorry for the thing, not *for any inconvenience caused*, which sounds
like a form.

**¶3 explains without blaming.** What happened, in plain words, and what changes so it doesn't happen
again. Don't blame the courier, a colleague or the customer, even if it's partly true.

**¶4 acts.** What you've done or will do, and when. How they can reach you.

**No contractions.** Write *I am*, *do not*, *it is*. That's this course's choice for formal emails.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the defence**: *We pack carefully. This has never happened before.*
The customer hears *we don't believe you*.

The second is **the blame**: *The courier must have dropped it.* Maybe. But the customer paid you, not
the courier.

The third is **the form reply**: *We apologise for any inconvenience caused. Your feedback is
important to us.* No date, no facts, no action. The customer can tell nobody read their letter.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often wait a little before replying to an angry letter, and write the reply once they
can read the complaint calmly. They don't wait too long, though. A slow reply feels like no reply.

Under pressure, when the complaint is unfair, ¶3 grows into a defence. Keep it to two sentences.

Replies to complaints are often shared. Other customers read them. A calm reply with an action in it
wins them too.
