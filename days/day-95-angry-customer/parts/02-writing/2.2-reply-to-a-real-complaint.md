## One-line answer

Take one real complaint and write it out first, in the customer's words. It can be one you made, one a
friend or family member received at work, or one you imagine for a place you know well. Then write the
full reply in four paragraphs, as the business. Give both to a reader who plays the customer, and ask
two questions: *Did they read my letter?* and *What happens now?*

## The story

Your cousin works in the office of a coaching centre. A parent wrote to complain that her son's class
had been cancelled twice with no message.

Your cousin's first draft began *Dear Parent* and never mentioned the dates. Before sending, she showed
it to her mother and asked her to read it as the parent. Her mother said:

> It could be to anyone. Did they even read what I wrote?

Your cousin put the two dates, the class name and the boy's name in ¶1. She said sorry for the missing
message, not for *any inconvenience*. And she said who would send the message next time. Her mother
read it again and said *Now I believe them.*

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **reply to a complaint**, **acknowledge**, **excuse**, **compensation**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-thank-sorry-explain-act.md).
- **their facts**: the dates, numbers, names and details the customer gave.
- **read-it test**: a reader, as the customer, answers *Did they read my letter?* and *What happens
  now?*

Writing the complaint out first is what makes the reply specific. You can't copy the customer's facts
into ¶1 if you don't have them in front of you.

## Why Vaani needs it

A formal reply that could go to anyone is the commonest failure of formal email. Here, a real customer
reads it and tells you whether it feels like it was written for them.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[A response to a complaint](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/c1/response-complaint)
advises "Emphasise the positive relationship with the customer", and its model reply opens with
*Thank you for …* before anything else.

**Step two: write the reply.** The four paragraphs: thanks with their facts; one specific sorry; a
short explanation with what changes; the action with a date, and how to reach you. Put it in the formal
frame: *Dear* + name, a comma, *Yours sincerely* with a name. No contractions. Don't invent a refund or
an action the business wouldn't really give. If it's a place you know, give what they really could.

**Step three: the read-it test.** Give the complaint and your reply to one reader. They read the
complaint first, as the customer, then your reply.

| Question | Their answer, in their words |
| --- | --- |
| Did they read my letter? How can you tell? | `TODO(me)` |
| What happens now, and when? | `TODO(me)` |

**Step four: fix it.** If they say *no* to the first question, put more of their facts into ¶1. If they
can't say what happens now, ¶4 needs an action and a date.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a reply with no facts from the complaint**: *Dear Customer, we are
sorry for your experience.* The reader can't tell which experience.

The second is **an action with no date**: *We will look into it.* The customer hears *nothing will
happen*.

The third is **compensation you can't give**: *We will refund you in full and give you a free year.* If
it isn't true, don't write it. A broken promise in writing is kept by the customer forever.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often start by copying the customer's key sentence into their notes, then answer it
point by point. That's the same as your facts table.

Under pressure, when there are many complaints, replies turn into forms. Even one real fact in ¶1 is
enough to show a person read it.

Most customers who complain want to be taken seriously more than they want money. A reply that names
their facts does that.
