## One-line answer

Make a copy of your reply that defends the business on purpose. It says *this has never happened*,
blames someone else, apologises only *for any inconvenience*, and ends with no action. Give that copy
and your real reply to two readers, each playing the customer. Ask each one: *From one to five, would
you use this business again?* and *What happens now?* The defending copy should score low, with no next
step. Your real reply should score 4 or 5 with a clear next step.

## The story

Your uncle ordered a sofa. It came a month late, in the wrong colour. He complained. The shop wrote
back:

> Dear Sir, we have checked our records and our delivery team followed all procedures. Colour can look
> different in different light. We apologise for any inconvenience caused. Regards, Customer Care.

He read it aloud to the family at dinner, in a funny voice. Everyone laughed. Then he told every
relative not to buy from that shop.

The shop may even have been partly right about the light. It didn't matter. The letter told him
nobody was going to help.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **excuse**, **acknowledge**: from [part 2.1](2.1-thank-sorry-explain-act.md).
- **read-it test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-reply-to-a-real-complaint.md).
- **defensive reply**: a reply that protects the business instead of solving the problem.
- **come-back test**: a reader, as the customer, scores from one to five whether they'd use the
  business again, and says what happens now.

| Copy | What's in it | What a reader should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D, defensive (deliberate) | *never happened before*; blame; *any inconvenience*; no action | come-back 1 or 2; no next step |
| R, real | your reply from [part 2.2](2.2-reply-to-a-real-complaint.md) | come-back 4 or 5; a clear next step |

## Why Vaani needs it

A defensive reply reads as fair to the person who wrote it, because they know their side. Only a reader
in the customer's place can tell you how it lands.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[A response to a complaint](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/writing/c1/response-complaint)
advises "Choose vocabulary that is more objective and reduces negative emotions where possible" and
"Emphasise the positive relationship with the customer".

**Step one: make copy D.** Copy your real reply and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Replace ¶1's facts with *We have checked our records.*
2. Add *This has never happened before.*
3. Blame someone else in ¶3: a courier, a supplier, the customer.
4. Change the specific sorry to *We apologise for any inconvenience caused.*
5. Delete the action in ¶4.

**Step two: the come-back test.** Give the complaint and D to one reader, and the complaint and R to a
different reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an R with *but* in the sorry**: *I am sorry your jar
broke, but we pack very carefully.* The *but* cancels the sorry. Take the second half out.

The second is **a reader who takes the business's side** because they know you. Ask them to be the
customer, and to read the complaint first.

The third, in real life, is **the reply to a complaint that's wrong**. Sometimes the customer is
mistaken. You can say so politely in ¶3, with a fact: *Our records show the parcel was delivered on
14 March and signed for.* Then still offer a next step.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

A defensive reply almost never changes the customer's mind about the facts. It only changes their mind
about you.

Under pressure, when the complaint feels unfair, write D first in your notebook if you need to. Then
write R, and send R.

Customers share bad replies more than good ones. A reply is written to one person but often read by
many.
