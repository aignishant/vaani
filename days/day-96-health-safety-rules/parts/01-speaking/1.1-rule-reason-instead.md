## One-line answer

When you tell someone a health or safety rule out loud, say three things: the **rule** (*You need to …*
or *You can't …*), the **reason** (*because …*), and what to do **instead** (*so …*). A rule with its
reason gets followed when you're not watching. A rule without one gets followed only while you are.

## The story

It's your first week managing the kitchen of a small restaurant. A new helper starts on Monday. You
walk him round quickly:

> Hair net. No phone in the kitchen. Don't touch the big fryer. Gloves for the salad.

By Wednesday his phone is on the shelf above the fryer, and he's making salad with bare hands.

The owner has a word with him:

> You need to wear gloves for the salad, because it isn't cooked, so anything on your hands goes
> straight to the customer. The gloves are in the blue box by the sink.
>
> You can't keep your phone in here, I'm afraid. If it falls into the oil, the oil jumps and burns
> people. Leave it in the locker, and you can check it on your break.

He never makes the same mistake again. Now he knows why.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **tune**: the way the voice goes up and down across a sentence. A warm tune starts high and falls,
  from [day 65](../../../day-65-polite-intonation/parts/01-speaking/1.1-start-high-let-it-fall.md).
- **I'm afraid**: a soft opening for bad news, from
  [day 93](../../../day-93-saying-no-politely/parts/01-speaking/1.1-thanks-no-reason-offer.md).
- **rule**: something a place says you must or must not do.
- **safety rule**: a rule that stops someone getting hurt.
- **health rule**: a rule that stops illness spreading, such as washing hands.
- **the instead**: what the person should do in place of the thing they can't do.

| Part | What it does | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rule | says what to do or not do | *You need to wear gloves.* / *You can't keep your phone in here.* |
| Reason | makes it make sense | *because the salad isn't cooked* / *if it falls in the oil, it burns people* |
| Instead | gives them something to do | *The gloves are in the blue box.* / *Leave it in the locker.* |

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Modals: permission and obligation](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/modals-permission-obligation)
says: "We use *can't* to talk about something that is against the rules, particularly when we didn't
make the rules." Its examples include "You can't take photos in the museum. They're really strict about
it." It also says "*May* is the most formal way to ask for and give permission."

**Saying the rule.** When you speak, these are the natural words:

| You mean | Say |
| --- | --- |
| it's required | *You need to …* / *You have to …* |
| it's not allowed | *You can't …* / *You're not allowed to …* |
| it's allowed | *You can …* |
| it's a good idea | *It's best to …* / *You should …* |

In speech, *You must …* sounds strong and stiff, like a sign reading itself aloud. Save it for the one
rule that really can't bend.

**Softening.** *I'm afraid* before a *can't* makes it kind without making it weaker: *I'm afraid you
can't bring food in here.*

**The reason is short.** One clause with *because* or *if*. The best reasons say what happens to a
person: *it burns people*, *someone could slip*, *people get ill*.

**The instead is practical.** Where the thing goes, what to use, who to ask.

**Name the trap.** A new rule said fast in a list sounds like an order. The same rule with a reason
sounds like help. Your tune matters too: let it start higher and fall, not stay flat.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the rule list**:

> No phone. Gloves. Hair net. Don't touch that.

The listener hears orders and remembers about half of them.

The second is **the rule with no instead**: *You can't park here.* Where can they park, then? They'll
park there anyway if there's nowhere else.

The third is **the reason that blames**: *because last time someone stupid burned their hand*. Keep the
reason about what could happen, not who did it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often tell a rule as a small story: *Last month the floor was wet and someone slipped,
so now we put the yellow sign out.* A story is a reason people remember.

Under pressure, in an emergency, drop the reason and the softener. *Stop! Hot oil!* is right. The
reasons are for the calm moment before.

A newcomer is usually scared of getting it wrong. A rule with a reason and an instead tells them
exactly how to get it right.
