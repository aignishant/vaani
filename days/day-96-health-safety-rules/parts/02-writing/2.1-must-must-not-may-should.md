## One-line answer

Written rules for a public place use four modals, each with one job: **must** for what is required,
**must not** for what is forbidden, **may** for what is allowed, and **should** for what is only
recommended. Don't use *shall*, and don't use *may not* for a ban, because both can be read two ways.
A reader decides from the modal alone whether they can skip a line.

## The story

Your residents' association rents out the community hall for family functions. The secretary wrote the
rules on a sheet by the door:

> 1. Music should end by 10 p.m.
> 2. Guests may not park in front of the gate.
> 3. The hall shall be cleaned after use.
> 4. Outside caterers are allowed.

The first family played music until midnight. *It says should,* they said, *not must.* The second family
parked in front of the gate. *May not means maybe not. We thought it might be fine on a Sunday.* The
third family left the cleaning. *Shall? Who shall? We thought the association does it.*

Every rule was on the sheet. None of them worked.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **modal**: a helper word such as *must*, *will*, *should*, *may* or *can*, which comes before the plain
  verb, from
  [day 91](../../../day-91-stress-in-long-words/parts/02-writing/2.1-three-formal-jobs-for-the-passive.md).
- ***should*, *must*, *have to*, *mustn't*, *don't have to***: advice, a rule you set, a rule from
  outside, *don't do it*, and *you don't need to*, from
  [day 65](../../../day-65-polite-intonation/parts/02-writing/2.1-advice-rule-no-choice-no-need.md).
  Day 65 was a note to someone you know. Today is a public sheet of rules for strangers.
- **written rules**: a list on a notice, a website or a form that tells everyone in a place what they
  must, must not and may do.
- **obligation**: something you are required to do.
- **prohibition**: something you are forbidden to do.
- **permission**: something you are allowed to do.
- **recommendation**: something that is a good idea but not required.

| Modal | Its job | A reader thinks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *must* | obligation | *I have to.* |
| *must not* | prohibition | *I can't.* |
| *may* | permission | *I'm allowed to.* |
| *should* | recommendation | *It's a good idea, but I can skip it.* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Familiar terms](https://digital.gov/guides/writing-understanding/familiar-terms), a government plain
language guide, says: "Use 'must' not 'shall' to impose requirements. 'Shall' is ambiguous, and rarely
occurs in everyday conversation." It gives "must" for an obligation, "must not" for a prohibition, "may"
for a discretionary action and "should" for a recommendation.
[may](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/may) notes that "may not is particularly likely to promote
confusion between the senses of 'will possibly not' and 'is forbidden to,'" and that some drafting rules
"require the use of 'must not' or similar for clarity."
[Modals: permission and obligation](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/b1-b2/modals-permission-obligation)
says "We use *must not* to talk about what is not permitted. It is common on public signs and notices",
with "Visitors must not park in the staff car park." It gives "Customers may request a refund within a
period of 30 days." for *may*.

**The pattern.** Modal + the plain verb, with no *to* after the modal:

> Guests must leave the hall by 11 p.m.
> Guests must not park in front of the gate.
> Guests may bring their own caterers.
> Guests should bring their own extension cables.

**The passive works too**, when the doer doesn't matter: *The hall must be cleaned after use.* But say
who does it if it matters. *The family who booked the hall must clean it after use* can't be misread.

**Two words to avoid.**

- *shall*: formal and old, and readers don't agree what it means. Use *must*.
- *may not* for a ban: it can mean *possibly won't*. Use *must not*.

**Name the trap.** In speech, *should* often means a rule: *You should be here by nine.* On a sheet of
written rules, a reader takes *should* as a recommendation. If it's a rule, write *must*.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is ***should* for a real rule**:

> Music should end by 10 p.m.

The reader decides it's optional, because on paper, that's what *should* means.

The second is ***may not* for a ban**:

> Guests may not park in front of the gate.

Some readers read *might not*, and park.

The third is ***must to***:

> Guests must to clean the hall.

A modal never has *to* after it. *Must clean.*

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers read a rules sheet as its laziest reader would. For every line, they ask *Could someone
say "it only says should"?* If the answer is yes, and it's a rule, they write *must*.

Under pressure, people make every line *must*, to be safe. Then nothing stands out. Use *must* for the
rules that matter, and *should* honestly for the ones that don't.

A reader of a public rules sheet has no one to ask. The modal is the only thing that tells them whether
a line is a rule, a ban, a yes or a tip.
