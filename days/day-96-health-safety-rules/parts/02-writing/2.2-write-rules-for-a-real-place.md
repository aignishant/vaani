## One-line answer

Write a real rules sheet of eight to ten lines for one place you know: a hall, a building, a clinic
waiting room, a shared kitchen, a class. First sort each rule into *required*, *forbidden*, *allowed*
or *recommended*. Then write each with the modal that matches. Give it to a reader and ask them to mark
every line *have to*, *can't*, *allowed* or *tip*. Their marks should match your sorting exactly.

## The story

Your aunt runs a small day-care centre from her ground floor. Parents kept asking the same questions:
*Can we send lunch? Do we have to pick up by five? Can grandparents collect?*

She sat down with a notebook and made four columns: *have to*, *can't*, *allowed*, *good idea*. She put
every rule in a column first. Then she wrote the sheet:

> Children must be collected by 5 p.m.
> Only adults named on the form may collect a child.
> Children must not bring nuts in any food.
> Parents should send a spare set of clothes.

The questions stopped. The one question that still came was about the spare clothes, and that was
fine. It was a *should*.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **modal**, **obligation**, **prohibition**, **permission**, **recommendation**, **written rules**:
  from [part 2.1](2.1-must-must-not-may-should.md).
- **sorting table**: four columns, *required*, *forbidden*, *allowed*, *recommended*, where you place
  each rule before you write it.
- **mark-it test**: a reader marks each line *have to*, *can't*, *allowed* or *tip*, and you compare
  their marks with your sorting.

Sorting first stops you choosing the modal by feel. You decide what the rule *is*, then the modal
follows.

Every column needs at least one rule.

**Step two: write the sheet.** A title that names the place and who it's for: *Community hall: rules
for families who book it.* Then one rule per line:

- modal + plain verb, no *to* after the modal
- the doer named: *Guests*, *Parents*, *Visitors*, *The family who booked*
- a number or a time where there is one: *by 10 p.m.*, *no more than 100 guests*
- no *shall*, no *may not*

**Step three: the mark-it test.** Give the sheet to one reader, without your sorting table. They mark
each line.

| 2 | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` | `TODO(me)` |

Add a row for every line.

**Step four: fix.** Any line that doesn't match gets a new modal, or a clearer doer. If the place is
real and the sheet is useful, give it to whoever runs the place.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the sheet with no *may***. It's all *must* and *must not*, and readers
start asking about everything that isn't listed. One or two *may* lines answer the questions before
they come.

The second is **the doer left out**: *Must be cleaned after use.* By whom? The reader decides it's
someone else.

The third is **two rules in one line**: *Guests must not smoke and should leave by eleven.* The reader
remembers one. Split them.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers of rules put the *must not* lines where the danger is: the no-nuts rule on the lunch
page, the no-parking rule on the gate. On one sheet, they put the most important *must* first.

Under pressure, a rules sheet grows every time something goes wrong, until nobody reads it. Keep it to
the rules that matter. Put the rest in a *should* or leave them out.

A reader who breaks a *should* isn't breaking a rule. If you'd be angry that they did it, it wasn't a
*should*.
