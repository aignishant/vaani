## One-line answer

Make a copy of your rules sheet with the modals swapped on purpose. Two *must* lines become *should*, a
*must not* becomes *may not*, and one *must* becomes *shall*. Give that copy and your real sheet to two
readers. Ask each one: *Which of these could you skip or ignore?* The copy's reader should skip real
rules. Your real sheet's reader should skip only the *should* lines.

## The story

Your friend's gym had a sign by the weights:

> Members should wipe the benches after use. Members may not drop the weights. Towels shall be
> carried at all times.

Nobody wiped the benches, because it only said *should*. The owner found a cracked floor tile, and a
young man said *It says may not. I thought it was a suggestion.* And nobody could say who *shall* was
talking to.

The owner rewrote it: *Members must wipe the bench after use. Members must not drop the weights. Members
must carry a towel.* It looked less polite. It worked.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

A swapped modal is one small word, and the writer doesn't see it, because they know what they meant.
The skip test shows you what a reader decides from that one word.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Familiar terms](https://digital.gov/guides/writing-understanding/familiar-terms): "'Shall' is
ambiguous, and rarely occurs in everyday conversation." It gives "should" for a recommendation.
[may](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/may): "may not is particularly likely to promote confusion between
the senses of 'will possibly not' and 'is forbidden to,'".

**Step one: make copy S.** Copy your real sheet and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Change two *must* lines to *should*.
2. Change one *must not* line to *may not*.
3. Change one *must* line to *shall*, with the passive: *The hall shall be cleaned.*

**Step two: the skip test.** Give S to one reader and R to another. Each underlines every line they
think they could skip or ignore.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who's too careful** and skips nothing on
either sheet. Ask them: *Imagine you're in a hurry and you don't like rules. What would you ignore?*

The second is **an R that uses *should* to sound polite**. Politeness goes in the title or a *please*
line at the top: *Please help us keep the hall open for everyone.* The rules themselves stay *must*.

The third, in real life, is **the complaint after the sign changes**: *It never said we had to.* Often,
it's true. That's what the skip test is for.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Many writers soften rules because *must* feels rude. On a public sheet, *must* isn't rude. It's clear,
and clear is fair: everyone knows where they stand.

Under pressure, when a rule has been broken, people add capitals and exclamation marks: *YOU MUST NOT!!!*
The modal was already enough. Keep the sheet calm.

A reader who follows a *should* is being kind. A reader who follows a *must* is following a rule. A
good sheet lets them tell the difference.
