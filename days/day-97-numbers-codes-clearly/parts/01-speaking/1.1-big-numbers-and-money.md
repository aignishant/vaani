## One-line answer

Say a big number in its groups, the way it's built: *two thousand, four hundred and fifty*. The *and* is
small and quick. The group words, *thousand* and *hundred*, are clear. After a decimal point, say
**point** and then each digit on its own: *twelve point seven five*. And when your listener may not be
from South Asia, turn *lakh* into *a hundred thousand*.

## The story

You're on the phone to an online shop's help line about a refund. The agent is in another country. You
say the amount the way you'd say it at home:

> The refund is one lakh twenty-four thousand.

There's a pause. *Sorry, one … lock?* You say it again, faster. The agent writes *1,024* and says *So,
one thousand and twenty-four?*

Your sister takes the phone:

> It's one hundred and twenty-four thousand. One, two, four, then three zeros.

The agent reads it back correctly the first time.

**Words to know**

- **numbers one to a hundred**, **the teens and the tens**: from
  [day 10](../../../day-10-numbers-times-dates/parts/01-speaking/1.1-numbers-out-loud.md).
- **chunk**, **read-back**: a few words said as one piece, and asking the other person to repeat
  what you gave them, from
  [day 76](../../../day-76-speaking-on-bad-line/parts/01-speaking/1.1-slow-chunk-spell-read-back.md).
- **stress**: the extra force on one syllable, making it louder and longer, from
  [day 7](../../../day-07-word-stress/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-loud-syllable.md).
- **group word**: *hundred*, *thousand*, *million*: the words that tell the listener how big the number
  is.
- **weak form**: the small, quick, quiet version of a little word in a normal sentence, from
  [day 19](../../../day-19-can-and-cant/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-quiet-can-and-the-loud-cant.md).
  Today it's *and* inside a big number.
- **decimal point**: the dot in 12.75, said *point*.
- **lakh**: a hundred thousand, used in South Asian English.

**Build it in groups.** Say the thousands, then the hundreds, then the rest:

> two thousand, four hundred and fifty
> one hundred and twenty-four thousand

**The *and* is weak.** In British English it comes before the last two digits. It's short: more like
*n* than *and*. The group words carry the stress. Both the British way with *and* and the American way
without it are correct. Pick one and keep it.

**The th in thousand.** It starts with /θ/, the
[tongue-between-the-teeth sound](../../../day-03-two-th-sounds/parts/01-speaking/1.1-tongue-between-the-teeth.md).
Put the tongue out for it. In a big number, *thousand* is the word the listener most needs to hear.

**After the point, one digit at a time.** *Twelve point seven five*, not *twelve point seventy-five*.

**Lakh and crore.** With a listener who uses them, they're fine. With anyone else, convert: one lakh is
*a hundred thousand*, ten lakh is *a million*. If you're not sure, say both: *one lakh, that's a hundred
thousand*.

**Name the trap.** If your first language groups big numbers differently, you may pause in the wrong
place: *one hundred and twenty … four thousand*. Pause only after a group word.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the digit string**: *one two four zero zero zero*. The listener counts
zeros and often gets it wrong by one.

The second is **the strong *and***: *two hundred AND fifty*. It sounds like two numbers, two hundred
and another fifty.

The third is ***lakh* to a listener who doesn't use it**. They hear a word they don't know and guess.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often say a big amount two ways when it matters: *a hundred and twenty-four thousand,
that's one, two, four, and three zeros*. The second way is the check.

Under pressure, big numbers speed up. Slow down on the group words. They're what the listener writes
first.

A listener writing a number down hears the group word and puts in the comma. If you say *thousand*
clearly, they'll put the comma in the right place.
