## One-line answer

Reference codes and email addresses have symbols in them, and each symbol has a spoken name: **slash**
for /, **hyphen** or **dash** for -, **dot** for ., **at** for @, and **underscore** for _. Say the
symbol's name in its place, with a small pause on each side, and say *capital* before a capital letter
if the case matters.

## The story

Your friend phones her college to ask about her certificate. The clerk asks for the application number.
It's written on her slip as ADM/2026-114. She reads out the letters and numbers:

> A, D, M, two zero two six, one one four.

The clerk types *ADM2026114*. *No record.* She tries again. *No record.*

Her brother takes the slip and says:

> A, D, M, slash, two zero two six, hyphen, one one four.

The file comes up at once.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **group word**: from [part 1.1](1.1-big-numbers-and-money.md).
- **chunk**, **read-back**: from
  [day 76](../../../day-76-speaking-on-bad-line/parts/01-speaking/1.1-slow-chunk-spell-read-back.md).
- **code**: a string of letters, numbers and symbols that an office uses to find something.
- **symbol**: a written mark that isn't a letter or a number, such as / or @.
- **case**: whether a letter is a capital or a small letter.

| Symbol | Say | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| / | slash | invoice and file numbers, dates |
| - | hyphen, or dash | reference codes, phone numbers |
| . | dot | email and web addresses |
| @ | at | email addresses |
| _ | underscore | email addresses, user names |

## Why Vaani needs it

hyphen in them, and many offices can only find your file if every symbol is in place. Your email
address, said on the phone, is one of the codes you'll say most often.

When you read the @ in an address aloud, you say just *at*.

**Say the symbol in its place.** Treat each symbol as one chunk, with a small pause either side:

> A, D, M … slash … two, zero, two, six … hyphen … one, one, four.
> r … dot … sharma … underscore … nine, two … at …

**Say *capital* only when case matters.** Many codes are all capitals, so you don't need it. For a
password or a code that mixes cases, say *capital R*, *small r*.

**Hyphen or dash?** Both are understood for - in a code. Pick one and use it every time, so the listener
doesn't wonder whether they're two different marks.

**Read it back.** Ask the listener to read the whole code back, symbols included. If they leave out a
symbol, say that symbol alone: *There's a slash after the M.*

**Name the trap.** It's easy to hear symbols as spacing, not as characters, and leave them out. For the
office's computer, a missing slash is a different code.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the silent symbol**, as in the story. You read the letters and numbers,
and the slash and hyphen disappear.

The second is ***full stop* in an email address**. In an address, it's *dot*. *Full stop* is for the end
of a sentence.

The third is **the underscore said as *line* or *dash***. The listener types a hyphen, and the email
bounces.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often say an email address in two passes: first the whole thing at normal speed, then
slowly, chunk by chunk, with every symbol.

Under pressure, on a bad line, symbols are the first thing lost. Say them a little louder than the
letters around them.

Most office systems won't find a code with one character wrong. The clerk won't know which character is
missing. You have to give them every one.
