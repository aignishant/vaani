## One-line answer

Choose five real items: a big amount, an amount with a decimal point, a reference code with a slash or
hyphen, an email address, and an amount you'd normally say in lakh. Record them as if you were giving
them to an office on the phone. A listener who has never seen them writes down exactly what they hear.
Compare their page with the truth, character by character.

## The story

Your uncle had to give his electricity account details over the phone: the account number with a
hyphen in it, the amount he'd paid, and his email for the receipt. Before calling, he wrote all three on
a card, with the symbols circled.

On the call, he said each one slowly, with every symbol named. Then he asked:

> Could you read that back to me, please?

The agent read the email back with a hyphen where the underscore should be. He said *No, it's an
underscore, the low line*, and she fixed it. The receipt came that evening.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a listener who guesses** the familiar part: *oh, it's a mail address,
I'll add the usual ending*. Use a listener who doesn't know your details.

The second is **fixing the listener's page instead of your voice**. If they wrote it wrong, the
recording was unclear. Record it again.

The third is **item 5 said in lakh, even for this test**. The test is whether a listener who doesn't use
lakh would get it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers keep their most-used codes on a note in their phone, with the symbols written clearly,
and read from it on the phone.

Under pressure, people rush the item they know best, usually their own email address, because they've
said it a hundred times. The listener hasn't heard it once.

The dictation test is what every office call really is. Somebody on the other end is typing what you
say.
