## One-line answer

Record your five items twice. In take F, say them fast on purpose, in one breath each: the big amount
as a digit string, the decimal as *point seventy-five*, the codes with the symbols left out, and the
lakh amount in lakh. In take C, use every rule from parts 1.1 and 1.2. Play each take to a different
listener who writes down all five. F should come back with errors. C should come back right, character
by character.

## The story

Your cousin phoned a courier company to change the delivery address on a parcel. The agent asked for
the tracking code. It was on the screen in front of him, so he said it the way he read it, in one fast
breath:

> DL-4471-K, the pin is one one zero zero one seven.

The agent heard *DL four four seven one K*, with no hyphens, and *one one zero zero seventeen*. The
system found no parcel, then found somebody else's. The parcel went to the old address.

The next time he called, he said each group slowly, with *hyphen* in its place, and asked for a
read-back. It took a little longer. It worked.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **one-breath take**: a number or code said fast, with no pauses and no symbols.

| Take | How it's said | What a listener should write |
| --- | --- | --- |
| F, fast (deliberate) | one breath each; digit strings; *point seventy-five*; symbols left out; lakh | at least two items wrong |
| C, careful | groups, a weak *and*, digit by digit after the point, every symbol named, thousands | all five right |

## Why Vaani needs it

When you say a code you know well, it sounds clear to you, even when you say it in one breath. The only
way to hear what a stranger hears is to let a stranger write it down.

by digit. Record C again and try a new listener.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take F that's still careful**. You slow down without
meaning to. Read F off the page at your normal fast speed.

The second is **a listener who can pause the recording**. Real calls don't pause. Play it once.

The third, in real life, is **the code said from a screen**. When you read from a screen, you read at
reading speed, which is too fast for listening. Look away from the screen after each chunk.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

A code said in one breath feels efficient. To the person typing, it's a puzzle. The careful way is
slower to say and faster to finish, because nobody has to call back.

Under pressure, with a queue on the line or an agent who sounds rushed, you'll speed up. The agent needs
the code right more than they need it fast.

Most agents will read back if you ask. Ask every time. It's the step that turns *I think they got it*
into *they got it*.
