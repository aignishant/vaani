## One-line answer

A sheet of instructions and rules has three parts on the page. A **title** says what it's for. **Numbered
steps** give the order, one action each, each a full sentence starting with a verb. **Bullets** give the
rules that have no order, under a lead-in line. Any **warning** goes before the step it belongs to, never
after it, and if it could hurt someone, it goes at the top as well.

## The story

In your paying-guest house, the owner pinned a note above the shared washing machine:

> Put clothes in and add powder then close door and choose programme and press start, the door stays
> locked after so don't pull it. Don't put shoes. Only use the blue powder. Also switch off
> the tap after, it leaks. Don't wash after 10 at night.

A new girl pulled the door the moment the machine stopped, and the handle snapped off. She'd read *so
don't pull it* after she'd already pulled. Someone else washed shoes, because the rule was hidden in the
middle of the steps.

Your friend rewrote the note on a clean page. Nobody broke the handle again.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **imperative**: a sentence telling somebody to do something, with no subject written: *Close the
  door.*, from
  [day 32](../../../day-32-giving-instructions/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-imperative-on-paper.md). Day
  32 taught the sentence. Today is the layout of a whole sheet.
- ***must*, *must not*, *may*, *should***: the four modals of written rules, from
  [day 96](../../../day-96-health-safety-rules/parts/02-writing/2.1-must-must-not-may-should.md).
- **instructions**: the steps to do something, in order.
- **step**: one instruction in a set of directions, ending at something visible, from
  [day 23](../../../day-23-asking-directions/parts/02-writing/2.1-one-instruction-per-step.md). Today
  it's one action in a set of instructions for a machine or a task.
- **numbered list**: a list with 1, 2, 3, used when the order matters.
- **bullet list**: a list with a dot or dash in front of each item, used when the order doesn't matter.
- **lead-in line**: the line above a bullet list that says what the list is about.
- **warning**: a line that tells the reader about a danger or a way to break something.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[A to Z style guide](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/)
says: "Use numbered steps instead of bullet points to guide a user through a process." and "Steps end in
a full stop because each should be a complete sentence." For bullets: "you always use a lead-in line;
you use more than one bullet; the bullets make sense running on from the lead-in line; you use lower
case at the start of the bullet; you do not use more than one sentence per bullet."
[Instructions](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-esc-technicalwriting/chapter/instructions/), a
technical writing textbook chapter, says notes and warnings belong in the steps, and that "you may need
to emphasize notes and warnings earlier in the instructions, especially if there is a possibility of
readers ruining their equipment, wasting supplies, causing the entire procedure to fail, and/or hurting
themselves."

**The shape on the page.**

```text
 WASHING MACHINE: HOW TO USE IT                         ← title: what it's for

 Warning: the door stays locked after the wash ends.    ← warning at the top
 Do not pull it. It opens by itself.

 1. Put your clothes in the drum.                       ← one action per step
 2. Add one scoop of the blue powder to the drawer.
 3. Close the door until it clicks.
 4. Turn the dial to your programme and press Start.
 5. Wait for the light to go off. Then open the door.   ← the warning, again, at its step
 6. Turn off the tap behind the machine.

 Please remember:                                       ← lead-in line
 • no shoes in the machine
 • only the blue powder
 • no washing after 10 p.m.
```

**Numbered steps.** One action each. Start with the verb. End with a full stop. If a step has *and* in
it, it's probably two steps.

**Bullets.** Rules with no order. A lead-in line with a colon. Each bullet reads on from the lead-in,
starts with a small letter, and is one short item.

**Warnings.** Put the warning *before* the step where the danger is, so the reader sees it before they
act. If it could hurt someone or break something, put it at the top too.

**Name the trap.** In a paragraph, the reader reads in order and acts as they go. A warning written
after the step is read after the damage is done.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the paragraph of steps**: *Put clothes in and add powder then close
door and …*. The reader loses their place, and every step is a little hidden.

The second is **the rule hidden among the steps**: *Don't put shoes* between step 3 and step 4. The
reader reads it as part of the process and forgets it next time.

The third is **the warning after the step**: *Press start. The door locks, so don't pull it.* By then,
someone has already pulled it.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers test instructions by handing them to someone who has never done the task, and watching
without helping. Every time the person stops, the writer changes the sheet.

Under pressure, instruction sheets grow by adding lines at the bottom: *Also …* *And please …*. After a
while the most important warning is the last line. Put each new line where it belongs, not at the end.

A reader of instructions is usually in the middle of doing something. They look at the sheet, then
look away, then look back. Numbers let them find their place again.
