## One-line answer

Write a full instruction sheet for one real machine or task that other people use: a title, any
warning at the top, numbered steps with one action each, and a bullet list of rules under a lead-in
line. Then hand it to someone who has never done the task, and watch them follow it without helping.
Every place they stop or ask is a line to fix.

## The story

Your uncle bought a water purifier. When he went away for a week, your aunt couldn't change the filter.
He'd told her how on the phone, but she couldn't remember the order.

When he got back, he wrote a sheet and taped it inside the cupboard. Then he asked your cousin, who'd
never touched the purifier, to change the filter using only the sheet. Your uncle stood behind her and
said nothing.

She stopped twice. At step 3 she asked *Which way do I turn it?* At step 5 she asked *Is it done now?*
He added *clockwise* to step 3, and a new step 6: *The green light comes on when it's ready.* The next
time, your aunt did it alone.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **instructions**, **step**, **numbered list**, **bullet list**, **lead-in line**, **warning**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-steps-numbered-rules-bulleted-warning-first.md).
- **first-timer**: someone who has never done the task.
- **do-it test**: a first-timer follows your sheet without help, and you note every place they stop,
  ask or go wrong.

The do-it test is the only way to find what you left out. You can't see a missing step yourself,
because you do it without thinking.

## Why Vaani needs it

Instructions are formal writing that someone acts on alone, with nobody to ask. This is where the page

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[A to Z style guide](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/):
"Use numbered steps instead of bullet points to guide a user through a process."
[Instructions](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-esc-technicalwriting/chapter/instructions/):
"Most instructions number the tasks and steps chronologically".

**Step one: choose a real task.** Something other people at home or work really do, or should do: a
machine, a lock-up routine, a first-aid box, a way to report a problem.

**Step two: write the sheet.**

- a title that says what it's for, and for whom if that matters
- any warning at the top, and again just before its step
- numbered steps: one action each, verb first, full stop at the end
- a lead-in line and bullets for rules with no order, using *must*, *must not*, *may* and *should*
  where a rule needs one
- a last step that tells the reader how they know they've finished

**Step three: the do-it test.** Give the sheet to a first-timer. If you can, stand by the real machine.
If you can't, ask them to *say* what they'd do at each step. Don't help, don't point, don't explain.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **helping during the test**. You say *No, the other way*, and the sheet
never learns that step 3 was unclear. Keep your hands in your pockets.

The second is **the missing finish line**. The reader does the last step and doesn't know whether it
worked. Add how they know: a light, a sound, a click.

The third is **steps written from memory, away from the machine**. You'll skip the ones your hands do
without thinking. Write it standing next to it if you can.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers of instructions expect the first version to fail the do-it test. The test is part of
writing, not a check after it.

Under pressure, when a machine is urgent, like a gas cylinder or a fuse box, the warning matters most.
Put it at the top in plain words, not buried in a step.

A real first-timer doesn't read the whole sheet first. They read step 1 and start. That's why the
warning goes before its step, not only at the top.
