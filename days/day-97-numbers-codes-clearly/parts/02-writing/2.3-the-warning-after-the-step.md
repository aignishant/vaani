## One-line answer

Make a copy of your instruction sheet that breaks the layout on purpose. The steps become one
paragraph, a rule is hidden between two steps, and the warning comes after the step it belongs to. Give
that copy and your real sheet to two first-timers. Ask each one to go through it one line at a time,
saying what they'd do, and stop at the first place they'd have done something wrong. The copy should
catch them. Your real sheet shouldn't.

## The story

At your office, the note on the paper shredder said:

> Put the papers in slowly and not too many at a time and it will pull them in, if it stops press
> reverse. Don't put staples or plastic. Be careful with ties and long hair near the opening.

A new colleague leaned over to feed in a thick pile, and her scarf went in with it. She pulled free,
and the scarf was ruined. She'd read *be careful with ties* at the very end, after she'd already
started.

The office manager rewrote it with the warning at the top, in its own line, before step 1.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **step**, **numbered list**, **warning**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-steps-numbered-rules-bulleted-warning-first.md).
- **first-timer**, **do-it test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-write-instructions-for-a-real-machine.md).
- **first-mistake test**: a first-timer goes through the sheet line by line, saying what they'd do,
  and stops at the first point where they'd have done something wrong or dangerous.

| Copy | What's in it | What a first-timer should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P, paragraph (deliberate) | steps run together; a rule between steps; the warning after its step | a first mistake before they reach the warning |
| R, real | your sheet from [part 2.2](2.2-write-instructions-for-a-real-machine.md) | no mistake |

## Why Vaani needs it

A warning after its step looks fine to the writer, because the writer reads the whole sheet at once. A
first-timer doesn't. This test makes the reader act one line at a time, the way people really use
instructions.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Instructions](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-esc-technicalwriting/chapter/instructions/): "you
may need to emphasize notes and warnings earlier in the instructions, especially if there is a
possibility of readers ruining their equipment, wasting supplies, causing the entire procedure to fail,
and/or hurting themselves."
[A to Z style guide](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/):
"Steps end in a full stop because each should be a complete sentence."

**Step one: make copy P.** Copy your real sheet and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Remove the numbers and join all the steps into one paragraph with *and* and *then*.
2. Move one rule from the bullets into the middle of the paragraph.
3. Move the warning to just after the step it belongs to, and remove it from the top.

**Step two: the first-mistake test.** Give P to one first-timer and R to another. Cover the sheet with a
piece of paper, and let them slide it down one line at a time. At each line, they say what they'd do.
They stop at the first place they'd have done something wrong.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a first-timer who reads the whole sheet first**. The
covering paper matters. Real readers act as they read.

The second is **a P that's still numbered**. The numbers help the reader so much that the test can't
show what a paragraph does. Take them all out.

The third, in real life, is **the warning in small writing at the bottom**. On a real sheet, the warning
is its own line, at the top and before its step, where the eye lands first.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

The first go is when a reader knows least, and it's when they read as they act. The layout is
for them.

Under pressure, a sheet gets fixed by adding a new warning at the bottom. It should go before its step.

A reader trusts numbered steps to be complete. If a warning isn't in the right step, they won't go
looking for it.
