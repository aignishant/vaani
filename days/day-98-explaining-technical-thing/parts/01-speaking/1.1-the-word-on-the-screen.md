## One-line answer

When you explain a technical thing to a beginner, first find your own **jargon**: the words you use
without thinking that they don't know. Replace each one with plain words, except the words they'll
**see on the screen or the machine**. Those you name, point to, and explain once. Then check each step
with a **do-question**, *What will you press next?*, never with *Do you understand?* And keep your hands
off: they press, you talk.

## The story

Your mother wants to pay the electricity bill on her phone. You sit beside her and say:

> Open the app, go to bills, select the biller, it'll autofetch, then just authenticate with your PIN,
> OK? Understood?

She says *Yes*. You take the phone and do it for her, because it's faster.

Next month she calls you at work. *Which one is the biller?* She'd said *yes* because you were in a
hurry.

Your brother tries the next month. He puts the phone in her hand and says:

> See the word *Bills* at the bottom? Press that. ... Good. Now you'll see a list of companies. Ours is
> the one that says *Electricity*. Which one will you press?

She points. *That one.* *Yes.* He never touches the phone. The month after, she does it alone.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **what it's for, what it's like, how it works**: the order for explaining something new, from
  [day 70](../../../day-70-explaining-how-it-works/parts/01-speaking/1.1-what-its-for-what-its-like-how-it-works.md).
  Day 70 gave the order. Today is about the words inside it.
- **jargon**: words used inside one field or workplace, from
  [day 33](../../../day-33-your-job-or-studies/parts/01-speaking/1.3-the-answer-nobody-could-follow.md).
- **technical**: to do with machines, phones, computers, tools or a specialist job.
- **screen word**: a word the beginner will actually see on the screen or the machine: a button, a
  menu, a label.
- **do-question**: a check question the beginner can only answer by doing or pointing: *What will you
  press next?*
- **hands off**: the beginner holds the phone or touches the machine; you only talk.

| Word type | What to do with it | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Screen word | name it, point to it, say what it does, keep it | *See* Bills *at the bottom? That's where all your bills are.* |
| Your jargon, not on screen | replace it with plain words | not *autofetch*, but *it finds the amount by itself* |

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Curse of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge) defines it as "a cognitive bias
that occurs when a person who has specialized knowledge assumes that others share in that knowledge."
It gives the example that "A knowledgeable professor might no longer remember the difficulties that a
student faces when learning a new subject."
[Familiar terms](https://digital.gov/guides/writing-understanding/familiar-terms), a plain-language
guide, says: "When you're making word choices, pick the familiar or commonly used word over the unusual
or obscure."

**Find your jargon first.** Before you start, say the explanation to yourself and listen for the words
you'd never say to a child. Those are the ones.

**Screen words stay.** If the button says *Scan*, don't call it *the camera thing*. The beginner needs
to find *Scan* when you're not there. Name it, point to it, say what it does, once:

> This button says *Scan*. It opens the camera so you can read the square code.

**Other jargon goes.** Put plain words in its place: *authenticate* becomes *type your secret number*.

**Check with a do-question.** After each step, ask a question they can only answer by doing:

> What will you press now?
> Show me where the amount is.

*Do you understand?* always gets *yes*. It tells you nothing.

**Hands off.** If you press it, they learn that you press it.

**Name the trap.** The better you know something, the harder it is to hear your own jargon. The
page above calls this the curse of knowledge. It's why experts often explain badly.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the jargon run**, as in the story: *select the biller, it'll
autofetch, authenticate with your PIN*. Every word is correct. The listener understands none of them.

The second is ***OK? Understood?*** The answer is always *yes*, especially from a parent, a customer or
anyone who doesn't want to seem slow.

The third is **taking the phone**. It's faster today and slower every month after.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent explainers often ask the beginner to explain it back at the end, as if to a third person: *How
would you tell Aunty to do this?* The words they use tell you what stuck.

Under pressure, when you're busy or the beginner is slow, the jargon comes back and your hand reaches
for the phone. Slow down. Ask one more do-question.

A beginner who says *yes* often means *I don't want to ask again*. A do-question lets them show you
what they know without having to admit what they don't.
