## One-line answer

Teach one real technical thing to one real beginner, with the recorder on. First make a jargon list:
the words you'd normally use, split into screen words to keep and jargon to replace. Then teach it hands
off, with a do-question after each step. At the end, the beginner does the whole task once more alone
while you stay quiet. Count how often they had to ask, and how often your jargon slipped out.

## The story

Your friend works in a shop that sells mobile phones. Her grandfather wanted to send voice messages to
his grandson abroad.

She sat down first and wrote her normal words in two columns. Keep: *the microphone button*, *the send
arrow*. Replace: *hold to record* became *keep your finger on it while you talk*. *Swipe to cancel*
became *slide your finger left if you made a mistake*.

Then she taught him, hands off. He asked four questions. At the end, he sent a message to his grandson
on his own, with her sitting on her hands. That evening his grandson sent one back.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **jargon**, **screen word**, **do-question**, **hands off**: from
  [part 1.1](1.1-the-word-on-the-screen.md).
- **beginner**: someone who has never done this task.
- **jargon list**: two columns, the screen words you'll keep and the jargon you'll replace, written
  before you start.
- **solo run**: the beginner does the whole task once more alone, while you say nothing.

## Why Vaani needs it

This is the first time in the course that someone else's success is the test of your spoken English.
If they can do it alone at the end, you explained it. That's the standard every long explanation from
here on is held to.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **a beginner who isn't really a beginner**. If they already half know
the task, the test can't show you anything. Find someone who has truly never done it.

The second is **talking during the solo run**. Even *good* or *careful* tells them they're about to
make a mistake. Silence.

The third is **counting questions as failure**. Questions during teaching are good. The solo run is the
test.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent explainers often write the screen words on a slip of paper for the beginner to keep: *Bills →
Electricity → Pay*. The explanation is heard once. The slip stays.

Under pressure, in a shop or at a help desk with a queue, people explain faster and touch more. The
beginner comes back tomorrow with the same question.

A beginner who succeeds alone remembers how that felt. They'll try the next technical thing more
bravely.
