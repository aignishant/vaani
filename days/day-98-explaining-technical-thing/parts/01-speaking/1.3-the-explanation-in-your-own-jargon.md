## One-line answer

Record your explanation twice. In take J, explain it on purpose the way you'd explain it to a
colleague: your jargon, no screen words named, and *OK? Understood?* at the end. In take P, use the
plain version from part 1.2. Play each take to a different listener who has never done the task. Ask
them to explain it back as if to a friend, and to score from one to five *Could you do it now?* J should
fail both. P should pass both.

## The story

Your cousin is a nurse. She explained to a neighbour how to use a blood pressure machine at home:

> Wrap the cuff above the elbow, arm at heart level, hit start, it'll inflate, stay still till it
> deflates, then note the systolic and diastolic and message me both. OK?

The neighbour nodded. That night he called her. *Which number is the systolic? The top one? And what's
heart level? My chest?*

She said later: *Every word was right. I've said it a thousand times. I just forgot he'd never heard
any of it.*

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **jargon**, **screen word**, **do-question**: from [part 1.1](1.1-the-word-on-the-screen.md).
- **jargon list**: from [part 1.2](1.2-teach-one-real-thing.md).
- **explain-back test**: a listener who has never done the task explains it to an imaginary friend, then
  scores from one to five whether they could do it now.

| Take | What's in it | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| J, jargon (deliberate) | your normal field words; no screen words named; *OK? Understood?* | an explain-back with gaps; a score of 1 to 3 |
| P, plain | screen words named and explained; other jargon replaced; do-questions | a working explain-back; a score of 4 or 5 |

## Why Vaani needs it

Your own jargon is the one thing you can't hear, because it doesn't sound like jargon to you. A
listener who has never done the task is the only instrument that can find it.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Curse of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge): "a cognitive bias that occurs
when a person who has specialized knowledge assumes that others share in that knowledge."

**Step one: take J.** Write *deliberate* in your notebook. Record the explanation as if to a colleague
who knows it well: every piece of jargon from the right-hand column of your jargon list, no screen
words pointed out, and *OK? Understood?* at the end.

**Step two: take P.** Record the plain version, with screen words named and do-questions. For a
recording, say the do-question and pause, as if waiting for their answer.

**Step three: the explain-back test.** Play J to one listener and P to another. Neither has done the
task. Then ask each: *Explain it to me as if I were your friend.* Write down what they say. Then: *From
one to five, could you do it now?*

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take J that's already too plain**. It's hard to be
unclear on purpose. Imagine you're telling a colleague in a hurry.

The second is **a listener from your own field**. They'll understand J perfectly. Use someone who has
never done the task.

The third, in real life, is **the jargon in the last sentence**. The explanation is plain until the
end, and then the one number or word that matters comes out in jargon: *systolic*. Listen hardest to
your last sentence.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Experts are often the worst explainers of their own field, not because they don't know enough, but
because they can't remember not knowing it. The explain-back test gives that memory back.

Under pressure, with a patient, a customer or a parent, your field's words are the fastest way to say
something. They're also the slowest way for it to be understood.

A listener who explains it back in their own words has understood it. A listener who repeats your
words may only have remembered them.
