## One-line answer

To explain a technical thing in writing to a beginner, open with **what it is for**. Then, the first
time you use each technical word, **define it** in plain words in the same sentence or the next one.
After that, **keep that word**. Use exactly the same word every time, never a different one for
variety. Add one **example** from the reader's life, and only then say how it works.

## The story

Your family has a power backup at home, and your cousin is coming to stay while you're away. You leave
her a note:

> If the power goes, the inverter will switch on automatically. The UPS gives about four hours. If
> the backup beeps, the battery is low, so switch off the AC. Don't overload the unit.

She reads it three times. *So there are four machines? An inverter, a UPS, a backup and a battery? And
what's the unit?* When the power went, she went looking for the UPS.

There was only one machine. You'd called it by four names, because at home everyone does.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **name, result, stages, end**: the shape of a written process, from
  [day 70](../../../day-70-explaining-how-it-works/parts/02-writing/2.1-name-result-stages-end.md).
  Day 70 was the order of the steps. Today is the words.
- **group, then difference**: a definition that says what kind of thing it is and how it differs from
  others of that kind, from
  [day 75](../../../day-75-thing-without-name/parts/02-writing/2.1-group-then-difference.md).
- **technical term**: a word from a specialist field, such as *inverter*, *router* or *circuit
  breaker*.
- **define**: to say in plain words what a term means.
- **elegant variation**: using a different word for the same thing, to avoid repeating yourself.
- **one-thing-one-word**: the rule that one thing gets one name, every time, all through the text.

## Why Vaani needs it

On day 99 you'll plan an essay, and on day 111 you'll write a report on your own field. Both need
terms defined once and kept. A reader who meets two names for one thing will think there are two
things.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Elegant variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elegant_variation): "Elegant variation is the use of
synonyms to avoid repetition or add variety."
[Style](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/style), a plain-language guide, says "Always
define an abbreviation the first time you use it" and "Good examples can substitute for long
explanations. The more complex the concept you are writing about, the more you should consider using
an example."
[Familiar terms](https://digital.gov/guides/writing-understanding/familiar-terms): "pick the familiar or
commonly used word over the unusual or obscure."

**The shape.**

```text
 WHAT IT'S FOR     Our home has a power backup, so the lights and fans keep
                   working when the electricity goes off.

 DEFINE, ONCE      The backup is a grey box under the stairs. It stores
                   electricity in a battery while the power is on, and gives
                   it back when the power goes.

 EXAMPLE           It works like a phone charger in reverse: it charges
                   while the power is on, and the house runs on it while
                   the power is off.

 HOW IT WORKS      When the power goes, the backup switches on by itself...
                   (the same word, "backup", every time)
```

**Define at first use.** The first time a term appears, say what it is in the same sentence or the next
one. Use *group, then difference*: *The backup is a box that stores electricity …*.

**Choose one word and keep it.** Pick the word the reader will see or hear most: the label on the
machine, the word on the screen, or the word the family uses. Then use it every time. In technical
writing, repeating a word is a kindness, not a mistake.

**Leave out terms the reader doesn't need.** If a word never appears on the machine and the reader
never has to say it, don't use it. That's two fewer things to learn.

**One example.** One comparison from the reader's life, after the definition, not before it.

**Name the trap.** Writers often reach for a different word so they don't repeat themselves. The page
above calls this elegant variation. In a story it can be fine. In an explanation, every new word can
look like a new thing.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **four names for one thing**, as in the story: *inverter*, *UPS*,
*backup*, *battery*. The reader counts four machines.

The second is **the term defined too late**: the word appears in the first paragraph and its meaning in
the third. By then the reader has guessed wrong.

The third is **the definition in jargon**: *The inverter converts DC to AC.* That's correct, and it
defines one unknown word with three more.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent technical writers often keep a small list of their terms beside them as they write, with the one
word they've chosen for each thing. Some documents print that list at the top.

Under pressure, writing quickly, you'll use whichever word comes to mind first. Read the text once more
just for names. Circle every word that refers to the same thing. There should be one.

A beginner reading an explanation is building a picture of the thing. Every new name adds a new piece
to the picture, whether you meant it to or not.
