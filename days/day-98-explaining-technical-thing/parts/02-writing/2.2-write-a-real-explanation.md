## One-line answer

Write a full explanation of one real technical thing, of a hundred and fifty to two hundred and fifty
words, for one real beginner. First make a term list: every technical word you'll use, your one chosen
word for each thing, and a plain definition. Then write it: what it's for, each term defined at first
use, one example, and how it works. Give it to a beginner and ask three questions it should answer.

## The story

Your sister works in an office where every new person gets lost in the shared folders. She wrote them
a page. Before writing, she made a list: *shared drive: the folders everyone in the office can open.
Team folder: one folder for each team, inside the shared drive. Archive: last year's files, which you
can read but not change.*

She used those three words and no others. She didn't write *server*, *network location* or *old
files*.

Then she gave the page to a new colleague and asked: *Where would you save a new invoice? Where would
you find last year's report? Can you edit last year's report?* All three answers came back right. She
printed the page and put it on every new desk.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Style](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/style): "Always define an abbreviation the
first time you use it" and "Good examples can substitute for long explanations."
[Elegant variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elegant_variation): "the use of synonyms to avoid
repetition or add variety."

**Step two: write it.** A hundred and fifty to two hundred and fifty words:

- a title that names the thing
- what it's for, in the first sentence
- each term defined the first time it appears
- one example from the reader's life
- how it works, and what the reader has to do

If there are steps, number them.

**Step three: circle the names.** Go through once, circling every word that names a thing. Check each
against the term list. Any word not in the *one word* column gets changed.

**Step four: the answer-three test.** Write three questions your explanation should answer: one about
what a term means, one about what to do, and one about what not to do. Give the page to a beginner. They
answer from the page alone.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the term list skipped**. Without it, the names drift as you write, and
you don't see it.

The second is **questions that only test memory**: *What colour is the box?* Ask about meaning and
action.

The third is **an explanation that's really a list of steps**. Steps tell the reader what to do today.
The explanation tells them what's happening, so they can cope when something's different tomorrow.
Keep both.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers of explanations often test on the least technical person they know. If that person gets
it, everyone will.

Under pressure, the definition is what gets cut. Keep it. Without it, the rest is a list of words.

Workplaces often use three names for one system. Your page can quietly fix that for every new person
who reads it.
