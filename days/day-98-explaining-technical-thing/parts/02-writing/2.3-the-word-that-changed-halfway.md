## One-line answer

Make a copy of your explanation with the words changed on purpose. Give the main thing three different
names, delete the definition of one term, and add one piece of jargon you never explain. Give that copy
and your real explanation to two beginners. Ask each one: *How many different things does this page talk
about? Name them.* Then ask your three questions again. The copy's reader should count too many things
and get answers wrong. Your real page's reader should count right and answer all three.

## The story

Your neighbour's son wrote instructions for his grandmother on how to use the new gas stove:

> Turn the knob to light the burner. If the hob doesn't light, check the igniter. When the cooktop is
> hot, don't touch the grate. Clean the stove top every week.

His grandmother read it and asked: *Which one is the hob? Is the cooktop the same as the stove top? And
where's the igniter?*

He'd written *burner*, *hob*, *cooktop* and *stove top*, some meaning the same thing and some not. He'd
never said what the igniter was. Every word was correct English. Together, they made four stoves.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## Why Vaani needs it

A synonym looks like good writing to the person who wrote it. It only shows up as a problem in the
reader's head. This test makes that visible.

## The rule

[Elegant variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elegant_variation): "Elegant variation is the use of
synonyms to avoid repetition or add variety."

**Step one: make copy V.** Copy your real explanation and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

2. Delete the definition of one term, but leave the term.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a reader who knows the thing already**. They'll count
right on any page. Use a real beginner.

The second is **a V where the three names are obviously silly**. Use names people really use, like the
four for the stove. The test is only useful if the variation looks normal.

The third, in real life, is **the label on the machine that uses a different word from the page**. If
the button says *Hob*, the page says *hob*, even if you'd call it something else.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Many readers treat a new word as a new thing, because in an explanation it usually is. Your repetition
is what tells them it's the same thing.

Under pressure, when a page gets updated by several people, each one uses their own word. A term list
at the top keeps them together.

A formal reader doesn't mind repetition in an explanation. They mind having to guess.
