## One-line answer

The middle of a talk is three points, and each point has the same three pieces: the **point** in one
sentence with its signpost (*My first point is …*), one **example** (*For example, …*), and a **turn**
that closes it and opens the next one (*So that's the cost. My second point is time.*). The turn is what
tells the audience that one point has ended and the next has begun.

## The story

At your residents' association meeting, a neighbour gives a talk about why the building needs a
water tank cleaning every six months. His opening is good: *I've got three reasons: health, cost and the
law.* Then:

> So the water has bacteria and people get sick and also the pipes get blocked which costs money and
> my cousin's building had a big bill and also there's a rule from the municipality, I think, and
> anyway the tank is very dirty, I saw it …

By the end, nobody can say where health stopped and cost began. Someone asks *What was the second
reason?*

The next month, a woman gives a talk about the lift. Same opening shape. Then:

> My first point is safety. The lift stopped twice last month with people inside. For example, Mrs
> Kaur was stuck between the third and fourth floors. So safety is the first reason.
>
> My second point is cost. …

Everyone could list her three points at the end. Two people repeated her example about Mrs Kaur.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **presentation**, **audience**, **the map (of a talk)**: a short talk to a group, the people
  listening, and the line near the start saying how many points are coming, from
  [day 89](../../../day-89-presenting-opening-closing/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-opening-four-lines.md).
- **recap**, **the one thing**: the map said again at the end, and the single thing you want the
  audience to take away, from
  [day 89](../../../day-89-presenting-opening-closing/parts/01-speaking/1.2-the-closing-four-lines.md).
- **the middle (of a talk)**: the points themselves, between the opening and the close, from
  [day 89](../../../day-89-presenting-opening-closing/parts/01-speaking/1.3-open-and-close-a-real-talk.md).
  Day 89 kept it short. Today it's the whole of the day.
- **signpost**: a short phrase telling the audience where they are in a talk, from
  [day 89](../../../day-89-presenting-opening-closing/parts/01-speaking/1.1-the-opening-four-lines.md).
- **point**: one reason or idea, said in one sentence.
- **example**: one real case that shows the point is true.
- **turn**: the sentence that closes one point and opens the next.

## The rule

**The page checked, opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Language for presentations](https://www.eapfoundation.com/speaking/presentations/language/) gives
these lines for the middle of a talk: to start, "To start with/Firstly, I'd like to look at..."; to move
on, "Let's now move on to/turn to... I now want to go on to..."; for an example, "Let me give you an
example... A good example of this is..."; and at the end, "To summarise... To recap... Let me now sum
up."

**The shape of one point.**

```text
  SIGNPOST + POINT   My first point is safety.
  EXAMPLE            For example, the lift stopped twice last month with people inside.
  SO                 So safety is the first reason.
  TURN               My second point is cost.  ← closes one, opens the next
```

**The point is one sentence.** If it needs *and also*, it's two points.

**One example, real and specific.** A name, a date, a number: *Mrs Kaur, between two floors, last
month*. The example is what makes the point believable.

**The *so* sentence** says the point again in a few words. It tells the audience the point is finished.

**The turn** uses a number: *My second point*, *Let's now move on to cost*, *Finally*. Pause before it.
The pause and the number together are what the ear hears as a new section.

**Name the trap.** In conversation, ideas run together with *and also* and *anyway*. In a talk, those
words hide the joins. The audience can't see your notes. They only hear the turns.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the river**, as in the story: points joined by *and also*, with no
turns. The audience hears one long point.

The second is **the point with no example**: *Safety is very important.* It's true, and nobody
remembers it.

The third is **three examples and no point**. The audience remembers the stories and can't say what
they were for.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often hold up fingers for *first*, *second* and *third*. It sounds simple, but it's
one more signal that a new point has begun.

Under pressure, when you're nervous, the pause before the turn disappears and the points run together.
Put a mark in your notes where each turn goes, and breathe there.

An audience can't scroll back. If they miss the start of a point, they need the turn to find their way
back in.
