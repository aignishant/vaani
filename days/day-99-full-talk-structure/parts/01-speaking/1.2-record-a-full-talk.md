## One-line answer

Give one real talk in full to an audience of two or three people: the opening with its map, three
points each with an example and a turn, and the close with its recap and the one thing. Prepare it on
a talk card of notes, not sentences. Record it in one take. Then ask each listener to name the three
points and one example from each.

## The story

Your cousin had to give a talk at her college about her summer project. She wrote the whole talk out
word for word, then read it to the class. She read too fast, looked at the page the whole time, and
lost her place twice.

Her teacher asked her to try again the next week with only a small card: the map, three points with one
word for each example, and the one thing. She looked up more. She paused at each turn, because she had
to look down at the card to find the next point. The class could list all three points afterwards.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **point**, **example**, **turn**, **signpost**: from [part 1.1](1.1-point-example-turn.md).
- **the map (of a talk)**, **recap**, **the one thing**, **map test**: from
  [day 89](../../../day-89-presenting-opening-closing/parts/01-speaking/1.3-open-and-close-a-real-talk.md).
- **talk card**: notes for the whole talk on one small card: the map, each point in a few words with one
  word for its example, and the one thing.
- **point-and-example test**: after the talk, each listener names the three points and one example from
  each.

A card of notes makes you speak rather than read. The pauses you need to look at it land at the turns,
which is where the audience needs them anyway.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **the talk read from a page**. The pace goes up, the eyes go down, and
the turns disappear into the reading voice.

The second is **a card of full sentences**, which is a page with smaller writing. Notes only.

The third is **a listener who already knows the topic**. They'll list the points from what they know,
not from what you said.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Fluent speakers often practise the turns more than anything else. The points come easily. The joins
are what make a talk sound planned.

Under pressure, an example will sometimes grow into a long story. Keep an eye on your card. When the
example has made the point, say the *so* sentence and turn.

If a listener remembers an example but not its point, the example was fine and the *so* sentence was
missing. Tie every example to its point out loud.
