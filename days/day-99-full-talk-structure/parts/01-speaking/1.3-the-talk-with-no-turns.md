## One-line answer

Record the middle of your talk twice. In take R, the river, run the points together on purpose with *and
also* and *anyway*: no numbers, no *so* sentences, no pauses at the turns. In take T, use every turn. Play
each to a different listener who hasn't heard the talk. Ask: *How many points were there? What were they?
Give one example.* R's listener should lose count. T's listener should name three points and an example.

## The story

Your friend's father is the secretary of a small temple committee. At the yearly meeting, he talked
about the year's accounts. He knew every number and every reason. He spoke without stopping for a long
time, joining everything with *and also*.

At the end, someone asked *So is there money for the new roof or not?* He'd said it, somewhere in the
middle. Nobody had noticed.

The next year, his daughter helped him make a card with three points: *money in, money out, the roof*.
He paused before each one and said *Third, the roof.* The room went quiet at *the roof*. Everyone knew
that was the one they'd been waiting for.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have heard from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **point**, **example**, **turn**: from [part 1.1](1.1-point-example-turn.md).
- **talk card**, **point-and-example test**: from [part 1.2](1.2-record-a-full-talk.md).
- **the river**: a talk whose points run together with *and also*, with no turns.
- **count-the-points test**: a listener says how many points there were, names them, and gives one
  example.

| Take | What's in it | What a listener should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R, river (deliberate) | points joined by *and also*, *anyway*; no numbers, no *so*, no pauses | the wrong number of points, or points they can't name |
| T, turns | signpost, point, example, *so*, a pause and a numbered turn | three points named, with an example |

## Why Vaani needs it

A talk with no turns sounds fluent. It may even sound more fluent than one with turns, because it never
stops. Only a listener can tell you that fluent and followable aren't the same thing.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **a take R that still has numbers in it**, because the
habit from part 1.2 is strong. Listen to R back once before the test. Take out any *second*.

The second is **a take T where the example is longer than the point**. The listener names the example
as a point. Keep each example to one or two sentences.

The third, in real life, is **the most important point said in the middle of a river**, like the roof.
If one point matters most, give it a turn of its own, and say it last.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have heard from you.

## In real life

Rivers are how most people speak in conversation, and that's fine in conversation. A talk to a group is
different, because nobody can interrupt you to ask *Wait, is this a new point?*

Under pressure, the pauses go first. A pause before a turn feels long to you and sounds normal to the
audience.

An audience that can count your points trusts you more. They can tell you planned it.
