## One-line answer

An essay plan fits on half a page and has four things. The **question**, with its key words underlined.
Your **answer** to it in one full sentence, which becomes the thesis. Three **body paragraphs**, each
with its topic sentence written in full and its example in notes. And a **conclusion** line. Everything
except the answer and the topic sentences can be single words.

## The story

Your younger sister had an English exam. The question was: *Should mobile phones be banned in schools?
Give your opinion.* She started writing at once. Her first paragraph was about how phones were
invented. Her second was about her cousin's phone. By the third, she'd written *on the other hand* and
was arguing the opposite. When the bell went, she had no conclusion and no clear answer.

Her friend spent the first part of the exam on half a page:

> Q: phones BANNED in schools? — my opinion
> A: Phones should be allowed in schools, but kept switched off in class.
> 1. Safety — parents can reach children → the bus strike last month
> 2. Learning — dictionary, calculator → my science project
> 3. BUT distraction in class → switched off, in the bag
> C: allowed, with one clear rule

Her essay followed the plan. It had one answer from start to finish.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **paragraph**: a group of sentences about one topic, written as a block, from
  [day 13](../../../day-13-sentence-rhythm/parts/02-writing/2.1-what-a-paragraph-is.md).
- **topic sentence**: the first line of a paragraph, saying the topic and the point, from
  [day 47](../../../day-47-stress-for-emphasis/parts/02-writing/2.1-the-first-line-makes-a-promise.md).
- **essay**: a piece of writing of several paragraphs that answers one question.
- **essay plan**: notes made before writing, showing the answer and what each paragraph will say.
- **key words (of a question)**: the words that say what the question actually asks, such as *banned*,
  *your opinion*, *compare*, *explain why*.
- **thesis**: your answer to the question, in one sentence. The whole essay argues for it.
- **body paragraph**: a paragraph in the middle of an essay, making one point.
- **conclusion**: the last paragraph, which gives the answer again in the light of the points.

## The rule

**The pages checked, all opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Making a plan](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/writing-exams/making-plan) says:
"Ideas can be single words, you don't have to write complete sentences." And: "When you have enough
ideas organise them into paragraphs." And: "Many pieces of writing need four clear paragraphs."
[Thesis Statements](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/thesis-statements/), from a university
writing centre, says a thesis is "usually a single sentence near the beginning of your paper (most
often, at the end of the first paragraph)" and "is a road map for the paper; in other words, it tells
the reader what to expect from the rest of the paper."

**The plan on the page.**

```text
 Q   Should mobile phones be BANNED in schools? Give your OPINION.
     ────────────── underline the key words ──────────────

 A   Phones should be allowed in schools, but kept switched off in class.   ← full sentence: the thesis

 1   Phones keep children safe on the way to and from school.               ← topic sentence, full
       → bus strike last month, mum called                                   ← example, notes

 2   Phones are useful learning tools.
       → dictionary, calculator, science project

 3   The problem is distraction in class, which one rule can solve.
       → switched off, in the bag

 C   allowed + one rule                                                     ← notes
```

**Underline the key words.** *Banned* is not *useful*. *Your opinion* means you must take a side.
*Compare* means two things. The underlines stop you answering a question you weren't asked.

**Write the answer in full.** One sentence that could be *yes*, *no* or *yes, but*, with its reason. If
you can't write it, you're not ready to write the essay.

**Three body paragraphs, one point each.** Write each topic sentence in full, because it carries the
point. Write the example in notes.

**The conclusion in notes.** A few words that show the answer again.

**Name the trap.** A plan feels like lost writing time, so it's tempting to skip it, especially in an
exam. Without it, the essay finds its answer while you're writing, often in the last paragraph, and
sometimes the opposite of where it started.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **no plan**, as in the story. The essay changes its mind halfway.

The second is **the plan with no answer**: three topics and no sentence saying what you think. The
paragraphs have nothing to argue for.

The third is **a plan written in full sentences everywhere**. It becomes a first draft, and there's no
time left for the essay.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers often change their answer while planning, and that's the right time to change it, before
a word of the essay is written.

Under pressure, in an exam, the plan is the first thing people skip and the thing that saves the most
time. Half a page of notes means you never stop to think *What do I say next?*

A reader of an essay, a teacher or an examiner, is looking for one thing first: your answer. A plan
makes sure there's one to find.
