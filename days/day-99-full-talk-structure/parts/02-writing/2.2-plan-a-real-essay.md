## One-line answer

Plan one real essay on half a page. First brainstorm every idea in single words. Then choose your
answer, write it as one sentence, and pick the three ideas that best support it. Write each as a topic
sentence with an example in notes, and add a conclusion line. Give the plan to a reader and ask them to
predict the essay: its answer, and its three points.

## The story

Your cousin was applying for a scholarship. The form asked for an essay: *Describe a problem in your
community and how you would solve it.* She had too many ideas: the garbage, the streetlights, the
water, the park, the traffic.

She wrote all of them down in single words. Then she chose one problem, the water, and wrote her answer
in one sentence. From her list she took the three ideas that supported it best and crossed out the
rest. She showed the half page to her uncle and asked *What will my essay say?* He told her the answer
and the three points in his own words. She knew then that the plan worked, before she'd written the
first paragraph.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **essay**, **essay plan**, **key words**, **thesis**, **body paragraph**, **conclusion**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-question-answer-three-points.md).
- **brainstorm**: write down every idea you have, fast, in single words, without judging them yet.
- **predict test**: a reader sees only your plan and says what the essay will argue and what its three
  points are.

Brainstorming gives you more ideas than you need. Choosing is what makes a plan. The ideas you cross
out are the ones that would have made the essay wander.

## Why Vaani needs it

The plan you make here can carry straight into day 101, where the thesis becomes the last line of the
introduction. A plan a reader can predict from is a plan an essay can be built on.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version is **keeping every brainstormed idea**, with five or six paragraphs and none
of them deep. Three chosen points beat six listed ones.

The second is **a point that doesn't support the answer**. It's a good idea, but it argues for something
else. Cross it out, however much you like it.

The third is **a reader who guesses from the question**. Cover the question line when you run the test.
The plan must work on its own.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

Fluent writers keep their crossed-out ideas. Sometimes one becomes the counter-argument in the third
paragraph. Sometimes it's the next essay.

Under pressure, the choosing step gets skipped because it feels like losing ideas. It's the step that
makes the essay clear.

A teacher reading your essay is checking the same thing the predict test checks: is there one answer,
and does every paragraph argue for it?
