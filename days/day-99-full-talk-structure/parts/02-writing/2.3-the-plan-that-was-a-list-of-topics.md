## One-line answer

Make a copy of your plan that is only a list of topics, on purpose: no answer line, and each paragraph
reduced to one noun, like *safety*, *learning*, *distraction*. Give that copy and your real plan to two
readers, with the question covered. Ask each one: *What will this essay argue? What side is it on?* The
topic list should leave the reader guessing. Your real plan should let them say the answer and the
side.

## The story

Your friend's younger brother showed his teacher his essay plan before writing:

> Question: Is it better to live in a city or a village?
> 1. jobs
> 2. pollution
> 3. family

The teacher asked him *Which is better, then?* He said *I haven't decided. I'll see when I write.* His
essay said the city was better in the first paragraph and the village was better in the third. The
teacher wrote in the margin: *Which one?*

His second plan started with one sentence: *A village is better for families, even though the city has
more jobs.* Every paragraph after that had a side.

This is a deliberate exercise. The wrong versions below are ones beginners commonly produce. They are
not ones we have seen from you.

## The idea in plain language

**Words to know**

- **deliberate**: done on purpose.
- **essay plan**, **thesis**, **topic sentence**: from
  [part 2.1](2.1-question-answer-three-points.md).
- **predict test**: from [part 2.2](2.2-plan-a-real-essay.md).
- **topic list**: a plan that names subjects, like *jobs* or *pollution*, but says nothing about them.
- **which-side test**: a reader sees a plan with the question covered and says what the essay will
  argue and which side it takes.

| Copy | What's in it | What a reader should report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| L, list (deliberate) | no answer line; each paragraph one noun | no answer; a guess at the side, or *can't tell* |
| R, real | your plan from [part 2.2](2.2-plan-a-real-essay.md) | the answer and the side, in their words |

## Why Vaani needs it

A topic list looks like a plan to the person who wrote it, because they know what they mean by
*pollution*. A reader doesn't know whether pollution is for the city or against it. The test shows you
which of your lines carry a point, and which only carry a word.

## The rule

**The pages checked, both opened on 2026-09-24.**
[Thesis Statements](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/thesis-statements/): a thesis "is a
road map for the paper; in other words, it tells the reader what to expect from the rest of the paper."
[Making a plan](https://learnenglishteens.britishcouncil.org/exams/writing-exams/making-plan): "When you
have enough ideas organise them into paragraphs."

**Step one: make copy L.** Copy your plan and write *deliberate* at the top. Then:

1. Delete the answer line.
2. Replace each topic sentence with one noun.
3. Delete the example notes and the conclusion line.

**Step two: the which-side test.** Cover the question on both copies. Give L to one reader and R to
another.

**Step three: the repair.** If R went red, the thesis is a topic, not an answer. Rewrite it so it could
be argued with: *A village is better for families* can be argued with. *Villages and cities* can't. Try a
new reader.

## When it breaks

The commonest wrong version of the exercise is **an R whose thesis sits on the fence**: *There are good
and bad points on both sides.* The reader can't name a side, because there isn't one. If the question
asks for your opinion, choose.

The second is **an L that still has a verb**: *Pollution is bad in cities*. That's a point, not a topic.
Make it one noun.

The third, in real life, is **the plan written after the essay**, to show a teacher. A plan's job is to
come first.

These are errors beginners commonly make. This day was written before your own error ledger had rows
in it, so none of them is a mistake we have seen from you.

## In real life

If an essay changes its mind halfway, look back at the plan before the paragraphs. Was there an answer
line? If not, that's where to fix it.

Under pressure, a topic list is fast to make and feels like a plan. The one sentence it's missing, the
answer, is the one that's hard to write, and that's exactly why it matters.

An examiner reading an essay with no answer can't give marks for arguing it. A plan with one sentence
of answer protects the whole essay.
